## Putting a desktop robot on a hoverboard — inverted pendulum control in practice
Laura: So the other thread making the rounds in robotics this week — someone put a desktop manipulator on a hoverboard and called it mobility.
Sarah: Wait, really? Like, an actual hoverboard? The two-wheeled self-balancing kind?
Laura: That's the claim, yes. Reachy Mini — it's a small research arm, about fifty centimeters tall, mounted on a commercial hoverboard base. The post says they're running an inverted pendulum controller on the robot's onboard computer.
Sarah: Okay, that is genuinely cool. I've seen the Reachy Mini videos — it's expressive, it's got the little antenna head — but it's always been stuck on a desk. Giving it legs... well, wheels... changes the whole use case.
Laura: It does, if it works. The physics here is the classic inverted pendulum. Center of mass above the wheel axis. It's inherently unstable — any tiny disturbance creates a torque, a twisting force, that wants to tip it over.
Sarah: Right, like balancing a broom upside down on your palm. You have to keep moving your hand under the center of mass to keep it upright.
Laura: Exactly. Only here the "hand" is the wheel acceleration. The controller commands the motors to accelerate forward or backward, which generates a reaction torque at the base. That torque counters the gravitational torque trying to rotate the robot face-down.
Sarah: So the wheels aren't just for driving — they're the actuators for balance. Every time the robot leans, the wheels spin to catch it.
Laura: Precisely. And the catch has to happen fast. The natural frequency — how quickly the pendulum wants to fall — scales with the square root of gravity divided by height. For a half-meter robot, that works out to roughly two radians per second. In cycles per second, hertz, that's about one-third of a hertz. Your control loop needs to run roughly ten times faster than that, so low single-digit hertz at minimum, ideally much higher.
Sarah: And they're claiming this runs on the robot's onboard compute? Not a beefy external PC?
Laura: That's what the post says. "Onboard compute with sufficient stability margin." But the independent check — we looked — this appears only on Reddit. No paper, no repo with benchmarks, no third-party replication. So treat it as unconfirmed.
Sarah: Got it. But assuming the architecture is what we'd expect — IMU for tilt rate and angle, wheel encoders for position and velocity — sensor fusion at high rates...
Laura: That's the standard stack. The IMU — inertial measurement unit — gives you accelerometer and gyroscope data. The gyro measures angular rate directly, low latency but drifts over time. The accelerometer gives you the gravity vector for absolute tilt, but it's noisy when the base accelerates. You fuse them, usually with a complementary filter or a small Kalman filter, to get a clean tilt estimate at high bandwidth.
Sarah: And the encoders close the loop on wheel position — so you know exactly how much torque you've actually applied, not just what you commanded.
Laura: Right. Because motor torque isn't instantaneous. There's electrical time constants, gear backlash, friction. The encoder tells you the resulting wheel acceleration, which is what actually generates the balancing torque.
Sarah: This is reminding me of rocket landing — Falcon 9 first stage. Same inverted pendulum problem. Center of mass above the engine gimbal point. They run the control loop at fifty hertz on flight computers from the early two-thousands.
Laura: Good analogy. And in both cases, latency budget matters more than peak torque. If your sensor-to-actuator delay is twenty milliseconds, the robot tilts about two degrees before your correction even starts. That eats your stability margin fast.
Sarah: So the hoverboard's stock controller — those are usually just for a human rider, right? Leaning forward to go forward?
Laura: Most consumer hoverboards use a simple proportional controller on the tilt angle. Human leans, board accelerates to catch up. But a robot has a different mass distribution, higher center of mass, and no ankles to shift weight. The post author mentions messing up the IMU setup and the robot hitting the ground — that's the latency budget biting you.
Sarah: The "sit → stand up → sit → stand up" demo they mention — that's a huge disturbance. The center of mass moves vertically, which changes the pendulum length and natural frequency in real time.
Laura: Exactly. The post author says the robot does sit-to-stand transitions smoothly. On a roughly half-meter robot, the center of mass might move down an estimated ten centimeters — that's an approximate figure, not from specs. That would be roughly a twenty percent change in effective length. Your controller gains need to handle that, or you need gain scheduling — different gains for different configurations.
Sarah: And they're claiming this generalizes to other desktop robots? Just bolt your manipulator on a hoverboard?
Laura: That's the third claim. "This approach generalizes to other desktop robots needing mobility." In principle, yes — any robot with mass concentrated above a two-wheeled base is an inverted pendulum. But the mass properties matter. Moment of inertia about the wheel axis, center of mass height, wheel radius, motor torque constant. A different robot means re-tuning at minimum, possibly redesigning the controller if the dynamics shift enough.
Sarah: So it's not plug-and-play. More like... a reference architecture you adapt.
Laura: That's how I'd read it. The hoverboard gives you wheels, motors, motor drivers, battery, and a chassis — maybe two hundred dollars of hardware that would cost thousands to custom-build. But you're inheriting the hoverboard's mechanical limits: small wheels, no suspension, gear backlash, unknown motor parameters.
Sarah: And the smartphone app with joystick control — that's teleoperation, not autonomy. The human is the high-level planner; the onboard controller just handles balance and velocity tracking.
Laura: Right. The post says "software interfaces to send speed commands." So the autonomy layer — navigation, obstacle avoidance, manipulation while moving — that's all still on the developer.
Sarah: Which makes sense. Balancing is the low-level stabilization. Like... the inner loop of a cascade controller. You still need the outer loop for "go to kitchen" or "follow person."
Laura: Exactly. And that outer loop runs at maybe ten hertz. The inner balance loop runs much faster. Different timescales, different compute.
Sarah: The JavaScript apps mention is interesting — they're pitching a browser-based telepresence interface. No Python environment, just click a link. That lowers the barrier for... I was going to say "hobbyists" but really it's any engineer who wants to test something without setting up ROS.
Laura: ROS — Robot Operating System — isn't an OS, it's middleware. Message passing, build tools, visualization. Powerful but heavy. A WebRTC stream with websocket commands? That's lightweight. Good for iteration.
Sarah: So the verdict: cool demo, plausible physics, unconfirmed claims, hoverboard is a clever hack but not a general solution.
Laura: That's fair. The inverted pendulum on a hoverboard is a legitimate controls problem — state estimation, latency management, disturbance rejection. Good learning platform. But "practical mobility without custom drivetrain" depends entirely on what you mean by practical. Indoor flat floors? Probably. Carpet, thresholds, cables? Those small wheels will struggle.
Sarah: And the stability margin claim — "sufficient" — without numbers, that's just words. We'd need to see step responses, disturbance rejection plots, maybe a Nyquist or Bode plot of the open-loop transfer function.
Laura: Or at minimum: how long does it balance untouched? What's the maximum push it recovers from? What's the latency from IMU interrupt to PWM update?
Sarah: Quick recap then. Inverted pendulum: center of mass above wheels, inherently unstable. Balance via wheel acceleration generating reaction torque. Sensor fusion — IMU plus encoders — at high rate. Latency budget tighter than torque budget. Hoverboard gives cheap hardware but unknown dynamics. Claims unconfirmed outside Reddit. Generalization needs re-tuning per robot.
Laura: Nailed it. And the rocket analogy holds — same math, different scale.
