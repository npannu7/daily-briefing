## Can Scientific Peer Review Survive Machine-Written Papers
Laura: Shifting from public markets to the lab, the next institution facing that same trust test is scientific peer review.
Sarah: Wait, peer review? Like, people are actually submitting machine-written papers to academic journals now?
Laura: That is the claim in a Hacker News thread I found today, though worth saying up front: so far there is only the Hacker News thread, and we could not check beyond our usual sources. The link to the paper would not load, so we only have the abstract quoted in the comments and the debate around it.
Sarah: Okay, fair enough. Still, the premise is wild. Are we talking about chatbots cleaning up a paragraph, or generating whole studies and reviews from scratch?
Laura: Both, according to the quoted abstract, specifically in economics. The authors argue that large language models—software trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next word and produce fluent prose—are now writing both papers and the referee reports that judge them. They warn that without a screening tool, the whole system could collapse.
Sarah: Hold on. Before we even get to the bots, what is peer review actually supposed to do? From the outside it sounds like professors grading each other's homework.
Laura: It can look that way. At its core, peer review is a volunteer quality-control filter for new research. Anyone can run an experiment, plot a chart, and type a convincing story. Before others build on that claim, how does the community decide whether the work is sound?
Sarah: Right, you need someone independent to check the math and make sure you didn’t just cherry-pick data.
Laura: Exactly. An editor acts like a dispatcher, sending the paper to a handful of independent experts in the same narrow field. Those reviewers read the methods, check whether the evidence supports the conclusions, and write a review report recommending accept, reject, or revisions.
Sarah: So it’s basically a design review gate before a part goes to production—except in a factory the quality team is paid and works to a drawing with tolerances. Who pays the reviewers?
Laura: Nobody. That’s the catch, and a huge part of why this thread got heated. Reviewers are usually unpaid volunteers doing it on top of teaching and their own research, mostly out of professional duty.
Sarah: Wait, really? Nobody gets paid? That sounds like a system held together by duct tape and goodwill. How did we even end up with that setup?
Laura: It grew organically. Centuries ago, scientific societies printed members’ letters, and the secretary decided what looked interesting. Only in the twentieth century, as science specialized and submissions exploded, could editors no longer judge every paper themselves. They started mailing papers to outside specialists, and that informal habit hardened into the standard gatekeeper.
Sarah: Okay, that makes sense. When you have many subfields, one editor can’t know everything. But now drop a large language model into the pipeline: an unpaid volunteer already behind on work faces an author who can generate many pages of smooth, academic-sounding text in seconds.
Laura: That asymmetry is the exact bottleneck commenters keep pointing to. One person put it bluntly: it takes almost no time to generate a polished-looking paper, and it takes hours of deep reading for an expert to verify whether the logic is real or just plausible-sounding fluff.
Sarah: It’s like trying to inspect parts when a machine suddenly spits out shiny, slightly warped brackets a hundred times faster than your measuring tool can probe them. Even if every one is junk, sorting through the pile buries you.
Laura: And it gets worse on the other side of the desk. Because reviewers are stretched thin, some are tempted to feed the submitted paper into a language model and ask it to spit out a review report.
Sarah: Oh, no. Wait—is that actually happening?
Laura: The quoted abstract claims it is, and one commenter explained why that makes researchers furious. In fields like economics, a career can rest on publishing a few papers in top journals. Someone spends a long time collecting data and refining a model, only to have a reviewer hand it to a bot that produces a generic, surface-level critique.
Sarah: Yeah, I’d be livid. You put years of your life into something and get back a computer-generated shrug. So what do the authors propose? Do they want journals to ban it?
Laura: They propose making automated text detectors—tools that try to spot machine-written text—a standard screening tool. In their abstract, they claim an experiment showed detectors can flag referee reports written by a machine, while leaving alone reports where a human wrote the critique and just used software to proofread or polish grammar.
Sarah: Wait a minute. Do AI text detectors actually work? Every time I see people test those tools online, they seem to fail constantly.
Laura: That’s the exact right question, and since we can’t read the full paper, we have to treat their claim with real skepticism. How would you actually verify whether a detector works?
Sarah: You’d need a clean test set: real human reports written before these models existed, mixed with machine-generated ones, plus a middle group where humans used the tool only for light editing. Then measure how often it misses a bot and how often it falsely accuses a human.
Laura: Right. And there’s a massive trap with false accusations. One commenter also raised a gray area: non-native English speakers using a language model to smooth out their prose so their ideas are clear—how do you even gauge human contribution there?
Sarah: So relying on a text detector is basically a cat-and-mouse game—trying to catch a counterfeit by looking at the font instead of checking whether the underlying engineering holds up.
Laura: That brings us to the deeper argument: whether machine-written text is the real disease or just a symptom. Several commenters argued peer review was already cracking long before these tools showed up.
Sarah: Cracking how? Because of the unpaid labor thing?
Laura: That, and misaligned incentives. When your career depends on publishing constantly, the volume of papers goes up and reviewers get fatigued. One commenter noted that if a paper gets rejected, authors can often just submit it to journal after journal until it lands on a tired reviewer who barely reads it and rubber-stamps it through.
Sarah: Wait, the flip side happens too. Someone in the thread mentioned an ETH professor who had landmark papers rejected twice, then resubmitted the exact same paper—and one of them later won an Intel industry award.
Laura: Yes, that was brought up as proof of how noisy human reviewing already is. If the quality gate is inconsistent even when humans do all the writing, adding fluent machine text just pours gasoline on an existing fire.
Sarah: Okay, if text detectors are shaky and the incentive system is messy, what are people actually suggesting instead? “Just let bots review bots” sounds like a joke, though some commenters half-seriously said that.
Laura: A few did! One person joked that if machines write the papers and machines write the reviews, at least they’re truly peers. Another commenter took a serious angle, arguing that if models keep improving at their current pace, traditional peer review might not even matter in a couple of years.
Sarah: Hmm, I don’t buy that. Even if a model gets super smart, who’s accountable when a bridge collapses or a drug trial fails? You can’t subpoena a probability distribution. If no human actually reads and verifies the work, you just have machines nodding at machines while everyone assumes someone checked the math.

Laura: One commenter wished the culture would shift toward shorter, clearer papers, noting that length and dense jargon have never been a reliable measure of quality. And that circles back to the hardest question: if a human does real, original science and uses a machine purely to turn rough notes into clear English, should we even care?
Sarah: Honestly, as long as the human owns the data, the methods, and the mistakes, I don’t care who polished the sentences. The problem is when the machine invents the reasoning and nobody checks it.
Laura: Let’s do a quick sanity check. Peer review is a volunteer quality-control filter where independent experts check new research before a journal publishes it. Large language models break the time balance because generating fluent text is nearly instant, while verifying the underlying science still takes hours of human effort. Automated text detectors are one proposed fix, though commenters questioned their reliability and how they handle edge cases like non-native speakers, arguing the real answer is changing incentives and submission rules rather than scanning the text.
Sarah: That’s a super clean summary. It feels like the same pattern we saw earlier—whenever automation makes output cheap, the human trust layer underneath gets tested immediately.
Laura: My take: you can’t fix a broken inspection process just by adding an automated scanner at the end of the line. If incentives reward volume over rigor, people will always find a way around the scanner, and you only end up trusting the system less.
