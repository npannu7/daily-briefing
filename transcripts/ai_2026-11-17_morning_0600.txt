## When Gut Bacteria Eat Their Own Home

Sarah: Wait, really? That sounds like something out of a sci-fi horror movie. Little microbes turning on their host because dinner was late.
Laura: It really does. And the thread blew up. People were immediately connecting it to rising colon cancer rates, arguing about fiber supplements versus whole vegetables, and calling out the whole modern protein-powder obsession.
Sarah: Okay, I can see why that would strike a nerve. Everyone has an opinion about fiber and protein right now. What were the top comments actually saying?
Laura: There was a lot going on in that thread. One commenter said they had just talked about this exact topic with their stomach doctor. They asked the doctor if gummy fiber supplements were enough to replace leafy greens, and the doctor said no.
Sarah: Really? Why not? Isn't fiber just fiber?
Laura: Apparently not, according to what their doctor told them. The commenter said gummy fiber helps keep things moving along, which is great for regularity, but your gut bacteria actually need a steady supply of leafy green bulk passing through so they have something to chew on.
Sarah: Huh. That makes intuitive sense — like there's a difference between something that just keeps things moving and actual physical plant matter for microbes to break down. What else were people saying?
Laura: Another commenter pointed out something that feels painfully relatable. They said the worst thing you can do, aside from never eating fiber, is to eat tons of fiber and then suddenly drop to zero, over and over.
Sarah: Oh, like starting a health kick, buying a giant tub of fiber gummies, taking them for three weeks, and then getting lazy and forgetting about them?
Laura: Yes, literally someone in the thread admitted they did that exact thing with fiber supplements. The worry in the comments was that when you load up on fiber, you grow a big population of fiber-eating bacteria, and then when you abruptly stop, you leave a huge, hungry crowd stranded on your intestinal wall.
Sarah: That is a vivid mental image. And I'm guessing that's where the colon cancer comments came in?
Laura: Right. Another commenter noted that around ninety percent of colon cancers start in the cells sitting right underneath that protective mucus layer. People also chimed in with personal stories — someone mentioned dealing with a really scary gut perforation a few years back and wondering if low fiber played a role.
Sarah: Okay, so the chain reaction the Reddit crowd was piecing together is: skip fiber, bacteria get hungry, mucus gets eaten, the underlying gut wall gets exposed, and cancer risk goes up.
Laura: That's the story the commenters were connecting in their heads. And then there was a sharp commenter who actually read closely and pointed out that the news article linked in the post was covering two separate studies from the same Princeton team, and the other study wasn't even about bacteria eating mucus.
Sarah: Classic Reddit — half the thread is spiraling on the headline, and someone in the comments has to remind everyone what the papers actually say. Independent check: did you read the linked article yourself?
Laura: I did. I read the ScienceAlert write-up directly. And the real story is actually more interesting — and a lot more nuanced — than just "hungry bugs chew through your stomach."
Sarah: Good. Let's ground ourselves in what the researchers actually found. Who ran the work, and what did they measure?
Laura: Both studies were led by molecular biologists Jenna AbuSalim and Joshua Rabinowitz at Princeton University, and they were published in two major science journals. Instead of just watching whether mucus disappears, they tracked something called phenol metabolites — which are small chemical byproducts made when gut bacteria digest certain building blocks of protein.
Sarah: Okay, so we're looking at the chemical output of the bacteria, not just what they're physically chewing on.
Laura: Exactly. Think of it like a fork in the road. Gut bacteria take different protein building blocks from your food and turn them into these phenol metabolites. When there is plenty of fiber around, the bacteria favor one building block and produce helpful, health-promoting chemicals.
Sarah: And when the fiber runs out?
Laura: They flip to a different protein building block and start pumping out potentially harmful chemicals instead. So it's not just "bacteria eat mucus." It's a chemical reroute. Same bacteria, same raw materials, completely different chemical output depending on what else is in the gut.
Sarah: That's a really important distinction. It's less like a monster eating a wall and more like a factory switching what it manufactures when its primary raw material runs out.
Laura: Yes! And here is where the second big piece of the study comes in. It's not just fiber that keeps the bacteria on the helpful track. The researchers coined a new term for certain hard-to-digest plant proteins: Prif, which stands for "proteins imitating fiber."
Sarah: Proteins imitating fiber. Why would a protein imitate fiber? Don't we usually digest and absorb protein pretty quickly?
Laura: Most protein, yes — especially the easy-to-digest kind, which gets broken down and absorbed high up in the digestive tract before it ever reaches the lower gut where most of these bacteria live. But Prif proteins are tough, hard-to-digest plant proteins. They survive the trip down, reach the lower gut mostly intact, and act right alongside fiber to keep the bacteria making the helpful chemicals.
Sarah: Wait, so the good chemicals in the study weren't even coming from fiber alone?
Laura: According to the researchers, the helpful chemicals came almost entirely from these Prif proteins, working together with fiber to steer the bacteria in the right direction and keep them there. AbuSalim even suggested that Prifs could be an emerging class of nutrients — to the point where food packaging might one day list Prif content right next to fiber grams.
Sarah: Imagine picking up a box of cereal or a bag of lentils and seeing a line for "Prif" under dietary fiber. That's a bold claim. Now, what about the mucus-eating part from the headline? How does that fit into the chemical story?
Laura: In the mouse experiments, when fiber ran out, the bacteria started feeding on the protective mucus lining the gut walls, and the chemical signature in the blood shifted toward those potentially harmful compounds.
Sarah: So the "eating your gut lining" part really did happen in the mice when fiber disappeared.
Laura: It did. They consumed the mucus layer and produced potentially harmful compounds. But the researchers also found that adding fiber or Prif back in flips that chemical signature back in the healthy direction within days.
Sarah: Days. That's surprisingly fast. It means the system is pretty responsive, at least in mice.
Laura: Right — and that "in mice" qualifier is huge. Mouse guts are not human guts. Transit time, acidity, total bacterial load, and diet are all different.
Sarah: So what does a mouse study like this actually prove for people?
Laura: It proves the biological mechanism exists in a mammal. The chemical pathways, the mucus lining, the way bacteria respond when fiber disappears — that machinery is real. What it doesn't prove yet is how much fiber or Prif a human needs to prevent the shift, how long it takes in people, or whether those chemical shifts directly cause disease over decades. In fact, the researchers noted that their next steps include looking more directly at how diet changes shape these phenol metabolites in humans over time.
Sarah: And what about the colon cancer leap in the Reddit thread? The researchers didn't claim they proved a direct link to colon cancer, right?
Laura: They didn't. The ScienceAlert write-up talks about disease prevention and future treatments in general terms, not proving colon cancer causation. We already know from population studies that low-fiber diets are associated with higher colon cancer risk, and this work offers a plausible biological bridge — mucus erosion plus harmful chemical byproducts — but a plausible bridge in mice is not clinical proof in humans.
Sarah: Right. Now tell me about that second paper the Reddit commenter mentioned. What was the plot twist there?
Laura: This part is fascinating. Researchers used to assume that a bunch of these health-linked chemicals in our blood came exclusively from gut bacteria. Take two chemicals with clunky names — indole-3-lactate and indole-3-acetate — that scientists thought only microbes could make.
Sarah: Wait — "previously thought." So the bacteria aren't the only ones making them?
Laura: Correct. The Princeton team ran experiments on cells from mice, rats, and humans with no microbes present at all, and those cells still produced indole-3-lactate and indole-3-acetate on their own.
Sarah: Oh, that complicates things! If your own body cells can make those same chemicals without any bacteria around, then seeing them in a blood test doesn't automatically tell you what your gut bacteria are up to.
Laura: Exactly. It's like trying to measure how much dust a factory floor is kicking up, only to realize the building's own air vents are shedding the exact same kind of dust. You can't use the total dust count as a clean readout of the machines.
Sarah: That's a great way to put it. So the authors are being careful — Rabinowitz said a clearer picture of how foods shape these bacterial outputs will help sharpen future guidance from nutritionists and doctors, not that we have brand-new dietary rules today.
Laura: Which brings us back to that last comment in the Reddit thread that made me laugh — someone called out the current "protein slop" fad, where everyone is chasing high protein numbers with ultra-processed shakes and powders while barely eating any fiber.
Sarah: Yeah, I've seen that everywhere. People hitting massive daily protein goals with isolated protein powders and bars, and almost zero actual plants. It's wild how fiber gets treated like an afterthought while protein gets put on a pedestal.
Laura: And if you think about what the Princeton studies suggest, that pattern is a double whammy. Those isolated, easy-to-digest proteins get absorbed high up in the gut. Nothing reaches the lower gut — no fiber, no Prif. So you're basically starving the bacteria down there, pushing them toward the mucus lining and the harmful chemical track.
Sarah: So a diet can look great on a basic protein-and-calorie tracker while quietly creating the exact gut environment this research warns against.
Laura: That's the hypothesis, anyway. Still unproven in long-term human trials, but it makes complete biological sense. And as the article points out, it's another reminder to get as many plant-based foods in your diet as possible.
Sarah: And what about the fiber-supplement yo-yo — taking gummies for a few weeks and then stopping?
Laura: The mouse data definitely makes that look like a bad habit. If the shift happens within days of fiber running out, consistency matters way more than occasional bursts. And if the stomach doctor's advice from the thread holds up, leafy greens and whole plant foods give you both the fiber and the tough plant proteins that gummies just don't have.
Sarah: So the mechanism is real in mice, but we still need human studies — how much fiber, how long it lasts, and whether these chemical changes actually lead to disease. It backs up the case for fiber, but it's not a new dietary rule yet.
Laura: Well put. The takeaway is simple: feed your gut bacteria fiber and tough, whole plant foods consistently every day, or they may start dining on your gut's protective coating — and changing their chemical output while they do it.
