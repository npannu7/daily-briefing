## Why entropy always increases: the statistical proof
Laura: So the thread I brought today — it’s a single Reddit post, unconfirmed, no linked paper or textbook citation, just a physicist on the Physics subreddit who’s genuinely stuck. And the comments don’t really resolve it.

Sarah: Okay, independent check first. No arXiv link, no DOI, just the post. We treat it as an unconfirmed question, not a claim.
Laura: Exactly. But the confusion they’re describing? That’s the exact historical fault line between Clausius’s macroscopic inequality and Boltzmann’s microscopic derivation. So let’s walk it from first principles — same way we’d validate a cycle: define the boundary, count the microstates, check the assumptions.
Sarah: Love that. Boundary definition — that’s our design input. So: isolated system, fixed energy, fixed volume, fixed particle number. Microstate is the exact position and momentum of every molecule. Macrostate is just the thermodynamic variables we measure — temperature, pressure, volume.
Laura: And the number of microstates corresponding to a macrostate? That’s the multiplicity, Omega. Boltzmann’s entropy is k log Omega. k is Boltzmann’s constant — a tiny number, joules per kelvin.
Sarah: Tiny constant. But log Omega gets huge — Avogadro-scale huge. So entropy is extensive, scales with system size. Now, the H-theorem. Boltzmann defines H as the integral of f log f over velocity space, where f is the single-particle distribution function. H is basically negative entropy per particle.
Laura: Right. And he shows dH/dt is less than or equal to zero — so entropy increases — if you assume molecular chaos. The Stosszahlansatz: pre-collision velocities are uncorrelated. You factor the two-particle distribution into a product of one-particle distributions.
Sarah: That’s the assumption. No memory of past collisions. In our terms: it’s like assuming every weld cools independently, no thermal crosstalk from the previous bead. Valid for sparse gas, not for dense fluid or a heat-affected zone.
Laura: Exactly. And Loschmidt pounced on this immediately — back in the eighteen seventies. Microscopic laws are time-reversible. Newton’s equations, Hamiltonian dynamics — if you flip every momentum, the system retraces its trajectory. So how can reversible micro-dynamics produce an irreversible macro-law?
Sarah: The paradox. Boltzmann’s answer: the H-theorem isn’t a theorem for every microstate. It’s statistical. For the overwhelming majority of microstates consistent with your macrostate, entropy increases. But there exist exceptional microstates — measure zero — where it decreases.
Laura: Then Poincaré recurrence — eighteen ninety. In a finite phase space, a bounded Hamiltonian system returns arbitrarily close to its initial state. Recurrence time? For a mole of gas, it’s astronomical. Vastly longer than the age of the universe.
Sarah: So practically irrelevant for our cycles. But theoretically — it means the second law is probabilistic, not absolute. Which brings us to modern fluctuation theorems. Evans, Searles, Crooks, Jarzynski — the nineties onward.
Laura: They quantify the probability of entropy decrease in small systems over short times. The Crooks fluctuation theorem: the ratio of forward to reverse path probabilities is exponential in the entropy produced. For a trajectory producing entropy sigma, P-forward over P-reverse equals e to the sigma over k.
Sarah: So in a single-molecule pulling experiment — optical tweezers unfolding a protein — you see negative entropy trajectories. They’re rare, exponentially suppressed, but measurable. That’s not a violation. That’s the theorem predicting the distribution.
Laura: And Jarzynski’s equality — the exponential average of work over non-equilibrium trajectories equals the free energy difference. You can extract equilibrium thermodynamics from irreversible measurements. That’s... honestly useful for us.

Sarah: The resolution is subtle. Clausius’s inequality — cyclic integral of dQ over T_reservoir less than or equal to zero — applies to the system undergoing a cycle. The T is the reservoir temperature at the boundary where heat crosses. When the textbook “sums over components” to prove entropy increases for an isolated composite system, they’re doing a different calculation. They’re calculating the actual entropy change of each component, dS = dQ_rev / T_system. For an irreversible heat transfer, dQ_irrev is the same for both, but to find the entropy change you must imagine a reversible path for each component separately. For component A cooling, you integrate dQ_rev over T_A, which runs from Th down. For component B heating, you integrate dQ_rev over T_B, running from Tc up.
Laura: So the textbook isn’t “swapping denominators” in the Clausius inequality. It’s calculating the state function change — entropy — for each subsystem using its own temperature, because entropy is a state function. The Clausius inequality with reservoir temperatures bounds the cyclic integral for a single system. The entropy increase proof for an isolated composite system sums the actual entropy changes of the parts, calculated via reversible paths at the system temperatures.
