## Google's DeepCrossAttention: three times faster without losing quality?
Sarah: Hey Laura — something popped up on HackerNews this week. Google's research blog posted an article dated November twenty-third, twenty twenty-six — a future date relative to the twenty twenty-five paper. The paper itself is from ICML twenty twenty-five, arXiv twenty-five-oh-two point zero-six-seven-eight-five. Title: "Supercharging Transformer Residual Connections." They call the method DeepCrossAttention, DCA for short.
Laura: Twenty twenty-six blog date for a twenty twenty-five paper? That's a gap.
Sarah: One commenter noted the paper is a year and a half old — "in ML years, somewhere between four years and a decade ago." The blog only appeared recently. No explanation given.
Laura: Okay. What's the headline claim?
Sarah: Comparable model quality up to three times faster. That's the blog's opening line.
Laura: Three times faster at training? Inference? Both?
Sarah: Training, mostly. They say DCA hits the same perplexity in up to five times fewer steps.
Laura: Perplexity — that's the standard quality metric for language models. Roughly, how surprised the model is by the next token. Lower is better.
Sarah: Right. And when they match steps, DCA ends up with lower perplexity. So more quality for the same compute budget.
Laura: Got it. Let's back up. The paper talks about residual connections. What are those?
Sarah: Think of a deep network like a game of Telephone. Data passes through layer after layer. Each layer changes the signal slightly. After dozens of layers, the original learning signal can degrade or vanish — that's called the vanishing gradient problem.
Laura: And residual connections fix that?
Sarah: They act like an information highway. Instead of forcing data through every layer's transformation, a copy of the input skips past the layer and gets added to the output. The model always has the raw signal to refer back to.
Laura: Standard residuals just add the input to the output — equal weight, fixed pipe.
Sarah: Exactly. Simple and efficient. But the paper argues this treats all prior representations equally. In very deep networks, specific details from early layers get averaged out. The network has to recompute them. That's the "information dilution" they mention.
Laura: So DCA changes how that skip connection works.
Sarah: Yes. They generalize residual connections using something called cross-attention. Let me explain self-attention first, since that's the baseline. Self-attention: every token in a sequence looks at every other token in that same sequence. It builds context inside one stream.
Laura: And cross-attention?
Sarah: Cross-attention: tokens from one sequence attend to tokens from a different sequence. Encoder-decoder models use it — the decoder queries the encoder's output. Retrieval models use it — the generator queries retrieved passages. The "cross" means across two distinct sources.
Laura: In DCA, what are the two sources?
Sarah: That's the twist. Not across sequences. Across depth — across layers. Each token queries a weighted mix of all previous layer embeddings for that same token position.
Laura: So instead of a residual just adding layer L's output to layer L-minus-one, DCA lets the model choose which earlier layers matter for this token, right now.
Sarah: Exactly. Two mechanisms. First: dynamic layer routing. A tiny module per layer computes mixing weights — a static learnable base plus an input-dependent projection. Per token. So the routing changes with context.
Laura: Second?
Sarah: Depth-wise cross-attention with split query, key, value. The token's current representation is the query. Keys and values come from the stack of prior layer embeddings. Standard attention math, just rotated ninety degrees — depth instead of sequence.
Laura: And the parameter cost?
Sarah: Less than two-tenths of a percent extra parameters. Negligible for model size. But the blog notes it increases memory bandwidth pressure because you're keeping representations from different layers intact instead of blending them into one residual stream.
Laura: That's the trade-off. You save compute by needing fewer layers, but you pay in working memory during training. Classic bandwidth-bound problem. Reminds me of memory-efficient attention methods — merging operations to avoid round-trips to high-speed off-chip memory, keeping things in fast on-chip memory instead.
Sarah: They don't name those methods explicitly. But the blog says "increasing memory bandwidth, as representations from different layers are presented intact rather than blended together." Same root cause: you're moving more distinct tensors.
Laura: If I keep thirty layer outputs alive instead of one fused residual, that's thirty times the activation footprint per token. Unless I fuse the routing and attention kernels so I never materialize the full stack.
Sarah: The HackerNews thread had someone asking exactly that — referencing multi-token prediction and a FlashAttention variant they've used for a ten-x speedup over two years, asking if this could push to thirty-x. Nobody had a clean answer.
Laura: Let's look at benchmarks. Two datasets: a large cleaned web corpus called C4, and the one-billion-word language model benchmark, LM1B.
Sarah: On LM1B, a standard transformer needed forty-two layers to get perplexity under fourteen.
Laura: DCA did better with thirty layers.
Sarah: That's a twenty-nine percent depth cut and thirty to forty percent fewer parameters.
Laura: And the speedup claim — "up to three times faster" — is that wall-clock time to target perplexity? Or steps?
Sarah: Steps mostly. "Up to five times fewer training steps" to match standard transformer performance. The three-x phrasing is in the blog's summary. Steps-to-target is the cleaner number.
Laura: Steps-to-target matters, but wall-clock depends on step time. If each DCA step is slower because of extra memory traffic, the three-x could shrink. Did they report step time?
Sarah: Not in the blog. The paper might. One commenter noted the paper is from twenty twenty-five — the blog only came out this week.
Laura: That gap is suspicious. If it worked that well, why sit on it?
Sarah: Comment thread had theories. "If Google published it, they're probably not using it." Someone else said their new consumer model Neon is "insanely cheap and fast for how good it is — maybe this has something to do with it."
Laura: Could be they moved past it. Or the benchmark conditions don't match production scale. C4 and LM1B are language modeling only — no human feedback training, no training on prompt-response pairs, no testing on very long sequences. Perplexity on next-token prediction doesn't always correlate with downstream task quality.
Sarah: They did test image classification too — said "significant improvements in convergence speed, training stability, and final model quality across these experiments." But no numbers in the blog.
Laura: The retrofitting claim is interesting. Initialize DCA to mimic standard residuals, continue training a pre-trained model. On LM1B, perplexity fell zero-point-one-nine versus zero-point-zero-two for continued standard training.
Sarah: That's a real signal if it holds. Clean ablation — you take a fixed model, add a tiny module, measure the delta exactly.
Laura: One commenter wrote: "Depth-wise cross-attention... unlocking the model's full historical context without the computational penalty usually required to scale traditional attention. I've often thought something like this might lead to a real solution to the infamous strawberry problem."
Sarah: The strawberry problem — counting letters in a word. That's a tokenization issue, not a depth issue. But the intuition: if early layers preserve character-level info that gets diluted in standard residuals, dynamic routing could retrieve it.
Laura: The thread also had a pet peeve about line graphs instead of bar graphs for architecture comparisons — "no temporal relation." Fair point.
Sarah: Community replication? Anyone running this open-source?
Laura: Not that I saw. The paper's on arXiv. No linked repo in the blog. One commenter asked "Where did this go?" referencing their own speedup stack, wondering if this adds to it.
Sarah: So independent check: primary source is Google's own blog and their ICML paper. No third-party replication yet. No open weights. Claims are plausible — dynamic residual routing has been explored before, an earlier method called AttnRes, and DenseNet-style connections that link every layer to every later layer — but the three-x speedup at production scale is unverified.
Laura: The dynamic routing means per-token weights depend on the input. That introduces a new variable in the computational graph.
Sarah: Standard residuals are deterministic. DCA adds a learned projection conditioned on the token. That's a new surface if you need exact reproducibility.
Laura: Still — twenty-nine percent fewer layers for same perplexity is a real architectural lever. If it replicates, it changes the depth-width trade-off curve. Fewer layers means shorter backward training paths, better flow of learning signals to early layers. That's the vanishing gradient problem they led with — the giant game of Telephone.
Sarah: Quick recap. Self-attention: tokens talking to tokens. Cross-attention: sequence A talking to sequence B. DCA rotates that: token at layer L talks to its own history across layers zero through L-minus-one. Dynamic routing picks which layers matter. Benchmarks: C4, LM1B, perplexity. Claim: up to five-x fewer steps, twenty-nine percent fewer layers, two-tenths of a percent parameter overhead. Open questions: step time at scale, memory bandwidth, downstream task transfer, replication.
Laura: And the paper's eighteen months old with no open release. That's the strongest signal.
Sarah: Agreed. Next segment: whether AI actually threatens the cryptography securing the internet. Spoiler — it's not Shor's algorithm.
Laura: Looking forward to it.

## Mathematicians warn AI could break online security — what the evidence shows
Laura: So I was going through HackerNews yesterday and there's this thread — title straight from The Times: "AI on brink of making online security useless, mathematicians fear."
Sarah: Wait, The Times? Like London Times?
Laura: Yeah. Paywalled, but someone posted an archive.is link. Thread exploded — over a thousand comments in a few hours.
Sarah: Okay, that's wild. What's the actual claim?
Laura: The article says recent breakthroughs mean AI has likely already cracked the equations billions of us depend on. Some fear discoveries are being hushed up.
Sarah: "Likely already cracked." That's... a lot. What breakthroughs?
Laura: That's the thing — the article doesn't name any. It quotes Peter Scholze, Fields medalist, saying breaking public-key crypto isn't harder than solving Riemann hypothesis. Then jumps to "AI could do it."
Sarah: Riemann hypothesis. Right. Unsolved since 1859. So the argument is: if AI can solve Riemann, it can break RSA?
Laura: Essentially. But the comments — oh, the comments. One person says they're switching to one-time pads. Another insists Anthropic and OpenAI must be spending millions breaking crypto right now. Someone else argues we need EU defence funds to build frontier LLMs just to defend ourselves.
Sarah: The defence-fund comment — that's the one about matching Colossus clusters? One to two gigawatts?
Laura: Yeah. Hundred-thirty-seven thousand B300s, ninety-three billion dollars. "Zero point four seven percent of GDP."
Sarah: People did the hardware math in the comments. That's... oddly specific.
Laura: It's HackerNews. They do that. But the mood shift one commenter noticed — that felt real. They said the discussion moved from "encryption is unbreakable" to "well, maybe it's breakable but only at O of n to the thousand" — government-scale, months of compute, not instant.
Sarah: O of n to the thousand. That's... not a standard complexity class.
Laura: No. It's hyperbole. But it captures something: people are pricing the attack now, not dismissing it.
Sarah: So what's actually new? Any papers? Any results?
Laura: That's where it gets thin. The Times piece references no specific AI-driven attack on RSA, ECC, or lattice schemes. Zero citations.
Sarah: Zero. So the "breakthroughs" are... vibes?
Laura: Mostly. But there's a real thread underneath — three different things getting conflated. One: AI finding new mathematical structure that breaks the hard problems. Two: AI optimizing known attacks. Three: AI writing exploit code for side-channels or fuzzing implementations.
Sarah: Okay, slow down. Let's separate those. First: the math. What are the actual hard problems?
Laura: Three main ones. RSA rests on factoring — take a two-thousand-bit number that's the product of two primes, find those primes. Easy to multiply, brutally hard to un-multiply.
Sarah: Like a one-way function. Mixing paint — easy to combine, impossible to separate back to the original colours.
Laura: Exactly. Elliptic Curve Cryptography — ECC — uses a different one-way function: discrete logarithm on an elliptic curve. Same principle, different algebra. Smaller keys for same security.
Sarah: And lattice-based?
Laura: That's the post-quantum frontrunner. Imagine a high-dimensional grid — hundreds of dimensions. Hide a point slightly off the grid lines. Finding the nearest grid point to that hidden point is the hard problem. No quantum algorithm breaks it efficiently yet.
Sarah: So three different mathematical locks. TLS, SSH — they all use these locks.
Laura: Right. TLS is the shipping container. RSA or ECC or soon lattice-based KEMs are the locks on the doors.
Sarah: KEMs?
Laura: Key Encapsulation Mechanisms. How two parties agree on a symmetric key using public-key math. The lock protects the key exchange; the symmetric key does the bulk encryption.
Sarah: Got it. So claim one: AI finds new math that breaks factoring, discrete log, or lattice problems. Any evidence?
Laura: None published. Scholze's quote about Riemann — that's about mathematical difficulty, not AI. The Riemann hypothesis connects to prime distribution. If AI solved it, maybe factoring gets easier. But that's a chain of "ifs" the length of your arm.
Sarah: Chain of ifs. I like that. Claim two: AI optimizes known attacks.
Laura: Here there's something. Number Field Sieve for factoring — best classical algorithm — has many tuning parameters. Polynomial selection, sieving bounds, linear algebra block sizes. People have used machine learning to pick better parameters. Modest speedups. Ten, twenty percent. Not asymptotic breakthroughs.
Sarah: Ten to twenty percent. That's... engineering optimization. Not "breaking crypto."
Laura: Exactly. Like using ML to tune CNC feed rates. You get better throughput. You don't change the physics of cutting steel.
Sarah: Claim three: AI for side-channels and fuzzing.
Laura: This one's real and present. Side-channel analysis — measuring power consumption, electromagnetic emanations, cache timing — to extract keys from implementations. AI excels at pattern recognition in noisy traces. Several papers show neural nets reducing trace counts needed for key recovery.
Sarah: So AI doesn't break the math. It breaks the implementation.
Laura: Precisely. And fuzzing — throwing random inputs at code to find crashes. LLMs now generate smarter fuzz inputs, find edge cases faster. Google's OSS-Fuzz uses this. But again: implementation bugs, not mathematical breaks.
Sarah: The Reddit thread conflates all three. "AI breaks RSA" becomes "AI helps side-channel" becomes "AI writes exploit code."
Laura: And the "hushed up" fear — one commenter said if any AI company solved public-key crypto, they'd never publish. Three-letter agencies would pay anything.
Sarah: That's the "secret breakthrough" trope. Unfalsifiable.
Laura: But there's a counter-argument in the thread too. One commenter — calm, seen cycles — said: new computational technology always attacks old encryption first. Mechanical cogs, tubes, transistors, quantum, now AI. Each defeat points to the next scheme. We'll make new ones.
Sarah: That's the historical view. Crypto evolves.
Laura: Right. And the lattice-based standards — ML-KEM, ML-DSA — just finalized by NIST. They're designed against quantum and classical attacks. No AI result threatens them yet.
Sarah: So verdict: the Times headline is... ahead of the evidence.
Laura: Half-right at most. AI is changing the attack surface — side-channels, fuzzing, parameter tuning. But the mathematical primitives? No published break. No asymptotic improvement. The "brink" language is speculation dressed as reporting.
Sarah: And the mathematicians' fear?
Laura: Scholze's quote is real. But he was talking about mathematical difficulty, not imminent AI breakthroughs. The article bridges from "Riemann is hard" to "AI might solve it" with no stepping stones.
Sarah: My take, for someone in QA: side-channel resistance is a validation requirement now. You test for timing leaks, power analysis — same way you'd test a sterilization cycle for worst-case load. AI just made the attack tools better. So your test tools need to be better too.
Laura: That's the practical takeaway. The math holds. The implementations need more scrutiny.
Sarah: One line to remember: AI hasn't broken the locks — it's just gotten better at picking the hinges.
Laura: Clean. I'll take it.
