Sarah: One commenter called it "representation engineering without the training phase." Representation engineering is steering model behavior by manipulating activation directions — like finding the "truthfulness" vector and adding it to activations at inference time. But here you're not steering; you're constructing the whole representation from a spec.
Laura: Let me make sure I have the distinction clear. Representation engineering: train a model, discover a direction in activation space that corresponds to a concept, then intervene at inference time. Weight-space surgery: train a model, then surgically edit specific weights to change behavior. Weight-space synthesis: skip training entirely, write weights that implement a known algorithm.

## How aircraft noise in your video gives away your location
Laura: So I was scrolling through the EverythingScience subreddit yesterday and someone posted this TechRadar piece about a YouTuber named Colsto who claims he can geolocate a video to within a hundred meters using just sixty seconds of audio you can't even hear.
Sarah: Wait, really? From airplane noise?
Laura: That's the claim. Inaudible aircraft noise captured by an ordinary microphone, matched against public ADS-B flight data, and the Doppler shifts from multiple passing planes give you the receiver position. It's multilateration via Doppler — same principle we use in radar and sonar, just inverted.
Sarah: Okay, that's wild. So the microphone picks up infrasound or something?
Laura: Not infrasound exactly. The article says "inaudible to you or other listeners" but still captured by the microphone. Think of it like aliasing in a sampled control system — the aircraft tone might be below your hearing threshold or masked by ambient noise, but the microphone's frequency response and the codec's sampling still record a trace of it. The signal-to-noise ratio is terrible, but if you know exactly what trajectory produced it, you can correlate.
Sarah: Right, because ADS-B gives you the aircraft position and velocity over time. That's the known input. The unknown is the receiver coordinates.
Laura: Exactly. Each aircraft gives you a Doppler curve — frequency versus time — that depends on the relative geometry. If you have multiple aircraft crossing the sky during that sixty-second window, you get multiple curves. The intersection of those constraints narrows down the receiver location. It's a nonlinear least-squares problem, basically. You're solving for the point on the ground that makes all the predicted Doppler curves match the measured ones.
Sarah: And the code — the post says several thousand lines of analysis code. Did you look at it?
Laura: The TechRadar article links to the YouTube video, not a repository. The video description mentions a "full scientific breakdown" but Colsto isn't releasing the tooling. He says it's educational, not a weapon. Hacker News had a thread on it — thirteen points, not huge — and one commenter said they've seen similar grid-hum geolocation and thought that was more practical.
Sarah: Grid hum — that's the fifty or sixty hertz mains frequency leaking into audio, right? ENF analysis.
Laura: Electric network frequency. Same idea: a pervasive, known signal with subtle variations that act like a watermark. But the aircraft method is different — it's geometric. You're not matching a temporal fingerprint; you're solving a geometry problem from frequency measurements.
Sarah: So the claim is a hundred meters accuracy. What's the physics limiting that?
Laura: A few things. First, the Doppler shift at audio frequencies from a commercial jet at cruising altitude is tiny. A seven-thirty-seven at ten kilometers altitude, moving two-fifty meters per second — the maximum frequency shift on a one-kilohertz tone is what, eight hertz? Something like that. You need frequency resolution better than a hertz to get hundred-meter precision. That means long observation windows or very high signal-to-noise ratio.
Sarah: But he says sixty seconds. That's your observation window.
Laura: Right. Sixty seconds at forty-eight kilohertz sampling gives you frequency bins of about one-sixth of a hertz if you use the full FFT. But the aircraft tone isn't a pure sinusoid — it's broadband engine noise, and the Doppler shift is a continuous sweep. You're not doing a single FFT; you're tracking instantaneous frequency, probably with a spectrogram and ridge extraction. The Cramér-Rao bound on frequency estimation depends on SNR and observation time. At low SNR, you need more time.
Sarah: And the SNR is terrible because the sound is "inaudible."
Laura: That's the tension. If it's truly inaudible, it's below the noise floor of the microphone preamp or the ambient acoustic noise. But "inaudible to humans" doesn't mean inaudible to the microphone — our hearing has a threshold curve, and microphones are flatter. Still, the dynamic range of a phone microphone is what, ninety decibels? If the aircraft tone is forty decibels below the ambient conversation or wind noise, you're digging deep.
Sarah: Could beamforming help? If the video has stereo audio?
Laura: Most phone videos are mono. Some newer ones capture stereo, but the microphone spacing is centimeters — not enough aperture for directional gain at low frequencies. You'd need an array. So no, this is single-channel spectral analysis.
Sarah: What about the ADS-B data? That's public, but what's the latency and accuracy?
Laura: ADS-B broadcasts position once per second typically, with horizontal accuracy around ten meters for modern transponders. Velocity is derived from position difference or from the aircraft's inertial reference system. The timestamp synchronization matters — if your audio clock drifts relative to the ADS-B timestamps, you get systematic error. Phone clocks can drift tens of milliseconds over a minute. That's a Doppler error.
Sarah: So you'd need to synchronize clocks. How?
Laura: Cross-correlation. You treat the ADS-B trajectory as a template, predict the Doppler curve for a candidate receiver position, and slide it in time to maximize correlation with the measured spectrogram ridge. The time offset that maximizes correlation gives you the clock offset. But that adds another dimension to the optimization.
Sarah: And multipath? Ground reflections?
Laura: At low grazing angles, ground reflection creates interference notches — comb filtering. That actually helps in a weird way: the notch frequencies move with geometry too, giving you more constraints. But it also means the model needs a ground impedance model. If you assume perfect reflection and the ground is absorptive, you bias the solution.
Sarah: So the hundred-meter claim — is that from controlled tests or just one demo?
Laura: The article describes one demo: "audio from some random security camera." No statistics, no confusion matrix, no Monte Carlo over varying geometries. That's a red flag for validation. In our world, you'd want a design-history file showing performance across the intended use envelope — different aircraft types, altitudes, receiver heights, noise conditions, microphone qualities.
Sarah: Right. IQ, OQ, PQ — installation qualification, operational qualification, performance qualification. You'd test the algorithm on a representative sample of real-world conditions.
Laura: Exactly. And you'd define the acceptance criteria: ninety-five percent of estimates within a hundred meters under specified conditions. The article doesn't mention any of that. It's a proof-of-concept video.
Sarah: What about the "thousands of lines of code" claim?
Laura: The video shows a Python notebook — signal processing, ADS-B parsing, optimization. Could be a few thousand lines including visualization and data download. But "full scientific breakdown" usually means the methodology, not a validated software product. He explicitly says he's not releasing tools.
Sarah: So the privacy risk — is it real or theoretical?
Laura: Both. The physics is sound — Doppler multilateration is a known technique. The implementation barriers are high: you need quiet audio, multiple aircraft with good geometry, accurate ADS-B, clock sync, and heavy computation. But the barriers are engineering, not physics. As microphones improve and compute gets cheaper, the attack surface grows. It's like side-channel attacks on cryptographic hardware — once theoretical, now standard in evaluation labs.
Sarah: And the grid-hum commenter had a point — ENF analysis is already used in forensics. Courts accept it.
Laura: ENF is easier because the signal is strong and the reference database is centralized — national grid operators publish the frequency history. Here the reference is distributed: you need ADS-B logs for the exact time and region. That's available from aggregators like OpenSky Network, but it's another dependency.
Sarah: Could you defeat it with a high-pass filter? The article says normal noise reduction can be ineffective.
Laura: If the aircraft tone is below the filter cutoff, sure. But if it's in the speech band — say two hundred to two thousand hertz — a high-pass filter removes speech too. And the Doppler sweep might cross the cutoff. Adaptive filtering could work if you have a reference channel, but you don't. It's a blind source separation problem — separating the aircraft component from speech and ambient noise using only the known trajectory as a guide.
Sarah: That's like blind deconvolution in machine tool chatter analysis — you know the cutter rotation frequency, you track its harmonics to separate chatter from forced vibration.
Laura: Perfect analogy. Same mathematics: a known kinematic model constraining the spectral support of the source. The difference is the SNR. In a machine tool, the chatter is often the dominant signal. Here it's buried.
Sarah: So the verdict — half-right?
Laura: The physics is right. The demonstration is plausible. The hundred-meter claim is unvalidated — single data point, no uncertainty quantification. The sixty-second window is theoretically possible under ideal geometry and SNR, but not guaranteed. The code exists but isn't released or independently reproduced. And the privacy threat is real in principle but currently limited to sophisticated actors with specific conditions.
Sarah: So not "enhance" magic, but not fiction either.
Laura: Exactly. It's a signal-processing inverse problem with all the usual demons: noise, model mismatch, identifiability, computational cost. The community reaction on Reddit was mostly "cool demo" with a few "this is why I strip audio metadata" — which doesn't help because the signal is in the acoustic content, not the metadata.
Sarah: Right. You'd need to add synthetic noise or resample to break the Doppler correlation. But that degrades the audio.
Laura: Or record in a Faraday cage — acoustically anechoic, not electromagnetically. But that's not practical for a phone video.
Sarah: One-line takeaway?
Laura: Ambient infrastructure — aircraft, power grid, cellular towers — is becoming a passive positioning system whether we design it that way or not. The physics was always there; the compute to exploit it is what's new.
Sarah: That's a good one. And it connects to the grid-oscillation story from the other night — synchronized controllers turning the grid into a sensor.
Laura: Same theme. Infrastructure leaks information through physics. Our job is to quantify the leakage before someone else does.
