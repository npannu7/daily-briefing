Laura: Okay, good, we have a peer-reviewed source right away. What is the headline figure everyone was reacting to?
Sarah: Almost ninety-seven percent of the young people surveyed said starting hormone therapy was the right decision.
Laura: That is a striking number. How long after starting treatment did the researchers check back in with them?
Sarah: On average, about five years later. And the study defines gender-affirming hormone therapy as taking prescribed estrogen or testosterone so a person's physical traits match their gender identity.
Laura: Right, and some of those physical changes become permanent over time, which is why people debate it so fiercely. What were the commenters making of that ninety-seven percent?

Laura: Fair point. With hormone therapy, critics are usually worried about something else entirely: whether the patient later changes their mind.
Sarah: Exactly. And that is where how this study was run matters. The researchers, led by a team at the University of Texas Southwestern, looked at former patients from a pediatric gender clinic.
Laura: Like a full team—mental health professionals, doctors, everyone?
Sarah: Yes, and most patients had to go through a structured mental health evaluation first, with consent from their parents or guardians, before starting.
Laura: Got it, not just walking in for a prescription. With any survey, my first question is the denominator: how many did they invite, and how many answered?
Sarah: They invited nearly seven hundred former patients who had previously agreed to be part of long-term research.
Laura: And how many completed this follow-up?

Laura: Okay, a response rate of just over half is decent for a multi-year follow-up, especially with young adults. Still, that leaves an unmeasured batch. Did they check if the non-responders were different?

Laura: And what was the average score?

Laura: And the median?
Sarah: Zero. Meaning the typical respondent reported literally no regret at all.
Laura: That is striking. What about the people who stopped taking hormones? Because critics often assume stopping equals regret.
Sarah: They specifically encouraged people who had stopped treatment to answer. Even among those who stopped, over eighty percent still said starting hormones was the right choice.
Laura: Over eighty percent. And PsyPost notes from adult surveys that many who stopped cited outside pressure, like family rejection or stigma, as contributing reasons.
Sarah: Right. So where do we land on the thread?
Laura: I think the headline holds up. It is a solid cohort study, not Reddit hype, and the data shows regret is rare—under three percent here. You just have to keep the response rate in mind, since we do not know how non-responders feel.
Sarah: Makes sense. The main takeaway is that in the largest multi-year follow-up of its kind, regret after teen hormone therapy was rare—even among most patients who later stopped.

## America loses measles free status after decades
Laura: I was scrolling through the EverythingScience subreddit and there was this post — the US has officially lost its measles elimination status after twenty-six years.
Sarah: Wait, really? We had elimination status?
Laura: Since 2000. Elimination doesn't mean zero cases — it means no continuous local transmission for twelve months. Any outbreak gets traced to an imported case and dies out.
Sarah: Okay, that's... that's a control-systems analogy right there. The loop stays closed.
Laura: Exactly. You break the chain of transmission long enough, the system settles at zero. Now the chain has stayed unbroken for more than a year — so the status is gone.
Sarah: And the thread blew up?
Laura: Eleven thousand upvotes in EverythingScience, cross-posted to medicine. Top comment was just "Being a pediatrician is so fun right now." Dark humour.
Sarah: Oof. Then the sarcasm — "Make America Healthy Again," someone wrote "MAHA bebe! You can get some measles with your delicious diarrhea lettuce."
Laura: That one's referencing the raw-milk lettuce recall from last month. Reddit never misses a callback.
Sarah: So what's the actual mechanism here? Measles didn't suddenly mutate.
Laura: No. Measles is one of the most contagious viruses we know — basic reproduction number around twelve to eighteen. That means each case infects that many others in a fully susceptible population. To keep the loop closed you need about ninety-five percent immunity.
Sarah: Ninety-five percent. That's a tight tolerance. Like holding a CNC spindle within five microns at full speed.
Laura: Good analogy. And the vaccine is ninety-seven percent effective with two doses. But pockets of undervaccinated communities — religious exemptions, access gaps, hesitancy — drop local coverage below that threshold. One importation lands, the fire catches, and it burns long enough to break the twelve-month clock.
Sarah: The post mentioned Dr. Nuwan Gunawardhana at Columbia Irvine — "reversing a generation of public health progress."
Laura: He's an infectious-disease epidemiologist. His point: elimination status isn't a trophy. It's a shield for people who can't be vaccinated — infants under twelve months, transplant patients, chemo patients. When the virus circulates freely, their risk goes up measurably.
Sarah: Right. Herd immunity as a safety factor. You design the structure for the weakest load case.
Laura: Precisely. And the independent check — Hacker News picked it up too, eleven points. One commenter noted Canada lost elimination status last year without any RFK figure involved. So it's not a single-policy failure; it's a systemic coverage drift.
Sarah: That's the key. It's not one decision — it's a thousand small coverage gaps aligning.
Laura: The verdict: the claim holds. The US lost elimination status because sustained transmission exceeded twelve months. The mechanism is simple — coverage fell below the ninety-five percent threshold in enough places, long enough. No new variant, no vaccine failure. Just math.
Sarah: One-line takeaway?
Laura: Elimination is a dynamic equilibrium, not a permanent state — and the control loop just opened.
