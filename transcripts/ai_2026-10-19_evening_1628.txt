## Reconstructing Classic Games from Machine Code
Sarah: So I was scrolling through the Claude AI community, and I found this project that made my inner retro gamer gasp. An independent developer rebuilt an entire classic real-time strategy game from the year two thousand, from scratch, in modern Rust.
Laura: Wait, what game was it?
Sarah: It is called Tzar: The Burden of the Crown. Think castles, trebuchets, European campaigns, and little pixelated peasants gathering wood. But here is the kicker: the developer did not have the original source code.
Laura: If you do not have source code, what are you feeding the model? You cannot just feed it a compiled binary file.
Sarah: Exactly! They took the compiled Windows executable and dumped it through a disassembler. That basically translates raw machine code into human-readable assembly instructions. Millions of lines of primitive operations like move this byte to that memory slot, compare two numbers, or jump to another line.
Laura: Okay, so essentially a mountain of low-level assembly. No variable names, no clear structure, no high-level logic.
Sarah: None of it! And the developer fed chunks of this raw disassembly into an AI coding agent called Claude Code, and had it systematically reconstruct the game logic into safe, modern Rust. It runs on modern Windows, supports crisp four-K displays, and plays the original campaigns. The whole project is called ReTzar, and it is open source on GitHub right now.
Laura: That is undeniably impressive, but my immediate engineering instinct is waving a few red flags. Disassembly is brutal. Translating machine instructions into a modern language is not just a syntax swap. In old games, logic is tangled up with memory addresses, integer limits, and CPU clock cycles. How did they actually pull this off without the whole thing collapsing into an unplayable mess?
Sarah: That was the big fight in the comment section. A lot of people were shouting that the AI just magically ingested the whole disassembled program and spit out a functioning game. But when the author showed up to explain the workflow, the reality was way cooler, and honestly way more grounded.
Laura: I had a feeling. Back on Saturday morning, we talked about that developer who used prompts to generate a physics script in WebGPU. And our big worry there was the monolithic trap: pouring an entire complex math codebase out of a model in one go with no way to inspect the internal gears.
Sarah: Yes! And this developer did the exact opposite. He explicitly said there was no magic single prompt. Instead, he treated it like a modular reconstruction. He broke the old game down into discrete subsystems: combat, artificial intelligence, the map generator, pathfinding, and mission scripts.
Laura: That sounds like proper reverse-engineering. If you are rebuilding a vintage gearbox without the original factory blueprints, you do not try to cast the entire engine block in one single pour.
Sarah: Exactly. What would you do instead?
Laura: You strip down the assembly, pull out one gear at a time, measure the tooth profile, verify the backlash, and recreate that individual component. Then you fit it into the assembly and test how it meshes with the adjacent gear before moving on.
Sarah: That is almost down to the letter what he did. Claude Code would search the massive disassembly text file, trace the function calls for a specific mechanic, and map out what that routine was doing. And to keep it verifiable, the developer made the model write the original assembly memory address directly next to every rule in the new Rust code.
Laura: That is smart. That gives you an audit trail back to the machine binary. But wait, what about the claim floating around that the AI did this without human engineers having to write extensive tests for state transitions?
Sarah: That claim is completely busted. When you check the author's actual notes and the GitHub repository, manual and automated verification was practically the entire job. He built over six hundred automated tests to keep the logic from drifting between sessions.
Laura: Over six hundred tests! That makes far more sense. In real-time strategy games, state machines are notoriously fragile. A unit might be in an idle state, then switch to pathfinding, then engagement, then attack animation. If the timing or state exit criteria drifts by even one simulation tick, your knight gets stuck spinning in a circle against a tree.
Sarah: Right! And pathfinding from that era was full of strange micro-optimisations. Developers in the late nineties had to cut massive corners so path algorithms could run on old processors. The developer said pathfinding and unit logic took relentless cross-checking. In fact, he and the AI built a dedicated testing harness called Tzar Arena.
Laura: Tzar Arena? What did that do?
Sarah: It was a parallel headless simulator. It could run the game logic without rendering any graphics, playing out combat encounters and unit movements hundreds of times in just a few seconds.
Laura: Fascinating. In quality engineering, that is essentially accelerated life testing. Instead of playing through a forty-minute campaign mission to see if an archer correctly targets a cavalry unit, you run the combat calculations through hundreds of cycles in parallel and watch for statistical anomalies.
Sarah: Exactly. And whenever a skirmish did not match the original game, he would flag the discrepancy, trace the memory address back to the original executable, and tell the AI to re-inspect the disassembly at that exact offset.
Laura: So the developer was acting as the metrology inspector, and the AI was acting as a tireless draftsman translating raw machine tolerances into modern code. That highlights the critical difference between using an AI agent as a black box versus using it as an interactive compiler with strict boundary checks.
Sarah: Yes, and he had another verification method that was distinctly human: running the new game on one monitor and the original game on the other. He would literally play the campaign side by side. If a peasant took a weird route to a gold mine, or an enemy faction stopped building farms, he knew an edge case had drifted.
Laura: Old game engines often tied their physics and game loops directly to frame rate or internal hardware timers. Did he run into frame-rate drift?
Sarah: He did mention scaling problems and compatibility bugs with modern systems were the whole reason he started. The original CD had issues playing Italian MIDI audio on modern Windows, and it choked on high resolutions. Rebuilding it on top of Bevy, which is a modern, modular game engine written in Rust, allowed him to separate the game simulation ticks from the modern graphics renderer.
Laura: That is an enormous benefit of porting into Rust. For someone working in device quality, Rust is fascinating because it acts like a strict traffic warden for your computer's memory. In older code, different parts of a program might try to grab the same shared memory box at the same instant and corrupt it, like two people grabbing the exact same steering wheel. Rust forces you to prove that every piece of data has only one clear owner before the code is even allowed to compile.
Sarah: It is like replacing an old leaky hydraulic line with a rigid, pressure-rated manifold where every valve has a physical lockout.
Laura: That is a remarkably apt comparison. But let us look at the verdict here on the Reddit claims. What can we actually conclude from this project?
Sarah: Claim one: that an AI agent can ingest raw disassembly and faithfully reimplement complex systems into Rust. That is verified, but with a massive asterisk. It cannot do it autonomously in one go. It requires an engineer breaking the binary into modular subsystems and providing constant steerage.
Laura: And claim two: that this happens without humans writing tests for each state transition?
Sarah: That is completely false. The project only succeeded because of those automated tests, the custom parallel arena, and hours of side-by-side behavioral validation. Without that testing harness, the AI would have hallucinated believable-looking rules that completely broke the game balance.
Laura: It proves that AI does not eliminate the need for rigorous metrology; it just changes where the engineer spends their time.
Sarah: When you are reconstructing complex legacy systems, an AI agent is only as reliable as the test harness you build to keep it honest.

## The One-Year Limit on Psychedelic Therapy
Laura: Speaking of tracking systems over time, I found a thread on the science subreddit this morning that had people arguing in circles about what clinical trial data actually means.
Sarah: Let me guess, was it about psychedelics? Reddit cannot resist a psilocybin post.
Laura: Spot on. It was covering a study published in JAMA Network Open by researchers at the Karolinska Institutet in Sweden. They tested a single dose of psilocybin against an active placebo in adults with moderate to severe recurrent depression, alongside five psychotherapeutic support sessions for both groups across seventeen days.
Sarah: And the headline was basically that depression lifted in two days, but the edge over placebo disappeared by the one-year mark.
Laura: Exactly. The Reddit comments immediately split into two camps. One group was furious at the phrasing. They were saying, why frame lasting a whole year as a failure? If a single pill works for several months, that beats taking daily medications that cause side effects.
Sarah: I saw that. People were pointing out that taking a dose every few months could lead to high patient compliance, meaning how reliably someone sticks to their prescribed treatment plan. In healthcare, getting someone to take a pill every day is often half the battle.
Laura: That is true from an adherence standpoint. But the other camp on Reddit was zeroing in on the control problem. They asked how you run a double-blind trial—where neither patients nor staff know who got the real treatment—when one group is experiencing intense psychedelic effects and the other is not.
Sarah: It is the elephant in the room. If one person receives an inactive capsule and someone else gets twenty-five milligrams of psilocybin, participants can often guess their assigned group quite quickly.
Laura: The researchers tried to address that. They used an active placebo, which is a substance that produces noticeable bodily sensations without treating the condition. In this case, they used one hundred milligrams of niacin.
Sarah: That is a form of vitamin B3. It produces a distinct skin flush and warmth.
Laura: Right. The goal is to make participants think they might have received the active drug because their body feels different. But as several commenters noted, skin flushing is vastly different from a psychedelic experience.
Sarah: Not even close. In engineering terms, that is like testing vibration damping on a machine by comparing a high-grade damper to a loose bolt and hoping the operator cannot tell the difference.
Laura: That breakdown in the masking is what clinical researchers call functional unblinding. When patients realize they received the active substance, expectancy bias—the psychological boost that comes purely from anticipating an improvement—can kick into high gear.
Sarah: So what did the study actually measure? Let us look at the primary endpoint, the main outcome chosen beforehand to test if the treatment worked.
Laura: They used the Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale. That is a standardized clinician-rated questionnaire that tracks the severity of depressive symptoms. Depressive symptoms fell by about seven points more in the psilocybin group than in the niacin group by day eight.
Sarah: That clinician-rated margin at day eight was the primary outcome of the study. And what about that claim that relief started so quickly?
Laura: That came from the secondary measures, which look at additional outcomes beyond the main question. On daily self-reported symptom scores, the psilocybin group showed a greater reduction in symptoms as early as the second day after dosing.
Sarah: That is fast. Mainstream antidepressant medications often take several weeks before patients notice meaningful improvements.
Laura: That advantage on the self-report scale persisted through day one hundred and two, which is more than three months. So the initial claim from the thread is supported by the data: a single dose produced rapid relief, and that edge held up beyond three months.
Sarah: But then comes the twist at one year. The post claims the separation narrowed until the two groups were statistically indistinguishable. Did the psilocybin effect fade out over time?
Laura: That was the intuitive assumption on Reddit, but the numbers show something different. At six weeks, fifty-three percent of the psilocybin group had reached remission, meaning their symptoms had dropped below the threshold for depression.
Sarah: And what was that remission rate at one year?
Laura: Still fifty-three percent. Their remission rate remained steady from the first week all the way to the end of the twelve months.
Sarah: If the treated group stayed at that same remission level, how did the gap between the two groups close?
Laura: Because the control group improved over the year. By six weeks, the remission rate in the niacin group was only six percent. But by month twelve, remission in that placebo group had risen to forty-one percent.
Sarah: That is a substantial climb for the placebo group over twelve months. How does that happen without the psychedelic drug?
Laura: The authors suggest it reflects the natural course of recurrent depression, where episodes can eventually resolve on their own over time.
Sarah: A natural return to a less extreme state after an acute peak.
Laura: Exactly. If you recruit people right when their symptoms are severe enough to qualify for a trial, you catch them during an intense episode. Over twelve months, some people will naturally improve without ongoing drug intervention.
Sarah: Plus, both groups received five psychotherapeutic support sessions. That provided structured professional guidance to everyone in the trial.
Laura: Right. And after six weeks, anyone who remained depressed was referred for standard care. In fact, seven participants in the placebo group and six in the psilocybin group started conventional antidepressants during that follow-up period.
Sarah: So by the one-year mark, you are comparing psilocybin against natural improvement, the support sessions, and standard rescue medications.
Laura: Exactly. That brings us to another point debated online: whether expectancy bias explains that rapid initial gap between the groups.
Sarah: That is difficult to separate. It is hard to tell how much of the early change comes from the drug itself versus knowing you received the experimental treatment.
Laura: The study authors acknowledged that unblinding was a major challenge, meaning the dramatic early drop in symptoms may have been amplified by participants realizing they received psilocybin.
Sarah: Observational signals can look striking until you evaluate them under rigorous controlled conditions.
Laura: That is why researchers track durability over long timelines, looking at how outcomes behave once the immediate setting of an intervention recedes.
Sarah: In mechanical design, you evaluate how a system behaves over its full service life rather than just inspecting it during initial setup.
Laura: The Swedish team presented this as a single dose that produced rapid, medium-term relief, while noting that long-term care may still be necessary.
Sarah: What about safety? Some comments online seemed to downplay the potential risks.
Laura: Most side effects were temporary, such as headaches and short-term anxiety. But two participants in the psilocybin group experienced severe anxiety that persisted for weeks after dosing and required medical attention.
Sarah: That is an important reminder that these compounds carry genuine psychological risks. So how do we sum up the thread?
Laura: The headline was right that relief appeared by the second day after dosing and lasted over three months before the group difference faded by year one. But that gap narrowed because the control group improved over time, not because the psilocybin group suffered a sudden relapse.
Sarah: The comparison group caught up as time passed.
Laura: Exactly. In clinical trials, the control group is not an unchanging baseline; it reflects everything that happens naturally over time.
Sarah: A treatment does not necessarily fail simply because people also have a capacity to improve on their own.
