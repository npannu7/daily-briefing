## An AI that predicts what a DNA change does to a cell
Sarah: Okay, so Google DeepMind dropped AlphaGenome 2 yesterday. The Reddit thread in the singularity subreddit is basically "another day, another ship" — they also released AlphaProof Code and Gemini Robotics 2.5 the same week. But this one actually made me stop scrolling.
Laura: That's the variant-effect predictor, right? The one that takes a DNA sequence and tells you what changes in which cell type?
Sarah: Exactly. Original AlphaGenome came out June 2025 — predicted molecular activity across broad tissues. This version zooms in to individual cell types and states. The blog post says they trained on over a hundred million individual cell profiles across sixty-eight tissues.
Laura: Hundred million profiles. That's not a dataset — that's a production run. What's the actual input-output structure?
Sarah: So the first model took a million-letter DNA window and predicted thousands of experiments across tissues all at once. Parallel heads, basically. AlphaGenome 2 is conditional — you give it the DNA sequence plus a target condition: tissue, cell type, or cell state. And that condition gets fed in at every layer of the architecture.
Laura: Every layer. So it's not just a prefix token or a final conditioning head — the cell-type signal propagates through the entire depth. That's a fundamental architectural change. It's like redesigning a controller so the setpoint isn't just a reference at the summing junction but modulates every gain stage.
Sarah: Right, and that lets it learn how the same DNA stretch gets read differently in a cardiomyocyte versus a macrophage. The blog calls it "cross-modality imputation" — you feed it single-cell gene expression from a new experiment, it compresses that into a cell-state representation, then predicts unmeasured stuff like chromatin accessibility or DNA methylation.
Laura: Hold on. It compresses the expression profile into a latent representation at inference time, then conditions on that? Without retraining?
Sarah: That's the claim. "Scientists can upload a single-cell RNA-seq snapshot from just a few dozen cells." The model matches or outperforms models fine-tuned directly on those datasets.
Laura: That's a bold generalization claim. Few dozen cells — that's barely a pilot lot. What's the compression mechanism?
Sarah: The blog doesn't detail it, but it's essentially an encoder that maps the observed gene activity to the same conditioning space the model saw during training. Think of it like... you've characterized a material library across thousands of compositions, then someone hands you a new alloy with only three property measurements. Your model infers the rest of the property space from where those three points land in latent space.
Laura: That analogy works if the latent space is actually well-structured and the new points fall inside the convex hull of training conditions. If they're extrapolating... that's where you get surprises. What are the benchmarks?
Sarah: They tested on the TenK10K dataset — a large single-cell map of twenty-eight primary immune cell types.
Laura: Twenty-eight cell types. Okay.
Sarah: AlphaGenome 2 identified thirty percent of causal variants.
Laura: Versus sixteen percent for the original fine-tuned on single-cell data. Nearly double.
Sarah: Directional gene effects: forty-three percent versus twenty-three percent.
Laura: That's a meaningful jump. But "causal variants" here means variants the model ranks highly that match experimental perturbation data?
Sarah: The TenK10K study did CRISPR perturbations across those cell types, measured expression changes. So yes — ground truth is interventional, not just correlational.
Laura: Good. Interventional ground truth is the gold standard. What about the IBD example they highlight?
Sarah: Landmark study found a variant in a distant regulatory switch drives inflammatory bowel disease by overactivating ETS2 in gut macrophages specifically. AlphaGenome 2 reproduced it computationally — singled out the causal variant, showed it selectively boosts ETS2 in gut macrophages, left other cell types and genes alone.
Laura: That's a strong qualitative check. A known mechanism, cell-type-specific effect, recovered without being handed the answer. But — and this is the engineer in me — one case study isn't a validation lot. What's the false-positive rate on variants that look cell-type-specific but aren't?

Laura: That's the classic leakage question. If the training set includes any cells from the same donors or same differentiation protocols... even if the exact held-out variants are masked, the cell-state distributions could be memorized. Did DeepMind address test-set separation?
Sarah: The blog says "on genome regions outside its training data" for the cell-type specificity metric. But for TenK10K specifically — it doesn't explicitly state the split strategy. The independent check I did: DeepMind blog is the primary source, no peer-reviewed paper yet. So this is pre-publication claims.

Sarah: And non-determinism sources. We talked about this Wednesday — GPU floating-point non-associativity, parallel reduction order, dropout if they use it during inference...

Different GPU architectures, different CUDA versions, different batch sizes — all can shift outputs at the fourth decimal. For a diagnostic lab running under CLIA or ISO 15189, that's a change-control trigger every time the underlying library updates.
Sarah: The API is available for non-commercial research now, private preview for commercial. So labs could start building workflows around it. But if I'm a lab director, I need to know: what exactly is the model artifact? Weights, tokenizer, conditioning encoder, preprocessing pipeline — all versioned together?
Laura: Exactly. And drift monitoring. If the model encounters a cell type far from the training distribution — say, a rare disease state with weird epigenetic reprogramming — the conditioning encoder might map it to a plausible but wrong region of latent space. The predictions would look confident but be hallucinated. You'd need statistical process control on the conditioning embeddings themselves. Track Mahalanobis distance from the training manifold.
Sarah: That's a great point. The blog mentions "continuous changes in gene expression, such as when human blood stem cells mature into red blood cells and immune lineages." So the model handles differentiation trajectories. But a disease state might jump off that manifold entirely.

There are way more than sixty-eight cell types in the human body, especially counting activation states.

Laura: Transformers handle context with attention — theoretically they learn to ignore irrelevant positions. But "theoretically" isn't a validation protocol. You'd need to test: does padding the window with random sequence change the prediction for a known pathogenic variant? Does a nearby benign variant in linkage disequilibrium create interference?

Laura: Go ahead.

## FDA adds vision-loss warning to every GLP-1 drug
Sarah: So I was scrolling the medicine subreddit yesterday and a post blew up — DrugWatch article from November ninth saying the FDA now requires every GLP-1 drug to carry a warning about a rare eye condition that can cause permanent vision loss.
Laura: Wait, every GLP-1? Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, Zepbound — the whole class?
Sarah: That's what the article says. Condition is non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy — NAION. Sudden, painless vision loss in one eye, usually permanent. Happens when blood flow to the front of the optic nerve drops.
Laura: Okay, that's wild. I've seen people on TikTok freaking out about "Ozempic blindness" for months. Now it's official?
Sarah: The article says FDA started investigating after a July 2024 JAMA Ophthalmology study linked semaglutide to NAION. Agency noted limitations but said results deserved attention. Then they reviewed 126 studies covering over 134,000 people.
Laura: Hundred thirty-four thousand — that's a decent dataset. What'd they find?
Sarah: NAION wasn't common, but it happened more often in the GLP-1 groups. About six cases per hundred thousand person-years for people on GLP-1s versus about four per hundred thousand for placebo or other diabetes meds.
Laura: So two extra cases per hundred thousand per year. That's... small.
Sarah: Small absolute increase. But the Reddit thread — the comments are where it gets interesting. One commenter broke it down by indication: adults without diabetes have a baseline NAION risk of two to ten per hundred thousand person-years without GLP-1s, and about six per hundred thousand with them — negligible difference. But type two diabetes raises baseline to nineteen to thirty-one per hundred thousand, and with GLP-1s it's twenty-seven to thirty-two — an absolute increase of roughly four to eight excess cases per hundred thousand person-years.
Laura: So the signal is basically all in the diabetic population. Which makes sense — diabetes itself wrecks microvasculature. The optic nerve head is basically a watershed zone for perfusion.
Sarah: Exactly. Another commenter pointed out the JAMA study had massive selection bias — they pulled patients from a neuro-ophthalmology clinic at a tertiary center. To even be in that clinic you've already been filtered through multiple referral layers. The NAION patients had totally different comorbidity profiles than the other clinic patients — things like idiopathic intracranial hypertension, optic neuritis.
Laura: Classic Berkson's bias. You're comparing sick people to other sick people and calling it a drug effect.
Sarah: Right. And someone calculated the number needed to harm at about twenty-four thousand — twenty-four thousand people on GLP-1s for one additional NAION case over background.
Laura: Twenty-four thousand. That's... actually reassuring in a weird way. But the FDA still went class-wide. Why?
Sarah: That's the regulatory question. The article says FDA recommends doctors quickly refer suspected NAION to ophthalmology, consider stopping the GLP-1 unless benefits clearly outweigh risks. Patients with prior NAION in one eye are at higher risk for the other eye — they should discuss with their doctor.
Laura: So it's a Warning and Precautions section, not a Boxed Warning?
Sarah: The post doesn't specify which section. Just says "warning about a rare eye condition." But the language — "consider stopping unless benefits clearly outweigh risks" — that's strong for a class-wide label change.
Laura: And this is all post-market. No prospective trial powered for NAION. The cardiovascular outcome trials were required pre-approval — those were huge, thousands of patients, major adverse cardiac events as primary endpoint. But ophthalmologic events? Not a prespecified safety endpoint.
Sarah: Exactly. This is pharmacovigilance 101 — signal detection from spontaneous reports and observational data, then causality assessment. Bradford Hill criteria: strength of association is weak, consistency across studies unclear, temporality is there but confounding by indication is massive. Plausibility? GLP-1 receptors exist in retinal vasculature, but the mechanism for ischemic optic neuropathy isn't established.
Laura: Someone in the comments compared it to PDE5 inhibitors — Viagra, Cialis — which have had NAION case reports for years but no class-wide warning.
Sarah: Yeah, that comment got a lot of upvotes. "Can't have anything getting in the way of a hard dick now can we" — someone replied.
Laura: laughs Reddit gonna Reddit. But the point stands — same adverse event, different regulatory response.
Sarah: Different risk-benefit context too. Erectile dysfunction isn't a progressive metabolic disease with cardiovascular mortality. GLP-1s reduce MACE, all-cause mortality in some populations. The benefit side of the ledger is huge.
Laura: So this is a CAPA — corrective and preventive action — triggered by post-market surveillance. Signal detected in FAERS and Sentinel, evaluated, label supplement filed under 21 CFR 314.70.
Sarah: Precisely. The Sentinel system — that's FDA's active surveillance network, claims data from insurance partners — runs data mining algorithms looking for disproportionality signals. Think of it like statistical process control on a production line: you're monitoring a control chart for adverse event rates, and when a signal crosses the control limit, you investigate.
Laura: Right, but instead of a dimension drifting out of spec on a CNC machine, it's a rare adverse event popping up more than expected in the claims data.
Sarah: Good analogy. And FAERS — the adverse event reporting system — is your complaint handling system. Passive, voluntary, subject to underreporting and stimulated reporting. The JAMA paper probably triggered a wave of stimulated reports.
Laura: So the CAPA investigation: root cause analysis says confounding by indication is likely, but mechanistic plausibility plus class effect plus irreversible harm equals label change. Even with weak signal.
Sarah: That's the precautionary principle in pharmacovigilance. Irreversible organ damage — vision loss — has a low threshold for regulatory action. You don't wait for a prospective trial when the harm is permanent and the drug class is exploding in use.
Laura: Twenty-eight million people estimated on GLP-1s now. That commenter scaled it: thirty-four hundred eighty cases versus twenty-three hundred twenty in non-users — eleven hundred sixty excess cases.
Sarah: Eleven hundred sixty people with permanent vision loss is not nothing. But against the background of prevented MACE events, hospitalizations, deaths... the population-level benefit-risk still favors the class.
Laura: Which is why the label says "consider stopping unless benefits clearly outweigh risks" — individualized decision. Not a contraindication.
Sarah: Right. And for patients with prior NAION — that's a higher-risk subgroup where the calculus shifts.
Laura: Okay, independent check time. You said the source was a DrugWatch article linked in the Reddit post.
Sarah: Yes. The independent check found this nowhere outside Reddit. The DrugWatch article itself couldn't be read — paywall or removed. No FDA press release, no Federal Register notice, no manufacturer Dear Healthcare Provider letters found in public sources.
Laura: So the entire claim — class-wide label change, exact wording, FDA review of 126 studies — is unconfirmed.
Sarah: As of this recording, yes. Treat it as a Reddit claim until FDA publishes the safety communication or the labeling supplements hit the Orange Book.
Laura: That's a big "if true." But the discussion in the comments — the epidemiology, the Bradford Hill reasoning, the regulatory framework — that's real regardless.
Sarah: Exactly. The community is doing the work regulators do: weighing signal against noise, confounding against causality, absolute risk against population impact. It's a masterclass in pharmacovigilance reasoning.
Laura: And it connects to what we talked about Wednesday — non-determinism in ML as a validation challenge. Here the "model" is the post-market surveillance system, the "training data" is spontaneous reports and claims data, and the "output" is a regulatory action. You need traceability: what signal triggered it, what analysis confirmed it, what uncertainty remains.
Sarah: Same validation mindset. Design history file for a label change: signal detection report, causality assessment, benefit-risk evaluation, proposed labeling text, manufacturer concurrence or dispute. All traceable.
Laura: One-line takeaway?
Sarah: A class-wide label change for a rare irreversible harm can be the right regulatory call even when the absolute risk increase is tiny — because in pharmacovigilance, irreversibility lowers the action threshold, and mechanistic plausibility plus class effect closes the loop when prospective evidence doesn't exist.
Laura: Nailed it.
