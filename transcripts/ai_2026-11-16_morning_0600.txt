## Are Language Models Really Getting Smarter?
Sarah: I was scrolling through Reddit and came across a post that really stirred things up. A software engineer who builds products with language models wrote a hot take saying frontier models—the newest flagship models from major labs—haven't actually gotten much smarter. They're way more useful now, sure, but the poster suspects a big share of that improvement is packaging rather than raw intelligence.
Laura: Worth saying up front: so far there is only the Reddit thread, and we could not check beyond our usual sources today. So we should treat this as an unconfirmed observation from the field, not something backed by an independent study.
Sarah: Totally fair. And it hits right at why people see public test charts climbing and assume the underlying brain is doubling in capability. This engineer says that is not what they see at work.
Laura: So what did they mean by packaging?
Sarah: They call it a harness—all the surrounding software wrapped around the model, like the instructions, document lookups, and tools to run code or search files. The harness decides what the model sees.
Laura: Got it. So what made this engineer think the bare model isn't improving?
Sarah: First, once a harness was already tuned for a task and hitting a solid score on a fixed set of known good answers, swapping in a newer frontier model rarely improved their scores. Failures usually came from messy information or weak tools.
Laura: Interesting. What were the other points?
Sarah: Next, in software engineering, the exact same model performs quite differently depending on the harness. And finally, smaller models you can run yourself have gotten good enough that they question how huge a model really needs to be for efficient problem solving—though they still credit frontier models with broader knowledge and best practices.
Laura: That's a classic engineering pushback. Once people integrate a hyped component into a real system, they realize the integration matters just as much. How did the thread react?
Sarah: The thread leaned toward agreement, with a few clear pushbacks. One popular comment argued models are better trained now, not smarter—give them a task unlike what they practiced on, and performance is still jagged, brilliant on familiar patterns and clumsy outside them.
Laura: That jaggedness complaint comes up a lot. If something is genuinely intelligent in a general way, competence should transfer smoothly to unfamiliar problems.
Sarah: Right, which sparked a side debate on human learning: someone noted people can learn from a single example while models need countless repetitions, and another person asked if any baby learns to speak without years of practice.
Laura: Fair point, though the sheer volume of data a language model chews through is on another planet from a toddler's experience. What else did the skeptics say?
Sarah: Another commenter argued flashy new abilities aren't general reasoning, just specific skills trained in. For three-dimensional design tasks, they said the model didn't suddenly realize we live in a solid world—labs just buy specific design datasets for that and train on those.
Laura: Okay, so that's the "memorized recipes and good tooling" camp. Who took the other side?
Sarah: One person working on machine learning tasks pushed back hard. They said they saw moderate jumps from GPT 5.4 to GPT 5.6 sol, and the largest jump by far to Opus 5.5. According to that commenter, Opus 5.5 comes up with theories, writes quick scripts to test data and prove or disprove them, and shows better taste in which problems matter—whereas they said GPT, even Astra, is too lazy to write those checks on the fly.
Laura: Writing a quick script to test your own idea is a very different behavior from guessing the next word. Did anyone explain why the original poster might have missed that when swapping models into an existing harness?
Sarah: Yes, and it cuts directly against the poster's own claim. One commenter argued that newer models don't look much better inside an old harness because the labs have started baking what the harness used to do directly into the model itself. So all those clever prompting tricks engineers built for older models might actually get in the way of newer ones.
Laura: From a control-systems perspective, that makes total sense. Let's step back from the Reddit debate and look at the underlying mechanics, because there are big claims here to untangle.
Sarah: One is that raw intelligence plateaued and usefulness rose through surrounding software. The other is that recent gains come from letting models spend extra steps checking answers, rather than smarter base weights.
Laura: Let's define weights, so the mechanical picture is clear. Inside a language model, the weights are the tuned numbers adjusted during training—like calibrated settings inside a complex machine. The bulk of what the model knows about language, logic, and code is baked into those numbers during training.
Sarah: And for years, the main way to make a model better was simply to scale up that initial training—more data, more computing power, more weights.
Laura: Think of a machining center on a shop floor. Base weights are the spindle stiffness, motor torque, and cast-iron frame rigidity. Improving those is like making the base weights smarter.
Sarah: So where does the harness fit into the machine shop?
Laura: The harness is everything around the spindle—the fixture, coolant, tool changer, and part program. With the same motor, upgrading a sloppy fixture and tooling makes finished parts way more accurate. That's what the poster saw: fixing the fixture beat swapping in a stiffer spindle.
Sarah: That makes so much sense. What about letting the model spend more steps checking its own answers?
Laura: That brings us to test-time compute. Normally, when you ask a model a question, it reads your prompt and starts generating one word at a time in a single forward pass, without pausing to check its own work. Test-time compute flips that around: you let the model spend extra computing steps while it is answering—drafting an approach, checking if a step makes sense, backtracking from dead ends, and testing alternatives before showing a final answer.
Sarah: Back in our machine shop, is that like adding an in-process measurement probe?
Laura: Yes! Instead of running a toolpath blind, the machine takes a light cut, pauses, probes the surface, adjusts the offset, and takes the finishing pass. The spindle didn't get stronger, but the machine spent extra cycle time checking its own work—and the part comes out closer to spec.
Sarah: Wait, so if test-time compute and harnesses can make the same base weights perform so much better, isn't the Reddit poster basically right?
Laura: Here is where the line blurs. The argument in the thread is that extra checking isn't just an external loop anymore. During later training, labs may train the model's own weights to plan, critique itself, and double-check—though remember, we only have the Reddit thread and could not check beyond our usual sources. In other words, the probing routine gets built into the controller's internal logic.
Sarah: And that loops right back to the commenter who said old harnesses get in the way of newer models!
Laura: If a control system already has internal damping and closed-loop feedback, bolting an old external feedback loop on top can make the two loops fight each other. So seeing messy results when swapping a newer model into an older harness doesn't prove the model isn't smarter—the old scaffolding may just clash with the new behavior.
Sarah: How would you actually test these claims fairly instead of just arguing online?
Laura: You isolate the variables. To test the base weights, hold the harness and step budget constant and swap only the model. To test the harness, freeze the model and step budget and vary the tools and information. Otherwise, you're just looking at the finished part and guessing whether to credit the spindle, probe, or fixture.
Sarah: And because we don't have independent test breakdowns in front of us today, we can't verify the poster's specific claims or say how much progress came from base weights versus extra thinking steps.
Laura: Right. So where do we land? Is the Reddit poster right, half-right, or wrong?
Sarah: Half-right. They are onto something real about day-to-day engineering: in practical products, the harness, the retrieved information, and the time a model spends checking itself often matter more than upgrading to the newest version. Hype makes it look like raw intelligence is shooting straight up, when a huge chunk of reliability comes from better tooling.
Laura: I agree with half-right, and here is why the other half misses. Saying raw intelligence has plateaued ignores that models are getting better at using tools, deciding when to check their own work, and following longer chains of logic. It's a real shift in what the model itself can do, even if it doesn't magically know the right answer on the very first word.
Sarah: Totally. And for anyone evaluating these systems, you can't just look at the model name on a datasheet—you're really looking at the whole setup, meaning the model, the surrounding tools, and the time budget it gets to verify its own output.
Laura: That is the cleanest way to put it. If there's one thing to remember from this: don't confuse a smarter engine with a better-instrumented machine. A lot of the leap we're seeing comes from giving the core model better tools, cleaner information, and extra time to check its own cuts.

## Why the Power Grid Cannot Keep Up With Rising Demand
Laura: That same gap between raw capability and the plumbing that actually delivers it shows up just as sharply in the physical world, and I found a thread on the science subreddit this week that proves it.
Sarah: Oh, good, because my brain is still chewing on server racks. What are we looking at?
Laura: The post was about the United States power grid falling behind surging electricity demand from electric vehicles, heavy industry, and data centers.
Sarah: Data centers again. They really are eating everything.
Laura: They are a big piece of it. And the post linked to a summary from Carnegie Mellon University's engineering department about a new paper in the journal Science, which I read directly to check the numbers.
Sarah: Okay, what did the Reddit post claim?
Laura: The headline said the country needs five thousand miles of new high-voltage transmission lines by the middle of the century to meet future energy needs.
Sarah: Five thousand miles. That actually sounds... doable?
Laura: Right, and the rest of the headline said only about five hundred miles are being built each year.
Sarah: Wait a second. Even doing mental math while driving, at that pace you would finish the whole job in ten years.
Laura: Exactly.
Sarah: That would be done way before twenty-fifty! Why is everyone panicking?
Laura: That was the exact reaction in the top comments. One person asked if this was a high school math trick question, and another joked that the headline had broken their brain.
Sarah: Okay, thank goodness. So what did the actual Carnegie Mellon report say?
Laura: The Reddit poster dropped one critical word. The paper does not say five thousand miles total.
Sarah: Wait, then what does it say?
Laura: It says five thousand miles every single year.
Sarah: Oh. Oh, that is a completely different problem.
Laura: Completely different. That means we are only building about ten percent of what researchers estimate is needed to expand the connections between regional grids.
Sarah: Ten percent! No wonder the comments were a mess. Once people sorted out the typo, where did the argument go?
Laura: Straight into finger-pointing. Some commenters blamed decades of deferred maintenance, arguing that the country built its grid backbone mid-century and treated it like a one-and-done investment.
Sarah: Which anyone who owns an old house or a car knows is a fantasy. Nothing is one-and-done.
Laura: Others blamed repeated tax cuts for starving infrastructure, or pointed at data centers and said the billionaires profiting from them should pay for the upgrades.
Sarah: Look, if a giant new data center drops into a town and doubles the local load, I get why people do not want households subsidizing the wires.
Laura: Fair, though the Science paper authors point out the bottleneck is not really about who has the cash. First, let us look at the physics of why we need these massive long-distance lines.
Sarah: Yeah, why not just build power plants closer to the cities and factories?
Laura: Wind and solar are cheapest in wide-open spaces far from big cities, and linking regions together acts like an insurance policy when extreme weather hits an area.
Sarah: Okay, so you have to move power long distances. Why does it have to be high voltage? Why not just push more current through a thicker cable?
Laura: Power is the product of voltage and current. You can push a huge current at low voltage, or a tiny current at very high voltage.
Sarah: Like hydraulics. Pressure times flow rate.
Laura: Exactly. Shoving a massive flow of water through a long pipe at low pressure wastes energy to friction against the pipe walls.
Sarah: Right, you need high pressure and lower flow.
Laura: In an electrical wire, that friction is called resistive heating. The energy wasted as heat goes up with the square of the current.
Sarah: Oh, squared. So doubling the current makes the heat loss four times worse.
Laura: Right. Stepping up the voltage lets you drop the current way down, and wasted heat plunges by a factor of a hundred.
Sarah: And I imagine hot wires also stretch and sag?
Laura: They do, which is a nightmare when lines droop toward trees and start wildfires.
Sarah: Okay, physics makes total sense. Now what happens when transmission capacity—the actual amount of power those big lines can carry—lags behind demand?
Laura: You get stranded power and local price spikes. Because the U.S. grid is a patchwork of regional networks with thin links between them, cheap electricity can sit in one region with nowhere to go.
Sarah: While another region is sweating?
Laura: Right. If a storm or heat wave knocks out plants in one area, you cannot easily borrow surplus power from next door, so consumers face higher prices and blackouts.
Sarah: It is like having a world-class milling machine on the shop floor, and a hallway so narrow you can only carry parts out in a bucket.
Laura: And that brings us to why we are stuck at five hundred miles a year. Most commenters assumed it was a money or engineering problem.
Sarah: Is it not? Building towers across mountains and rivers sounds hard.
Laura: Engineers know how to build towers. The Carnegie Mellon authors—Madeline Yozwiak, David Victor, and Granger Morgan—put it plainly: the fundamental challenge is jurisdictional, not technical.
Sarah: Meaning too many cooks in the kitchen?
Laura: Worse. Authority is fractured across federal agencies, state regulators, local counties, and private utilities, and almost every single one holds a veto.
Sarah: Wait, a single county or state can block an interstate line?
Laura: Yes. Even if a line benefits millions across several states, a local permitting fight can stall it for years.
Sarah: That sounds exhausting.
Laura: And incumbent utilities sometimes have a financial incentive to drag their feet on regional connections.
Sarah: Why would a power company not want more power lines?
Laura: Because connecting to a neighboring region with cheaper electricity undercuts their local market.
Sarah: Oh, turf protection! They would rather keep their island walled off than let cheaper power flow in.
Laura: That is what the paper argues. When every stakeholder can say no, national projects grind to a halt.
Sarah: Okay, so the authors diagnosed the gridlock. Do they have a way out?
Laura: They lay out three concrete strategies, starting with upgrading the lines we already have instead of fighting for new land corridors.
Sarah: Can you really get that much more juice out of old towers?
Laura: You can. Reconductoring means swapping old aluminum-and-steel cables on existing towers for modern composite wires that stay stiff and do not sag when hot.
Sarah: Nice. Keep the towers and the right-of-way, and thread better material through them.
Laura: Another option is converting older lines to high-voltage direct current, which has fewer losses over long distances and pushes far more power through the same corridor.
Sarah: Can you bury those lines too, so nobody has to look at them?
Laura: Yes. With modern equipment at each end to tie into the local grid, utilities can bury high-voltage direct current cables right alongside existing highways and railroad tracks, avoiding years of fights over private land.
Sarah: Use the corridors we already carved into the landscape. What is the second strategy?
Laura: Learning from abroad. The authors point out that Britain, Australia, India, China, and much of Europe already use high-voltage direct current at scale.
Sarah: Why were they able to build it out when we are stuck?
Laura: Across Europe, regional networks helped operators navigate those political veto points and build underground lines at scale, while Britain and Australia used the same technology to keep their grids stable as they added more wind and solar.
Sarah: That is embarrassing. Same physics, same cables, just stuck on paperwork.
Laura: So the paper suggests having U.S. utility regulators form an international forum to copy what works abroad, while federal agencies use the corridor-designation powers they already have.
Sarah: And the third strategy?
Laura: A dedicated national commission backed by Congress and the White House to cut permitting red tape, clarify federal authority, and align investor incentives.
Sarah: Good luck getting Congress and the White House to agree on a commission.
Laura: That is the tough part, and why the authors warn that muddling through with the current patchwork will keep hurting reliability and raising bills.
Sarah: Alright, verdict on the Reddit post. Claim one: the U.S. needs five thousand miles of new high-voltage lines.
Laura: False, based on a misreading. The Science paper says five thousand miles every single year.
Sarah: Claim two: only about five hundred miles are currently being built per year.
Laura: True. That matches the Carnegie Mellon report, which puts recent building right around five hundred miles annually.
Sarah: So the headline botched the math, making the crisis look ten times smaller than it is.
Laura: Exactly. It is like a highway project where every town along the route has a veto and nobody is in charge of the whole map—the individual stretches work fine, but the connection stays permanently on hold.
Sarah: Everything stuck in limbo waiting for an approval that never comes.
Laura: That is the grid right now. The bottleneck is not the physics of moving electricity—it is a permitting system that lets anyone say no and almost no one say yes.
