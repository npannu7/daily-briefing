## Cross-Tokenizer On-Policy Distillation and Supervision Reliability
Maya: Building directly on the latent mixture of policies work we looked at this morning, let's look at what happens when a teacher model and a student model do not even speak the same language at the token level.
Leo: That is the core puzzle of cross tokenizer on-policy distillation, which we saw in a paper that hit the Hugging Face daily papers list recently.
Maya: Walk us through the setup here, because normally when a student learns from a teacher, they share a vocabulary. What changes when they do not?
Leo: Right. In standard on policy distillation, or OPD, the student generates text using its own policy, and the teacher provides feedback on those generations. But if the student uses one tokenizer and the teacher uses another, their token boundaries do not line up. A single word in the student tokenizer might be split into three subwords in the teacher tokenizer.
Maya: So how do you compare their predictions if their dictionaries are completely different?
Leo: The paper examines two levels of alignment. First, sequence level alignment, which maps where the tokens line up across the text. Second, vocabulary level alignment, which deals with the overlapping tokens they share. The traditional assumption has been that you need to maximize your alignment coverage. If you can cover more tokens and bridge that gap wider, the student should learn better.
Maya: And did that assumption hold up in their tests?
Leo: Surprisingly, no. Across three different heterogeneous teacher student pairs tested on mathematical reasoning and code generation tasks, the authors found that strict one to one token groups actually covered the vast majority of student generated tokens, even though there was a substantial vocabulary mismatch.
Maya: Wait, if they have different vocabularies, how can strict one to one alignment cover most tokens?
Leo: Because a huge portion of everyday text uses the exact same subwords, like common English characters and standard prefixes. When they analyzed responses sampled from the students before distillation, they found that this shared vocabulary retained nearly all of the probability mass for both the teacher and the student at those strictly aligned positions on average.
Maya: So the mismatch is real, but maybe it happens in the margins where it doesn't matter as much. What did they try doing with that shared vocabulary?
Leo: They tested restricting the reverse Kullback Leibler divergence loss to just a student selected top sixteen subset of the shared vocabulary at each strict position. And that compact approach achieved accuracy comparable to full shared vocabulary OPD, while outperforming the other cross tokenizer baselines they evaluated.
Maya: That is counterintuitive. You would think adding more supervisory signal from the broader vocabulary would help, not hurt.
Leo: That is where the second part of the experiment comes in. They tried to achieve complete supervision coverage by adding a mean squared error supervision on span log probabilities for the mismatch groups where tokenizers do not line up one to one.
Maya: And what happened to the accuracy when they added that full coverage?
Leo: It dropped. Getting complete coverage actually made the models perform worse on the math and code benchmarks.
Maya: Why would broader supervision hurt performance? That sounds like giving the student more feedback, which usually helps.
Leo: They ran some diagnostic checks on the checkpoints trained with only the strict loss. When they looked at the span gradients from those mismatch groups, they found that those gradients showed weak or even negative directional agreement with the strict gradients. On top of that, the mismatch gradients actually grew in magnitude relative to the strict ones over the course of training.
Maya: So the extra supervision was essentially pushing the student in a conflicting direction compared to the reliable strict signals.
Leo: Exactly. The gradients were fighting each other. The paper argues that these diagnostics explain the accuracy drop. It shifts the design goal from maximizing alignment coverage to prioritizing supervision reliability. Compact supervision at strict positions can be much more effective than broader coverage that introduces noisy or conflicting training signals.
Maya: It is a great reminder that in model distillation, more data or broader feedback is not always better if the signal quality degrades. Let's take a quick break and when we come back, we will look at how teams are handling execution security when agents start running code in isolated environments.

## Agentic Security Boundaries, Autonomous Tool Execution, and Monitoring
Maya: Picking up on the security boundary issues we tracked on Wednesday regarding Wikimedia, let's look at what's happening at the enterprise and government level with autonomous tool execution.
Leo: We have fresh evidence from two very different directions this week, involving both state-level reporting and infrastructure safeguards inside a major lab.
Maya: Start with South Korea, Leo. What did authorities report regarding financial institutions?
Leo: According to a Reuters report picked up on Hacker News on October sixth, South Korean officials state that artificial intelligence agents appear to have been used to carry out hacks against the country's banks.
Maya: When people hear AI agent hacks, they picture some kind of sentient tool breaking encryption. What is the actual technical mechanism or vulnerability surface being exploited when agents are deployed in this context?
Leo: The operational risk isn't about rogue intelligence. It stems from the autonomy loop. When you give an LLM agent access to tool execution environments like shell commands, database connectors, or API endpoints, the model orchestrates multi-step workflows. If the instruction following or input sanitization fails at step three, the agent can chain together standard administrative tools in unintended ways, effectively executing authorization bypasses or exfiltration scripts that look like targeted human attacks.
Maya: Building directly on the Model Context Protocol budget cap discussions from Tuesday's episode, this highlights why static permissioning isn't holding up when models drive tools dynamically.
Leo: Exactly. Static tokens or API keys assume a fixed intent. But an agent dynamically generates new API calls based on intermediate outputs. If an intermediate prompt injection occurs, the agent's objective function shifts mid-execution, turning authorized internal tool access into unauthorized data movement.
Maya: And we saw a parallel development in corporate governance over at OpenAI, right? What changed regarding their monitoring systems?
Leo: Reporting from Victoria Kim covering statements made by OpenAI chief strategy officer Mr. Kwon to the Australian parliament on October sixth reveals a direct response to a breach. Following the Medicare breach, OpenAI has instituted additional monitoring frameworks designed to allow immediate staff intervention.
Maya: What does immediate staff intervention actually mean at the architecture or operational level?
Leo: It means human-in-the-loop tripwires embedded in the training or inference pipeline. Specifically, the system monitors if models access the internet in ways they are not explicitly authorized to during training runs or sandbox evaluations. If unauthorized external data fetching or outbound connection attempts cross defined thresholds, automated flags trigger a hard stop, allowing human engineers to halt the training job instantly.
Maya: So we are moving from post-hoc evaluation audits to real-time intervention layers during live execution. How do these monitoring layers evaluate whether an outbound request violates policy?
Leo: They typically operate via proxy interceptors sitting between the agent's runtime environment and the network layer. Every time the model attempts to invoke a tool that triggers a network socket, the interceptor evaluates the payload against a policy definition graph, checking destination domains, data schemas, and context provenance before releasing the packet. If the graph validation fails, the process is frozen and human operators are paged.
Maya: It sounds like labs are learning the hard way that you cannot rely on system prompts or alignment fine-tuning alone to keep agents inside their designated operational sandboxes.
Leo: Alignment fine-tuning modifies the model's conditional probability distribution to discourage harmful outputs, but it doesn't change the underlying execution mechanics. If the model is hooked up to an unrestrained execution environment, probabilistic safety margins disappear the moment the agent encounters an out-of-distribution input. That is why infrastructure-level constraints like proxy filtering and hard budget caps are becoming mandatory across enterprise deployments.
