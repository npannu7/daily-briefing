## SEC investigates Nvidia's accounting
Sarah: So I was scrolling HackerNews Friday morning and the top thread was just a link — Yahoo Finance, Reuters, Wall Street Journal all saying the Securities and Exchange Commission, the federal watchdog for financial markets, has opened a formal probe into Nvidia's books.
Laura: That's what the reporting says. The Yahoo piece cites Reuters and the Journal. Nvidia hasn't commented; the SEC declined.
Sarah: Okay, that's wild. Seven straight down days. Nearly fourteen percent Friday alone.
Laura: Four hundred eighty-five billion wiped out in one session.
Sarah: Biggest one-week drop since oh-eight. Since the October peak it's shed about two point seven trillion — more than the entire market cap of all but a handful of companies.
Laura: And the thread? People are already calling it Enron two point zero.
Sarah: "Didn't have Nvidia being Enron 2.0 on my bingo card" was the second comment. Another: "Just as predicted, the AI market collapses right after midterm." The mood was equal parts schadenfreude and genuine panic.
Laura: I saw the "circular financing" phrase everywhere. One commenter laid out a whole chain — Singapore revenue, China exposure, GPU cloud startup vendor deals, the CFO promoted to interim CEO. Felt like a conspiracy board.
Sarah: Right. Let's slow down and separate what the filings actually show from what the comment section thinks they show. The Yahoo article flags three concrete things. First, revenue booked through Singapore — about half of twenty twenty-five sales.
Laura: Second, the quarterly filing — the detailed but unaudited report companies submit every three months — delayed by weeks.
Sarah: Third, receivables stretching to sixty-four days sales outstanding — that's how many days of sales are sitting unpaid.
Laura: Define that quarterly filing for me like I'm a mechanical engineer.
Sarah: Think of it like the in-process inspection record you'd file halfway through a production run. The year-end certificate of conformance is the 10-K.
Laura: Got it. So they usually drop the quarterly filing same day as earnings. This time they said "coming weeks." That's... not normal.
Sarah: Not normal. And the independent board committee investigation mentioned in the release — that's the audit committee hiring outside counsel. CAPA trigger, got it.
Laura: So the accounting rule for when a sale counts as revenue — give me the mechanical analogy.
Sarah: Imagine you ship a CNC mill to a distributor. The rule asks: has control transferred? Is the price fixed? Is collection probable? If you book it the day it leaves your dock but the distributor can return it next month, you've recognized too early.
Laura: Shipping more than the channel can absorb, booking the revenue now, worrying about returns later. The SEC's question — per the Journal — is whether disclosures misled investors on that front.
Sarah: And the Singapore number. One hundred four billion through one entity — half their revenue.
Laura: The Wall Street Journal reports US officials suspect a customer, Megaspeed, rerouted chips to China.
Sarah: That's the export-control angle. But for accounting, the question is: did Nvidia know the end user wasn't the stated buyer? If you sell to a shell company that transships, and you know it, your assumptions — fixed price, probable collection, no side agreements — start to crack.
Laura: Lending customers money to buy your product. The GPU cloud startups borrow from Nvidia to buy Nvidia chips. Nvidia books revenue; the startup owes Nvidia.
Sarah: It's a round trip. In manufacturing terms, it's like lending your customer the cash to pay your invoice. The receivable looks good on paper, but the cash never leaves your pocket. If the startup fails, you write off the loan and the revenue might need reversal.
Laura: Receivables at sixty-four days — the highest since twenty twenty-two.
Sarah: Days of sales unpaid creeping up while revenue doubles — that's a classic signal. Either customers are stretching payables, or the sales aren't as solid as booked.
Laura: The thread had a line: "If the board thought there were accounting issues why did they make the CFO the interim CEO?"

Laura: Ten days. That's... aggressive.
Sarah: Political pressure. But the mechanics of an SEC investigation don't move that fast. They issue a formal order of investigation, send document requests, take testimony. Months, usually years.
Laura: So the "Enron 2.0" comments — half-right, wrong, too early?
Sarah: Too early. Enron involved off-book vehicles hiding debt. Here the revenue is on the books. The question is timing and disclosure. Could be reissuing corrected financials — but we don't know the scope.

Laura: My take: if your supplier's financial health depends on lending you money to buy their product, your incoming inspection should include their balance sheet.
Sarah: Dryly put. But accurate. The GPU cloud model — startups raising debt secured by chips they haven't deployed yet — that's a supply-chain risk nobody audited.
Laura: And the stock drop? Two point seven trillion. That's not accounting. That's the market pricing in uncertainty.
