## Flying a Drone With Just One Motor
Sarah: I was browsing the robotics subreddit over the weekend, and someone dropped a video that completely broke my brain. The title was just: I built a drone with only one motor.
Laura: Wait, one motor? No control surfaces? Not even tiny steering vanes or a tail rotor?
Sarah: Nothing! Just one electric motor spinning a single two-bladed propeller on top of a 3D-printed body. And it hovers. It pitches, it rolls, it translates across the room, all off that single shaft.
Laura: Okay, as someone who spent years around rotating machinery, my immediate reaction is: how on earth do you deal with torque balance? A regular quadcopter uses four motors spinning in opposite directions so the body does not just whirl itself into oblivion. If you only have one motor, conservation of angular momentum says the fuselage has to spin the other way.
Sarah: It actually does spin! The body slowly rotates beneath the prop. But the real magic is how it steers. People in the comments were losing their minds. One mechanical engineer said they worked on helicopter rotor heads back in the day, and getting rid of the swashplate—the mechanical linkage that changes blade pitch as it goes around—felt like witchcraft. Another commenter who worked on classic underactuated pendulums in the nineties said they never dreamed you could solve this with pure software.
Laura: Underactuated just means you have fewer control inputs—fewer motors or actuators—than the degrees of freedom you need to control. A classic quadcopter has four inputs for six degrees of freedom, which already requires clever mixing. But one actuator for three-dimensional aerial control? You have to be borrowing physics from somewhere else. Did the author show the actual mechanism?
Sarah: Yes, and this is where it gets into your wheelhouse, Laura. The creator published the entire computer-aided design files and code on GitHub. The trick is inside the propeller hub. It is not rigid. It has an angled mechanical hinge.
Laura: An offset hinge on the blade grip?
Sarah: Exactly. So imagine the prop is spinning at high speed. The motor does not just run at a steady pace. It intentionally pulses torque, which is rotational twisting force, hundreds of times every single second, timed perfectly to the blade position. In fact, according to the post, it pulses three hundred times a second.
Laura: Oh, that is clever! When the motor suddenly accelerates, the inertia of the blades makes them want to lag behind the hub. Because of that angled hinge, lagging behind physically twists the blade.
Sarah: Yes! One blade pitches up, while the opposite blade pitches down.
Laura: It turns motor acceleration into cyclic pitch, which is tilting the blades differently as they rotate to steer! Think of it like a child on a swing. If you push at the exact right moment in every cycle, you build up amplitude in one specific direction. If the motor delivers an angular kick every time the rotor sweeps past one spot, the blade tilts up on that side, generates extra lift right there, and tilts the entire craft in the opposite direction.
Sarah: That is the whole ballgame. You do not need servos, you do not need pushrods, and you do not need four separate speed controllers. You are using the rotor blade's own rotational inertia as the actuator.
Laura: But the bandwidth requirements on that motor must be absurd. A small drone propeller spins fast. What kind of speeds are we talking about here?
Sarah: In the build notes, the author claims the rotor turns at almost twenty thousand revolutions per minute.
Laura: Twenty thousand? Good grief.
Sarah: Right! And at that extreme velocity, a control delay of just zero point two milliseconds means the blade has already swept through twenty degrees of rotation before your command arrives.
Laura: Your control phase margin would just evaporate. For anyone who has not tuned a feedback loop, phase margin is basically your safety buffer against lag. If you command a push to correct a tilt, but the system delivers it late, you are not stabilizing the drone anymore—you are actively feeding energy into the wobble until it flips over.
Sarah: Which is why the author could not run this through a standard flight controller. He had to rewrite custom low-level firmware directly on the motor controller board to calculate the torque pulses on bare metal, synchronized to the angle sensor on the shaft.
Laura: That ties right back into that simulation-to-real gap from our previous discussions. You can simulate the equations of motion on a laptop all day, but in the real world, actuator latency kills you unless the hardware can react almost instantaneously. How did he verify the blades were actually twisting the way the math predicted?
Sarah: He built what he called a poor man's high-speed camera. Commercial high-speed cameras cost thousands of dollars, so instead, he hooked an everyday digital camera up to a flash, triggered by the motor controller.
Laura: Stroboscopic measurement! That is an old-school mechanical shop trick. You fire an intense, fleeting pulse of light at the exact same point in every revolution, freezing the high-speed motion for the camera sensor.
Sarah: Exactly. He could sweep the timing by fractions of a degree, watch the passive hinge flex under load, and measure the exact blade lag angle. He checked the mechanical deflection against his control model before he ever let the craft take off.
Laura: That is proper bench testing. But what about the material holding that hinge together? If you are pulsing torque into a spinning blade thousands of times a minute, the fatigue loading on that joint must be brutal. What did he make the hub out of?
Sarah: He 3D-printed it using carbon fiber-reinforced polyphthalamide, often called PPA-CF. That is basically a high-performance, heat-resistant plastic infused with chopped carbon fibers.
Laura: Ah, polyphthalamide. Materials nerds will appreciate that choice—it is a semi-aromatic polyamide. People in manufacturing love it because it handles high temperatures and resists creep, which is the slow, permanent bending of plastic under constant stress. Regular nylon absorbs ambient moisture like a sponge, which softens the polymer chains and lowers its dimensional stiffness. In a precision mechanism, you cannot have your hinge tolerances drifting whenever it rains.
Sarah: The carbon fiber strands also stiffen the plastic matrix so it does not flex unpredictably under that torsional shock. One commenter asked if the whole thing could be scaled up to make a full-sized passenger helicopter cheaper, but another engineer pointed out that dynamic fatigue scales horribly.
Laura: Oh, scaling this up would be a nightmare. In a small drone, the mass of the rotor is tiny, so the inertial forces are manageable for a small polymer part. Scale that up to a full-sized helicopter blade, and the cyclic fatigue stress from hammering the drive shaft back and forth will tear the rotor hub apart in hours. Not to mention the gyroscopic precession loads, which are tilting forces that happen whenever you try to twist a spinning object!
Sarah: There is also the heading issue. Remember, the drone's body is freely spinning counter to the prop. So standard onboard compasses get completely scrambled by the magnetic field of the motor sitting right next to them. To know which way is forward, the craft uses a photodiode that catches a sweep from an infrared lighthouse beacon placed across the room, pinging it once per body rotation.
Laura: So it can only navigate relative to that fixed beacon in the room. That is an important caveat. It is not an autonomous field vehicle yet; it is a constrained laboratory prototype.
Sarah: True, but check out the pedigree here: this was not a university lab with a massive government grant. It was an independent developer writing open-source code and designing parts on free design tools in their own workshop.
Laura: And that is what makes it so impressive. We see so much venture-backed hype promising radical new vehicle architectures, but this maker simply sat down, looked at the classical dynamics of underactuated systems, and made software do the work of multiple mechanical actuators.
Sarah: So, what is our verdict on the post? The claims were that a single motor can achieve fully controlled multi-axis flight using dynamic blade lag, and that 3D-printed polyphthalamide can handle the fatigue loads.
Laura: The verdict is solid: fully verified, at least for this prototype scale. The flight video, the public code, and the optical strobe imaging all confirm the physics hold together. It is not going to replace your delivery drone tomorrow, but as an exercise in mechanical minimalism, it is brilliant engineering.
Sarah: Sometimes the best mechanical design is simply replacing multiple moving parts with one very fast math equation.

Sarah: Early-onset means being diagnosed at age forty-five or younger, right?
Laura: Exactly. That form progresses rapidly, and blood sugar levels deteriorate much faster than in late-onset cases.

Laura: It claimed over fifty percent of participants put their condition into remission without any medication.
