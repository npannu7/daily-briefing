## Anthropic Claude Models: The foundations, from scratch
Laura: So the thread that made me stop scrolling wasn't a benchmark post — it was someone who pointed Claude at four hundred years of historical archives and came back with a forgotten meteorite strike, lost rhino populations, and volcanic eruptions no modern record had caught.
Sarah: Right, I saw that one. The OP basically built an ingestion pipeline — OCR, chunking, embedding, retrieval — and let the model loose on scanned journals, ship logs, colonial reports. The model wasn't "discovering" in the scientific sense. It was connecting references across documents that no human had time to cross-index.
Laura: Exactly. And that's a perfect way into what these models actually are under the hood. Because the community reaction split cleanly: half the comments treated it like the model knew history, the other half correctly called it a very expensive grep with better summarisation.
Sarah: Grep with a context window. That's the mental model I keep coming back to. But the context window isn't just bigger RAM — it's more like... imagine a coordinate measuring machine where the probe can reach any point on the part without moving the gantry. The "attention" mechanism lets every token see every other token in the same forward pass.
Laura: Good analogy. Let's unpack that, because "attention" is the word that gets mystified. In a transformer — which is the architecture behind Claude, GPT, all of them — you take your input sequence, project it into three vectors per position: query, key, value. Think of it like a manufacturing execution system. Each work order — each token — broadcasts what it's looking for (query), what it offers (key), and the actual payload (value). Then you compute a compatibility score between every query and every key, normalise with a softmax — that's your weighting — and sum the values.
Sarah: So it's a weighted average, but the weights are computed on the fly from the content itself. Not fixed like a FIR filter coefficients.
Laura: Precisely. And you do this in parallel across multiple "heads" — each head learns a different relationship. One head might track subject-verb agreement, another tracks variable scope in code, another tracks temporal order in those ship logs. Then you concatenate the heads, project again, add a residual connection — that's your skip connection, like a bypass line in a hydraulic circuit — and pass through a position-wise feedforward network. Two linear layers with a non-linearity between them. That's one layer. Claude Opus 5.5 has something like eighty to a hundred of these stacked.
Sarah: Eighty to a hundred layers. And each layer is doing that full all-pairs attention. That's quadratic in sequence length. Which is why the context window — the maximum sequence length — is such a hard constraint. It's not just memory; it's compute that scales with the square.
Laura: Right. And Anthropic's been pushing that window. The Reddit threads mention two hundred thousand tokens for the current Sonnet, up to a million for some enterprise tiers. A million tokens is roughly seven hundred thousand words — eight to ten technical books in one prompt. But the quadratic cost means you can't just keep doubling. That's where you get techniques like sliding window attention, or the "ring attention" trick some open models use — splitting the sequence across GPUs so each GPU only attends locally but information propagates through the ring.
Sarah: Ring attention — that's like a cellular manufacturing layout where each cell only talks to its neighbors but the work piece moves through the whole line. Clever. But the thread on the historical archives — the OP wasn't stuffing four hundred years in one prompt. They were chunking, embedding, retrieving relevant chunks, then feeding those to the model. That's RAG — retrieval-augmented generation.
Laura: And that distinction matters. The model's parametric knowledge — the weights frozen after training — is like a calibrated torque wrench. It applies a learned pattern. But it can't know the meteorite strike from 1743 unless that pattern was in the training data. RAG gives it a reference library it can look up at inference time. The embedding model — usually a separate, smaller transformer — maps text to vectors. Cosine similarity in that vector space becomes your "relevance" score. It's not semantic understanding; it's geometric proximity in a high-dimensional space trained to correlate with semantic similarity.
Sarah: High-dimensional space. That's where the "lost rhinos" example gets interesting. The embedding model has to put "rhinoceros" and "unicorn" and "extinct megafauna" in neighboring regions even if those words never co-occurred in training. That's the generalisation claim. But we've seen embedding models fail on negation — "not a rhino" ends up near "rhino" because the vector for "not" doesn't flip the direction cleanly.
Laura: Which is why the OP's pipeline probably had a reranker stage — a cross-encoder that takes the query and candidate chunk together and scores relevance more accurately. More compute, better precision. Classic precision-recall tradeoff, just in vector space.
Sarah: Okay, so we've got the architecture: transformer blocks, attention, feedforward, residuals, layer norm. Training objective: next-token prediction on a massive corpus. But Anthropic's differentiator — the thing they publish papers on — is what comes after. Constitutional AI. RLAIF — reinforcement learning from AI feedback — instead of RLHF with human annotators.
Laura: Right. The standard RLHF loop: generate multiple completions, humans rank them, train a reward model on those rankings, then use PPO — proximal policy optimisation — to tune the policy against the reward model. Anthropic replaced the human ranking step with an AI ranking step guided by a "constitution" — a set of principles like "be helpful, harmless, honest." The AI critiques its own outputs against the constitution, revises, and those revisions become the preference data.
Sarah: So it's like... automated optical inspection where the camera system is trained on a spec sheet written by the quality engineer, rather than on a stack of human-approved parts. The spec sheet is the constitution. The camera learns to flag deviations from the spec.
Laura: That's a clean analogy. And it scales — you don't need thousands of annotators. But it inherits the specification's blind spots. If the constitution doesn't forbid a particular failure mode, the model won't learn to avoid it. That's the "alignment tax" debate on Reddit — some users swear Opus 5.5 is more "reflective" or "nuanced," others say it's just more verbose and hedged.
Sarah: The "hedging" complaint shows up in the decompilation threads too. People posting that Opus 5.5 decompiles PS2 games overnight, adds DLSS frame gen to titles that never had it. But then you read the comments and the model still hallucinates register mappings or invents function signatures that look plausible but don't match the binary.
Laura: Decompilation is a great stress test. You're asking the model to reverse a compilation pipeline — C to assembly to binary — with massive information loss. The model has to reconstruct intent from artifacts. That's not pattern matching; it's abductive reasoning. And the Reddit consensus seems to be: Opus 5.5 is better at holding the global structure — the control flow, the data structures — but still fails on local precision. Like a coordinate measuring machine that gets the overall geometry right but drifts on hole positions.
Sarah: Which connects to the "video format" thread — Frame Grid. Someone built a browser tool that samples video frames into a contact sheet, adds ROSA suffix automaton fingerprints — structural signatures of each frame — and feeds the grid to the model. The model "watches" by reading the grid as an image.
Laura: That's clever engineering. The model's vision encoder — usually a ViT, vision transformer — patches the contact sheet, runs attention across patches. The ROSA fingerprint is a deterministic feature: a suffix automaton captures repeated substrings in the pixel data, giving you a complexity measure invariant to simple transformations. So the model gets both the visual content and a structural descriptor. It's like giving a CMM both the point cloud and the CAD nominals.
Sarah: But the model still has to infer temporal dynamics from static frames. The contact sheet loses the timing — the GIF's native frame delays, the video's variable frame rate. The JSON manifest preserves timestamps, but the model only sees them as text labels on the grid. It's not processing video natively; it's doing visual reasoning over a spatialised representation of time.
Laura: Exactly. And that's the fundamental constraint: these models are static function approximators. One forward pass, fixed compute per token. No internal recurrence, no iterative refinement unless you chain multiple calls. The "thinking" you see in long outputs is just the model generating intermediate tokens that condition later tokens. It's not a separate reasoning module — it's the same next-token predictor unrolled.
Sarah: Which brings us back to the historical archives. The model didn't "discover" the meteorite. It retrieved a passage mentioning an unusual sky event in 1743, another passage from a different continent describing crop failure that year, a third noting sulfur spikes in ice cores — and the human recognised the pattern. Sarah: So to recap the foundations: transformer architecture, attention as dynamic routing, parametric knowledge frozen at training, RAG for external knowledge, constitutional AI for preference alignment, static forward-pass inference. Every flashy demo — decompilation, video watching, historical synthesis — is an application of those primitives.
Laura: With one more primitive we haven't named: tool use. The model emits a structured token sequence — a function call — which an external executor runs, then feeds the result back into the context. That's how the Frame Grid tool works, how the decompilation scripts work, how the historical pipeline works. The model becomes a planner, not just a predictor.
Sarah: Planner with a context window. And the planner's quality depends on... what? The training data's coverage of planning tasks? The constitutional AI's emphasis on stepwise reasoning?
Laura: Both. Plus the base model's ability to maintain coherence over long horizons — which comes back to attention depth and residual stream capacity. There's a paper from Anthropic's interpretability team — "In-context Learning and Induction Heads" — showing that certain attention heads implement a copying algorithm: they look back for a previous occurrence of the current pattern and copy what followed. That's the mechanistic basis for in-context learning. The more layers, the more compositions of these primitive algorithms you can stack.
Sarah: Induction heads. That's a concrete mechanism. Not "emergent reasoning" — a specific circuit that copies sequences. And you can ablate them and watch in-context learning degrade.
Laura: Right. That's the level of explanation we need. Not "the model understands." The model has circuits that implement algorithms. Some of those algorithms look like reasoning when composed. But they're still circuits — differentiable, distributed, trained by gradient descent on next-token prediction.
Sarah: Gradient descent on a loss landscape. Which brings us to scaling laws. The Reddit threads keep citing "Opus 5.5 is smarter" — but the independent check is: what changed? More parameters? More data? Different data mix? Constitutional AI v2? Anthropic doesn't publish the recipe.
