## FDA Rejects a Closely Watched Lung Cancer Drug from China
Laura: So I was reading the New York Times piece from Sunday — Rebecca Robbins, business section — about the FDA turning down a lung-cancer drug that started in Chinese labs and became something of a poster child for China's biotech rise.
Sarah: Oh, the ivonescimab one? I saw that thread blow up on the technology subreddit over the weekend. People were furious.
Laura: Summit Therapeutics, the Florida company that licensed U.S. rights from the Chinese developer, put out a press release Sunday disclosing the decision. The Times article is our independent check here.
Sarah: Right, and the Reddit post was basically the Times story with a spicy headline. Top comment: "FDA: Just drink some raw milk, you'll be fine." Another: "We love our patriotic cancer deaths." The cynicism was thick.
Laura: There was also a more technical thread pointing out the drug hit on one measure but missed on the more important one. That distinction matters.
Sarah: Okay, pause. For anyone who doesn't live in oncology endpoints: progression-free survival means the scans show the tumor isn't growing for a certain time. Overall survival means patients actually live longer. They're not the same thing.
Laura: Exactly. Think of it like a pressure-vessel test. The first measure is holding pressure for the hold period. The second is the vessel still being in service five years later. The first is necessary but not sufficient for the second.
Sarah: That's a great analogy. So the post-treatment study — the one Summit based its application on — enrolled patients who'd already tried a targeted treatment. They got the drug plus chemo versus chemo alone. The first measure separated nicely. The second? Not statistically significant.
Laura: And Summit knew this was the weak spot. They sent the FDA additional analyses — after-the-fact, not pre-specified — trying to show longer-term benefit. The agency said not enough.
Sarah: A Complete Response Letter usually cites one of three buckets: clinical efficacy, safety signals, or manufacturing quality. Here it sounds squarely efficacy.

## If elephants had gone extinct millions of years ago and we only had their fossil
Sarah: Okay, so I was scrolling the askscience subreddit the other day and someone asked — if elephants vanished millions of years ago and all we had were fossils, could we tell they had trunks just from the skeleton?
Laura: That is a great question. Worth saying up front: so far there is only the Reddit thread, and we could not check beyond our usual sources today.
Sarah: Right. But the thread blew up. Top comment — facial bones have these ridges and knobs where muscles anchored. They called them Sharpey's fibers — tiny collagen strands that act like anchor bolts tying muscle into bone. You'd see a muscly nose, sure, but not the full trunk length.
Laura: Makes sense. Bone remodels under load — Wolff's law, meaning bone strengthens where it's stressed. A heavy trunk would load the skull differently than a tapir's short snout. The nasal opening alone would be massive, and the surrounding bone would show heavy reinforcement.
Sarah: Exactly. One commenter pointed out — only mammals have facial musculature. Crocodiles don't smile because they literally can't. So the muscle scars themselves tell you "mammal face."
Laura: And the feeding mechanics argument — elephant necks are short and stiff. Without a trunk, the calorie math doesn't work. You'd infer a long reach from the mismatch between body mass and neck movement.
Sarah: Community was split on length though. Several said — without living elephants, we'd reconstruct a scaled-up tapir. Short trunk. A Reddit commenter noted the first scientist to propose a four-foot trunk would have been laughed out of conferences.
Laura: That tracks. Inverse problems are underdetermined — same bone could fit multiple soft-tissue solutions. We'd pick the nearest living analog. Unrelated animals evolving similar features fools us constantly.
Sarah: Someone pushed back — bones show feather attachments on dinosaurs, muscle scars on everything. The "shrink-wrapped" look was a real fossil artwork problem, but it's gotten better as we compare with animals alive today.
Laura: So the verdict — half right. We'd know something muscular lived there. We'd know it was a mammal. We'd probably guess short trunk like a tapir. A grasping trunk that long? That's a stretch without soft tissue.
Sarah: One-line takeaway: bone writes the contract, but soft tissue signs it — and without a living witness, we'll still pick the wrong template.
