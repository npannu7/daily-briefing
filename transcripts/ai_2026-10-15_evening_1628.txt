## Stretchtext: A 1967 Idea Finally Working in Code Assistants
Sarah: Okay, picture this. Nineteen sixty-seven. Ted Nelson — the same person who coined the word "hypertext" — writes about something he calls stretchtext. Text that grows when you want detail and shrinks back when you don't. The reader stays oriented the whole time. Gaps appear between phrases, new words pop in, an item at a time.
Laura: Nelson's Hypertext Note Eight. I've seen the reference. The idea was elegant. The problem was always authoring.
Sarah: Exactly. Someone had to write every fidelity level by hand. The summary, the medium version, the full version — times every paragraph. It's why the concept stayed, in the repository's words, "quaint." Then a developer on the Claude Code subreddit wired it into a plugin — that's a small add-on program — called cc-stretch. The model itself generates all the levels on demand. The result: you read about seventeen percent of the words Claude writes.
Laura: Wait. The model writes the full reply anyway?
Sarah: Yes. Every level up front. Token cost — that's the pricing unit for model usage — is the same. You just don't read most of it.
Laura: So it's a presentation layer. The reply arrives pre-collapsed. You click a phrase — a "stretch" — and it expands in place. The words you already read never move.
Sarah: Right. And here's the clever part: the plugin tells Claude which stretches you actually opened. So next turn, the model doesn't assume you read the parts you kept closed. It adjusts its response based on that signal.
Laura: That's a stateful context mechanism. The interface becomes a signal back to the model about the human's actual attention budget.
Sarah: One Reddit commenter called it "if TLDR and ELI5 had a baby." Another pointed out a real usability gap: when you expand something, the new words appear in white, same as the old words. You lose track of what just got added. They suggested a visited-link color scheme — blue when unclicked, purple after.
Laura: That's a classic change-detection problem. In a drawing revision, you cloud the delta — that means you highlight what changed. Here the delta just appears. If you're scanning fast, you re-read.
Sarah: The repository acknowledges it. They're on the function-hook plugin API — that's an early-access programming interface that changes between releases. Terminal clicks need fullscreen mode. Windows clicks don't work yet. The desktop app shows its own tool-call folding, not the plugin's.
Laura: Let's step back. Mechanically, what is a stretch? It's a markup pair — short form, long form. The model outputs both. The renderer shows short by default. Click expands to long. Nesting works: a long form can contain its own stretches.
Sarah: And streaming stays clean. While the reply streams — that means arrives word by word — stretches already show as their short forms. Tool calls — those are the model's actions like running code — fold to a one-line summary: "ran three shell commands, show" — unless they're running or errored.
Laura: So the cognitive load drops because you're not parsing the tool-call noise unless you ask for it. And the model's context window — that's its working memory of the conversation — isn't polluted with "I already told you this" because the plugin feeds back your expansion history.
Sarah: The developer measured it across sixty-nine real replies from four coding projects. The skim — everything closed — was seventeen percent of the full words.
Laura: And the skim carried about two-thirds of the most important points?
Sarah: That's what the developer reports. The rest is one click away. The bench folder in the repo has the methodology. They ran it on their own transcripts. You can replicate.
Laura: Good. Reproducible. Now, the engineering analogy you teased: configurable tolerance band.
Sarah: Think of a technical drawing. Default tolerance block says plus or minus point one millimeter. But on the bearing seat, you tighten it to plus or minus five microns. You don't tighten the whole drawing — cost would explode. You tighten only where the fit matters.
Laura: Here the "drawing" is the model's reply. Default fidelity is coarse. You locally refine — click a stretch — only where you need the dimensional accuracy. The model learns which dimensions you actually inspected.
Sarah: And because nothing moves when you expand, you never lose your place. Nelson's phrase: "the reader remains oriented." Progressive disclosure usually means the interface decides what to hide. This is progressive attention: you spend it one phrase at a time.
Laura: The distinction matters. In a manufacturing line, you don't inspect every feature at CMM resolution — that's coordinate measuring machine, ultra-precise. You gauge the critical ones. The rest get a go/no-go. This plugin lets the developer be the inspector, not the QA manager who mandates full inspection.
Sarah: The repo quotes Wadge and Schraefel, nineteen ninety-nine: "how to author the text in the first place" was the hard part. Language models write every level for free. They're good at it. So a fifty-nine-year-old idea plugs into a terminal.
Laura: Most of the code is Claude's, the developer says. They mostly clicked things. That's the shift: the authoring burden moved from human to model.
Sarah: One Reddit commenter: "It's wild how we live in a world where one-shot video games evoke eye-rolling, but something like this strikes me as very cool."
Laura: Because this solves a real workflow friction. Coding agents are verbose. They explain the monad, then the bind, then the functor laws — when you just needed the type signature.
Sarah: The example in the repo: reply opens with "A monad is a type that wraps values and lets you chain steps that each return a wrapped value." Click that sentence, it becomes "In programming, a monad is a generic type that wraps values, and it lets you chain steps that each take a plain value and return a wrapped value, without unwrapping anything by hand between the steps." New words appear on both sides. The original sentence never moves.
Laura: In-place expansion. That's the orientation anchor. In a CAD model, if you suppress a feature, the tree doesn't reorder. The geometry updates. Same principle.
Sarah: Three views defined: skim — all closed; full — all open; read — skim plus what you opened. The gap between skim and read is where the attention savings live.
Laura: And the plugin tells the model what you read. So the next reply can account for your actual consumption, not the full generated text.
Sarah: Independent check: the GitHub repo is real, MIT licensed, benchmarks in the bench folder. The eighty-three percent reduction figure comes from the developer's own sixty-nine-reply sample — they're transparent that their replies run long, so if yours are short you'll save less. Ted Nelson's nineteen sixty-seven and nineteen seventy descriptions are documented in Hypertext Note Eight and the nineteen seventy paper. The "quaint because authoring is hard" line matches Wadge and Schraefel nineteen ninety-nine.
Laura: So the claims hold: Nelson coined it, the plugin achieves roughly that reduction on verbose replies, and the authoring bottleneck is why it waited fifty-nine years.
Sarah: Quick recap. Stretchtext: text that expands and collapses in place, reader stays oriented. Nineteen sixty-seven idea, blocked by manual authoring cost. Large language models remove that cost — they generate all fidelity levels upfront. Plugin renders short by default, expands on click, feeds back which parts were read. Model adjusts next turn based on actual read set. You read about one-sixth the words, token cost unchanged. Analogy: drawing with default tolerance, local refinement only where fit matters.
Laura: And it's a Claude Code plugin today, terminal and desktop, early API, some platform gaps. But the pattern — progressive attention with feedback to the generator — that's transferable. Any agent interface drowning in its own verbosity could use it.
Sarah: Which is basically all of them right now.
Laura: Precisely.

## What Ovaries Do After Menopause
Laura: So I was scrolling the science subreddit yesterday and hit a post that made me pause — a 2026 study in Aging Cell arguing ovaries don't retire after menopause, they retool.
Sarah: Wait, really? I've always heard they just... shut down. Like, done.
Laura: That's the textbook version. But this paper — they did proteomics on twenty-eight healthy postmenopausal ovaries.
Sarah: Proteomics — what's that, exactly?
Laura: Right, good catch. Proteomics is the large-scale study of proteins — all the proteins a cell or tissue makes, when it makes them, and in what amounts. Think of it like taking a complete inventory of every tool in a workshop, not just checking if the hammer's there.
Sarah: Okay, so they inventoried the proteins in twenty-eight ovaries.
Laura: Twenty-eight. Removed for other reasons — no disease in the tissue itself. And they used mass spectrometry — think of it like a very precise scale that tells you not just how much material you have, but exactly which proteins are present and in what ratios. Like running a full compositional assay on every batch coming off a line, not just spot-checking tensile strength.
Sarah: Right, right. So they're not just sitting there. They're actively secreting different stuff as you age.
Laura: That's the claim. The "career change" line comes from the researchers — Duncan at Northwestern, Schilling at Buck Institute. They grouped the donors into three age brackets. The first bracket — women in their fifties.
Sarah: And what did the fifties group show?
Laura: Proteins related to regulating RNA and DNA, and maintaining tissue structure and cells. So the ovary is still doing housekeeping — keeping its own genetic instructions in order, keeping the tissue architecture intact.
Sarah: Okay. Next bracket?
Laura: Sixties. The paper doesn't give a clean separate profile for the sixties — it's more of a transition. But the cancer-linked proteins? Those showed up most abundantly in this group. In tumor-free tissue.
Sarah: Hold up. Tumor-free tissue, but cancer-linked proteins are high? That's... not great.
Laura: It's correlation, not mechanism. The paper says the fibrotic, inflammatory microenvironment is one cancer cells "prefer to home in on" — their words. Duncan calls it a potential "third hit" after genetic and environmental factors. But we don't know if the ovary drives systemic inflammation or just reflects it.
Sarah: And the third bracket?
Laura: Seventy and older. Here the protein profile shifts again — more proteins involved in lipid metabolism, innate immunity, and inflammation. Lipid metabolism means processing fats. Innate immunity is the body's first-line defense — the generic, rapid response system, not the targeted antibody stuff. And inflammation — that's the immune system's alarm state, basically.
Sarah: So the ovary goes from maintenance mode in the fifties to... metabolic and immune mode in the seventies?
Laura: That's the pattern. And the inflammatory profile in the older brackets includes collagen loading in the extracellular matrix.
Sarah: Extracellular matrix — that's the scaffold between cells, right?
Laura: Exactly. The structural mesh that holds tissue together. Collagen loading means that mesh gets denser, stiffer. In mechanical terms, you're changing the modulus of the tissue — its stiffness. Stiffer microenvironment, different cell behavior. We see that in fibrosis, in tumor stroma — stroma being the supportive tissue around a tumor.
Sarah: So the cancer link — is it that the stiff, inflamed ovary becomes a better landing pad for metastatic cells? Or does it drive primary transformation?
Laura: Paper doesn't say. It shows the environment exists. The "third hit" framing is hypothesis. And the ScienceAlert write-up — which I read directly, by the way, Fiona MacDonald fact-checked it — presents the findings straight but doesn't overclaim.
Sarah: Good. What about the Reddit thread? What were people saying?
Laura: Oh, the comments were a mix. One person immediately thought of every hysterectomy where ovaries came out "because they're useless anyway" — that's a Reddit commenter's words, not the study. Another pushed back — recent papers suggesting postmenopausal ovaries become part of the immune system, protective even. That's also from the thread, not this paper. Someone else noted the sample size. "N equals twenty-eight, not great."
Sarah: Fair point. Twenty-eight across three decades? That's like... nine per bracket.
Laura: About nine. And they're surgical specimens — women having ovaries removed for other reasons. Selection bias baked in. Healthy ovaries only, but the indication for surgery might correlate with something else.
Sarah: So the "ovaries talk to brain and bone" quote — Schilling said that?
Laura: Yeah. "Speaks to all these other organs." The analogy that landed for me: it's not a shutdown, it's a change in the signaling protocol. Like a controller switching from position mode to force mode — same hardware, different output map. The ovary keeps broadcasting, just different cytokines, different growth factors.
Sarah: Cytokines — those are the signaling proteins, right? Like the messages cells send each other?
Laura: Exactly. And growth factors — proteins that tell cells to grow, divide, or repair. So the ovary's message board changes with age.
Sarah: And the extracellular matrix remodeling — collagen loading — that's structural. Stiffening the scaffold. In mechanical terms, you're changing the modulus of the tissue. Stiffer microenvironment, different cell behavior. We see that in fibrosis, in tumor stroma.
Laura: Right. Fibrosis is basically scarring — excess connective tissue. Tumor stroma is the neighborhood a tumor builds around itself.
Sarah: So what's actually new here? We knew ovaries make hormones after menopause, just... less.
Laura: Hormones, yes. But this is the first full proteomic map across postmenopausal decades in humans. The mouse data from earlier this year showed the same directional shift — conserved across species.
Sarah: Conserved across species — that's the replication piece. Like validating a material model in two different alloys.
Laura: Precisely. Same basic pattern in mice and humans. That's what gave Duncan confidence it's not noise.
Sarah: And the clinical angle: if the ovary stays active, removing it at hysterectomy might have consequences we haven't quantified. Bone, brain, systemic inflammation. The thread had women saying "I wish I'd known."
Laura: That hits different. It's not just academic — it changes consent conversations.
Sarah: It should. But we need the mechanism. Is the ovary driving bone loss via inflammatory signaling? Or is it a bystander?
Laura: The paper gives a roadmap — seventeen core proteins that track the shift across three age brackets — but no intervention data. No knockout experiments — meaning no studies where they disable a gene to see what happens. No longitudinal tracking — meaning they didn't follow the same women over time.
Sarah: Cross-sectional — snapshot across ages, not the same women tracked over time. Big difference. Like testing coupons from three different heat treats versus following one heat through aging.
Laura: Nailed it. And that's the key limitation. Cross-sectional means you're comparing different people at different ages. Longitudinal would mean tracking the same person's ovaries as they age — which you can't really do in humans without repeated surgery.
Sarah: So verdict: half-right? The activity shift is real, measured. The organ crosstalk is plausible but not proven. The cancer link is associative.
Laura: I'd say: the dormancy model is wrong. The remodeling model is supported by this dataset. Everything downstream — systemic effects, cancer causality, therapeutic targeting — is the next study. Or the next ten.
Sarah: And the sample size... we need to say it loud. Twenty-eight. Surgical cohort. Cross-sectional, not longitudinal.
Laura: Exactly. Small sample, selected population, single time point per person.
Sarah: One-line takeaway?
Laura: Menopause isn't an off switch — it's a firmware update with side effects we're just starting to read.
Sarah: ...I'm stealing that.
