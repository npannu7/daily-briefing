## Diagnosing Patients Step by Step
Sarah: I was browsing through the medicine subreddit yesterday, and people were losing their minds over a post about a new study published in Science.
Laura: Losing their minds in genuine excitement, or losing their minds in pure clinical horror?
Sarah: Oh, mostly horror mixed with generous helpings of dark sarcasm. The discussion focused on a paper from Microsoft Research, led by Harsha Nori, testing large language models on sequential clinical diagnosis. The headline everyone latched onto was that their artificial intelligence dramatically outperformed practicing physicians at diagnosing complex medical cases.
Laura: We have heard that exact song before, Sarah. Every few months a tech team feeds medical licensing exam questions into a model, gets a passing score, and declares human doctors obsolete.
Sarah: Exactly! That is usually the party trick. But the reason this thread caught fire is that the paper explicitly called out those traditional tests as unrealistic junk. Multiple-choice licensing exams present what doctors call a static clinical vignette, which is essentially a tidy written summary where an expert already gathered the relevant history and lab values.
Laura: Right. In engineering terms, that is like diagnosing a mechanical vibration when a technician has already handed you the exact Fourier transform—the mathematical breakdown of the noise into distinct frequencies—filtered out the background motor hum, and circled the damaged bearing frequency.
Sarah: Yes! Whereas in actual medical practice, a patient walks into the clinic and simply says they feel dizzy and short of breath. They do not arrive with a clean narrative. So the researchers created an interactive sequential diagnosis benchmark, which is a standardized test that unfolds step by step rather than all at once.
Laura: Step by step, meaning it operates like an interactive state machine where each decision changes the system state?
Sarah: Precisely. It works just like the conversational state-machine workflows we talked about recently. The model starts with only the chief complaint. It has to choose what specific question to ask, which physical exam to perform, or what lab test to order. Then it receives only that isolated result, updates its running differential diagnosis—which is the list of possible diseases—and decides whether to gather more data or commit to a final conclusion.
Laura: Speaking as an engineer, that is a much sounder testing harness. Real diagnostics is an exercise in managing an information budget, because every physical test consumes time, drains funds, and introduces sensor noise or patient discomfort.
Sarah: And that brings us to the metrics that caused absolute chaos across the thread. According to the claims summarized in the Reddit post, human generalist doctors managed an accuracy score of roughly thirty-six percent on difficult clinicopathological cases from the New England Journal of Medicine.
Laura: That thirty-six percent figure for human clinicians sounds astonishingly depressed at face value.
Sarah: Meanwhile, the Reddit thread claims the Microsoft multi-agent system reached over eighty-three percent on those identical cases.
Laura: An eighty-three percent success rate on diagnostic puzzles that stumped real clinicians sounds almost unbelievable.
Sarah: The claims get even bolder. The post also reported that on emergency department admissions from the MIMIC hospital database, the physicians achieved roughly forty-seven percent accuracy.
Laura: And let me guess, the thread reports that the software outscored them on that emergency set as well?

## Personalized Vaccines for Skin Cancer
Laura: Let's shift from clinical software over to the manufacturing floor.
Sarah: Oh, my favorite pivot. What broke, Laura?
Laura: Nothing broke, exactly, but Wall Street had quite a reaction. I was browsing the biotech subreddit this morning, and there was a very lively thread about Moderna and Merck's personalized mRNA cancer vaccine. The poster was completely baffled. They pointed out that the reported trial data showed a forty-two percent reduction in the risk of recurrence or death for resected melanoma.
Sarah: Wait, forty-two percent?
Laura: That is the claim from the forum. And a thirty-eight percent reduction in distant metastasis, meaning the cancer spreading to distant organs. Yet on the very day those headlines hit, the biotech stocks tied to it took a sharp drop.
Sarah: Okay, that's wild. Usually, you drop numbers like that and the ticker goes straight to the moon. Why on earth were investors selling?
Laura: That was precisely the fight in the comments. One side was convinced investors are just shortsighted, or that automated trading algorithms were running a classic buy the rumor, sell the news play. But the folks working in biopharma pushed back hard. They argued the market was reacting to the trial design, coming patent cliffs, and the brutal reality of manufacturing bespoke medicine.
Sarah: Bespoke medicine. That means every single dose is custom-made for one specific patient, right?
Laura: Exactly. But before we get into the gears of the factory, I have to give our standard reality check. The Reddit post linked to a Reuters article that returned a hard access error when we tried to verify it. So while earlier mid-stage trials have reported similar figures, these specific Phase Three numbers are completely unconfirmed by primary literature or accessible reporting right now. We have to treat them as unverified claims on a forum.
Sarah: Good catch. But even as an engineering case study, the mechanism here is fascinating. How does a vaccine teach your immune system to hunt down skin cancer?
Laura: Think of it like precision tooling. A standard vaccine, like the ones we get for the flu, shows your body a standardized off-the-shelf target that looks like a virus. But tumors do not come off a standard production line. Every patient's tumor has random genetic glitches.
Sarah: Right, somatic mutations. Little typos in the cell's code.
Laura: Yes. And those typos cause the cancer cell to display tiny, abnormal protein fragments on its surface. Immunologists call those neoantigens. To your immune system, a neoantigen is a flashing red light saying this component does not belong on the assembly line.
Sarah: But cancer is sneaky. It puts up a cloaking shield so the body's patrol cells just walk right past it.
Laura: That is where the combination therapy comes in. Merck has an approved checkpoint inhibitor called pembrolizumab, better known as Keytruda. In control systems language, it basically clips the wire on the cancer cell's off-switch. It disables that cloaking signal so your T-cells can actually attack.
Sarah: Ah, so Keytruda takes the safety brake off the immune system. But the immune system still needs to know who to punch.
Laura: Precisely. And Moderna's mRNA shot is the briefing document. They take a biopsy of the patient's tumor, sequence the DNA, and run an algorithm to predict which abnormal protein fragments will provoke the strongest immune response. Then they synthesize an individualized strip of messenger RNA that instructs the patient's own muscle cells to produce a set of those specific neoantigens.
Sarah: Wow. It is literally giving your immune system a personalized mugshot of your exact tumor.
Laura: That is the concept. And in the trial being discussed, they gave this combination to patients with high-risk melanoma after their tumors were surgically removed. In clinical terms, that is the adjuvant setting. You cut out the visible mass, and then you treat the patient to mop up any microscopic rogue cells left behind.
Sarah: Which brings us back to the trial endpoints. The post said a forty-two percent reduction in recurrence or death. To an engineer, that sounds like a massive reliability improvement. Why did the biopharma commenters shrug?
Laura: Because context is everything in system design. Several commenters pointed out that treating patients after surgery might not even be the best operational sequence. There is a rival approach called neoadjuvant therapy, where you administer the immunotherapy drug before the surgeon cuts the tumor out.
Sarah: Wait, why leave the tumor in place while giving the drug? Wouldn't you want the bad part out immediately?
Laura: It sounds counterintuitive, but think of it like training a vision model. If the bulk tumor is still physically sitting there, your immune system has a massive library of target material to train on while the drug activates it. Clinical studies have shown that giving checkpoint inhibitors before surgery can produce huge survival gains, rivaling or beating the added boost of this custom vaccine.
Sarah: So if the existing standard of care is already shifting toward treating before surgery, this new vaccine might be fighting for a narrower slice of patients.
Laura: Exactly. Payers, like insurance companies, might look at an extraordinarily expensive custom shot and say they will only pay for it if a patient is completely ineligible for that earlier treatment. And as one commenter noted, generic versions of older checkpoint inhibitors will hit the market in a few years, making basic therapy far cheaper.
Sarah: That explains the financial chill. But what about the quality engineering side? That is what really makes my head spin. In manufacturing, mass production is easy because your process tolerance stays fixed. You make a million identical widgets. How do you maintain quality control when every single batch is a batch of one?
Laura: That is the core headache. In traditional pharmaceutical quality, you validate a single process that produces hundreds of thousands of vials. You do destructive testing on a few samples from the lot, verify sterility and purity, and release the whole pallet.
Sarah: But here, the lot size is one vial. You cannot do destructive sampling on the whole batch, or there is nothing left for the patient!
Laura: Right. Every individual patient becomes their own closed-loop manufacturing run. You biopsy the tissue, transport it, sequence the genome, run the computational pipeline, synthesize the customized mRNA, formulate it into lipid nanoparticles, and ship it back to the clinic.
Sarah: What does the turnaround time look like for that?
Laura: Earlier trials had lead times around eight or nine weeks from surgical biopsy to the first injection. When you are dealing with aggressive cancer, waiting two months for a bespoke therapy is a major clinical hurdle.
Sarah: Two months is an eternity when micro-metastases could be seeding other organs. That is a pure cycle-time bottleneck. If a single sequencing run fails or a batch fails sterility testing, you have to scrap and start over while the clock ticks.
Laura: And remember our discussion about pre-market safety margins and genetic target validation. When a target is validated across broad human populations, you have immense statistical confidence in the mechanism. With a custom set of neoantigens predicted by an algorithm for a single person, your margin for error is razor-thin. You are trusting predictive software to pick the right biological targets on the fly every single time.
Sarah: That is incredible engineering, but what a nightmare to scale under strict Good Manufacturing Practice rules. So where does that leave our verdict on the Reddit debate?
Laura: The claims are half-right, but mostly unverified. The reported efficacy figures of forty-two percent less recurrence and thirty-eight percent less distant spread mirror earlier mid-stage trial trends, but this specific Phase Three data is unconfirmed outside forum chatter.
Sarah: And the market sell-off?
Laura: That part was entirely rational. The drop was not a rejection of the biology. It was an engineering reality check on supply chain latency, manufacturing cost, and where this bespoke tool actually fits into existing clinical workflows.
Sarah: In personalized medicine, designing an effective molecule in code is only half the battle; the real test is building a factory that can deliver a batch of one before the clock runs out.
