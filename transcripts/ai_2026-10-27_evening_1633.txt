Sarah: Imagine a needle, a line segment of length one. You want to rotate it a full circle in the plane. What's the smallest area you need?
Laura: The classic Kakeya needle problem. Besicovitch showed in nineteen nineteen you can do it in arbitrarily small area. A set of measure zero. But it has to be fractal — infinitely spiky.
Sarah: The conjecture in higher dimensions says any set containing a unit line segment in every direction must have full Hausdorff dimension. Hausdorff dimension is a way to measure the "size" of fractal sets — it can be fractional. In three dimensions, dimension three. In n dimensions, dimension n. It's about how big a set has to be to point every which way.
Laura: Engineering analogy: you're machining a part. You need a tool path that reaches every orientation. The conjecture says if you can reach every orientation, your workspace can't be thin in a fractal sense. It has to fill the volume. The OpenAI blog post says Wang and Zahl proved the three-dimensional case in twenty twenty-five. OpenAI claims their model proved the local smoothing conjecture in every dimension — which implies Kakeya in every dimension, plus Stein's restriction conjecture.
Sarah: Local smoothing — that's the wave version. Solutions to the Schrödinger equation, which describes how quantum waves evolve. The conjecture says extreme focusing of a wave is short-lived. On average, the wave is much less focused than the worst-case moment. The model produced a new auxiliary PDE — partial differential equation, an equation involving rates of change — that unlocks the proof.
Laura: Auxiliary PDE. That's the mechanism. Like adding a helper variable in algebra to make a hard problem tractable. The model found that function. And the Lean proof checks it.
Sarah: The Lean file is public. People are reading it. One commenter on Hacker News said the proof uses a novel induction on scales — multiscale analysis, a technique that examines a problem at different zoom levels. That's the technique. It's not just the model hallucinating a proof. The structure is there.
Laura: But a Lean proof verifies logical correctness. It doesn't verify mathematical significance. The community still has to decide: is this auxiliary PDE genuinely new? Does it open doors? Or is it a clever but brittle trick that only works for this statement?

Laura: Good. Now — second claim. Exponential Time Hypothesis. ETH. False.

## Forever Chemicals in Tap Water: What Models Show vs What Tests Find
Sarah: So I was scrolling the science subreddit yesterday and there's a post — "US drinking water could contain double the reported PFAS levels, model estimates" — and the comment thread is exactly the kind of measurement-validation argument we deal with in medical devices.
Laura: Oh, I saw that one. The comments are already fighting about whether "non-detect" means zero or just "we can't see it yet."
Sarah: Right. And that's the core issue. The EPA requires water systems to test for twenty-nine PFAS compounds — those are per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, a huge family of man-made chemicals with carbon-fluorine bonds that basically don't break down in the environment.
Laura: But the analytical method has a minimum reporting level, an MRL. Think of it like the resolution limit on a coordinate measuring machine: if a feature is smaller than the probe tip, the machine doesn't say "it's not there," it says "below detection."
Sarah: And in the regulatory reports, anything below that MRL gets recorded as "less than MRL" — but then when agencies average the data, they treat those as zero.
Laura: Exactly. The study — out of Texas Christian University, published in Cell Reports Sustainability — analyzed one point eight million test records from the EPA's January 2026 data release.
Sarah: Ninety-eight percent of individual PFAS results were below the MRL.
Laura: So the researchers built what they call censoring-aware models — statistical tools that account for data cut off at the low end. They took each below-limit record and generated five hundred possible values it could be, based on the statistical distribution of the detectable results. Then they averaged those five hundred estimates to get a best prediction for that sample.
Sarah: That's multiple imputation, right? Like when we have censored fatigue-test data — some specimens didn't fail before the test stopped — and we impute the missing failure times from the Weibull distribution of the ones that did fail. The Weibull distribution is just a flexible curve often used to model time-to-failure data.
Laura: Precisely. Same statistical machinery. And what they found: treating below-limit as zero captures only forty-four percent of the average concentration across twenty-five PFAS compounds in about ten thousand community water systems.
Sarah: So the reported average for the six regulated PFAS — PFOA, PFOS, and four others — is about two point six nanograms per liter. The model says the real average could be five point two.
Laura: Double. But here's the careful part the paper emphasizes: these are model-generated predictions, not measurements. The study doesn't claim every below-limit sample actually has PFAS at that level. It says the summary statistic — the national average — is systematically biased low.
Sarah: Which is exactly what that top commenter nailed: "When water utilities send in regulatory reports many things require averaging. Well, if you have four data points averaging is easy. But what if the results are three, three, three, non-detect? The non-detect is typically treated like a zero."
Laura: And another commenter pushed back: "If the regulations say your average contaminant concentration over a week must be below ten parts per billion, and they have six days of non-detect and one day of thirty, they're in compliance."
Sarah: That's a compliance-versus-reality gap. In medical device validation, we'd call that a Type Two error risk — accepting a lot as conforming when the true mean is above spec, because our measurement resolution truncates the low tail.
Laura: The EPA has set legally enforceable limits covering six PFAS in drinking water, including PFOA and PFOS, that have been linked to serious health problems. Two point six versus five point two nanograms per liter — both are below the limit. But the uncertainty around that average is what matters for risk assessment.
Sarah: And the Reddit thread immediately jumps to "my body doesn't work on averages" versus "actually chronic exposure is about time-integrated dose."
Laura: That's the toxicokinetics debate — how chemicals move through and stay in the body. For PFAS, half-lives in humans are years — PFOA about three to four years, PFOS about five. So the body does integrate over time. A spike matters less than the area under the curve.
Sarah: But the regulation is written on averages because that's what the analytical method supports. You can't regulate what you can't reliably measure.
Laura: Which brings us to the second claim in the thread: "Model estimates are more accurate than current regulatory measurements." The paper doesn't say that. It says the summary is biased, and the model corrects the summary — not the individual measurement.
Sarah: Right. The model doesn't tell you this glass of water has five point two nanograms per liter. It tells you that across ten thousand systems, the population mean is likely higher than the reported mean.
Laura: In quality terms: the model reduces bias in the estimator, not the measurement. It's like using a Bayesian prior to correct for left-censoring in a gauge R-and-R study — you get a better estimate of the process mean, but you haven't improved the gauge resolution. Gauge R-and-R stands for gauge repeatability and reproducibility, a standard way to assess a measurement system.
Sarah: And the paper is explicit: "Our study only examines how PFAS measurements are summarized. It does not establish that every below-limit result poses a health risk."
Laura: The lead author, Gehendra Kharel, says the method gives water agencies a way to see where more testing would help — where the model predicts concentrations close to the regulatory limit but the data are all below MRL.
Sarah: That's actionable. Target your high-sensitivity confirmatory testing — maybe LC-MS-MS with lower detection limits — at the systems the model flags. LC-MS-MS is liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry, a very sensitive lab technique that can measure trace chemicals.
Laura: Exactly. It's a screening tool. Like using a fast optical scan to decide which parts get the slow CMM inspection.
Sarah: The Reddit comments on home filtration are practical though. One Redditor mentioned reverse osmosis, about a thousand dollars installed, ninety-five percent removal. Another commenter has an under-sink unit for two seventy-five, seventy bucks a year for cartridges.
Laura: RO is effectively a high-rejection membrane process — size exclusion plus diffusion resistance. It works for PFAS because the hydrated ionic radius is larger than the membrane pore cutoff. But it also strips minerals, so you need remineralization — adding back things like calcium and magnesium.
Sarah: And the ZeroWater pitcher filters — those are ion-exchange resin, not RO. Different mechanism, shorter contact time, capacity-limited. Ion-exchange resin swaps PFAS anions for chloride or hydroxide ions. Finite capacity, so breakthrough happens faster than with RO. But for a pitcher, it's a reasonable trade-off.
Laura: So the verdict on the claims: "Double the reported levels" — half-right. The model-estimated national average is double the reported national average for the six regulated PFAS. But that's a population statistic, not a tap-water guarantee.
Sarah: And "models are more accurate than measurements" — wrong. The model corrects a statistical bias in how censored data are aggregated. It doesn't replace analytical chemistry.
Laura: What it does do is quantify the information loss from left-censoring — the same problem we see in medical device shelf-life testing when degradation products fall below the assay's limit of quantification.
Sarah: And it gives regulators a framework to say: here's where the uncertainty is largest, go measure better.
Laura: One-line takeaway: When ninety-eight percent of your data are "below detection," treating them as zero doesn't make the contamination disappear — it just hides it in the average.
Sarah: And the model is a flashlight, not a meter. It shows you where to point the better instrument.
