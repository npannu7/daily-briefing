## What Happens to the Nitrogen and Other Gases We Breathe
Laura: So the thread I brought today starts with a deceptively simple question from the askscience subreddit: when we breathe in, oxygen isn't the only thing entering our lungs — nitrogen, carbon dioxide, argon, trace gases all come along for the ride. What actually happens to them?
Sarah: Oh, this is a great one. The top comment is basically "nothing much, unless you're a SCUBA diver" — which made me laugh because it's true but completely undersells the physics.
Laura: Right. Independent check: we found essentially nothing outside Reddit itself. No primary source, no textbook citation. So treat everything as the community's current understanding, not verified fact. We'll flag where the physics is solid textbook material versus where the thread is speculating.
Sarah: Good framing. Air enters the alveoli — tiny air sacs at the end of the bronchial tree — and gases must cross the alveolar-capillary membrane into blood. But they don't all cross equally. Why?
Laura: First principle: Dalton's law of partial pressures. In plain words, the total pressure of a gas mix is just the sum of the pressures each individual gas would exert if it occupied the space alone.
Sarah: At sea level, total atmospheric pressure is about one hundred kilopascals.
Laura: Oxygen makes up roughly twenty-one percent of that.
Sarah: So its partial pressure — its share of the total — is about twenty-one kilopascals.
Laura: Nitrogen is the bulk, around seventy-eight percent.
Sarah: Giving it a partial pressure near seventy-eight kilopascals.
Laura: Argon and the rest make up the last one percent.
Sarah: Partial pressure is the driving potential — like voltage in a circuit, the gradient that pushes mass from high to low.
Laura: Exactly. Second principle: Henry's law. In plain words, it describes how much gas dissolves into a liquid based on its pressure above that liquid.
Sarah: The proportionality constant — the Henry's law coefficient — differs for every gas-liquid pair. For nitrogen in blood at body temperature, it's low. Nitrogen is poorly soluble.
Laura: Oxygen is about twice as soluble as nitrogen.
Sarah: Carbon dioxide? About twenty times more soluble than oxygen.
Laura: Wait, twenty times? That's huge. So CO2 dissolves way more readily — which is why we offload it efficiently even though the partial pressure gradient is smaller.
Sarah: Precisely. Venous blood arrives at the alveoli with a CO2 partial pressure of about six kilopascals.
Laura: Alveolar air is around five kilopascals.
Sarah: That's a tiny gradient — one kilopascal — but because CO2 is so soluble, the flux is massive.
Laura: Meanwhile, oxygen: alveolar partial pressure about thirteen kilopascals, venous blood about five.
Sarah: Eight kilopascal gradient, but lower solubility. The two effects balance to give comparable molar exchange rates.
Laura: Beautiful trade-off. Like designing a heat exchanger where you tune surface area and flow rates to match two different thermal conductivities. The body "chose" solubility coefficients that make O2 uptake and CO2 removal work on the same breath cycle.
Sarah: Nice analogy. Now — nitrogen. Alveolar partial pressure: about seventy-four kilopascals.
Laura: Venous blood: also about seventy-four kilopascals. The gradient is essentially zero. So nitrogen doesn't net-move across the membrane. It dissolves until the liquid is saturated at that partial pressure — Henry's law equilibrium — and just sits there.
Sarah: So every tissue in my body right now is saturated with nitrogen at sea-level partial pressure. Not "building up" — steady state. Like a hydraulic accumulator charged to system pressure.
Laura: Exactly. The thread's top comment is right: "all your tissue is saturated with them as much as it can be." But it doesn't emphasize that saturation is pressure-dependent.
Sarah: Take a diver down to thirty meters — four atmospheres absolute. The nitrogen partial pressure in the breathing gas quadruples.
Laura: Now the gradient drives nitrogen into tissues until they re-equilibrate at the new pressure. That's the saturation process. Ascend too fast? Dissolved nitrogen comes out of solution — bubbles. Decompression sickness.
Sarah: Which is cavitation. Straight-up hydraulic cavitation in a closed fluid circuit. Drop pressure below the dissolved-gas equivalent, and nucleation sites form bubbles. The "bends" is cavitation damage in biological tissue.
Laura: That's the analogy I was hoping for. The thread's diver anecdote — "exploding air cylinder head," uncontrolled ascent, recompression chamber — is the extreme case of the same physics. Recompression squeezes bubbles back into solution; slow decompression lets gas diffuse out through the lungs.
Sarah: The thread also mentions sperm whales and free divers. One commenter notes they don't breathe at depth — one lungful, hold it, descend.
Laura: So the nitrogen in that single breath compresses but doesn't increase in absolute amount. No continuous "topping up" like SCUBA.
Sarah: But even then, at extreme depths, compressed nitrogen could still be problematic — except marine mammals have adaptations: collapsible alveoli that shunt gas away from the blood-gas interface, high myoglobin stores so they don't need as much oxygen.
Laura: The thread gets that mostly right. Key phrase: "they don't breathe at depth." No fresh gas at elevated partial pressure means no increased driving force for nitrogen dissolution. It's a batch process, not continuous feed.
Sarah: Okay, noble gases — argon, helium, neon. Trace amounts in air. The thread says they "dissolve into your blood and tissues, roughly present in equilibrium with the surrounding atmosphere." Same physics?
Laura: Same physics. Henry's law applies to each gas independently. Argon is about one percent of air, so its partial pressure is about one kilopascal.
Sarah: It's more soluble than nitrogen — roughly twice as much in lipids — so tissues hold more argon per unit partial pressure. But at sea level, the absolute amount is tiny.
Laura: Helium is far less soluble, which is why it's used in deep-diving mixes: less narcotic effect, faster washout.
Sarah: Lipid solubility — the partition coefficient between fat and blood. Nitrogen and argon are lipophilic. They love fat.
Laura: So adipose tissue, myelin sheaths, cell membranes — they're reservoirs. That's why decompression sickness hits the nervous system hard.
Sarah: The "mechanically dynamic" part the prompt mentions: these gases are chemically inert, but they partition, diffuse, and nucleate bubbles based on pressure history.
Laura: Right. The thread's claim about CO2 — "fighting too steep a concentration gradient, largely a one-way system pointing towards the exit" — is directionally correct but imprecise.
Sarah: The gradient is venous blood to alveoli. Not "steep" in absolute terms — one kilopascal — but consistent because metabolism constantly produces CO2.
Laura: The "one-way" language is a simplification. Gas exchange is bidirectional at the molecular level; net flux is unidirectional.
Sarah: And the hemoglobin point — one commenter brings it up: "vast majority of oxygen moved through blood is bound to hemoglobin." That's the carrier mechanism.
Laura: Not free diffusion in plasma. Hemoglobin acts like a chemical buffer that maintains a low free O2 concentration in blood, preserving the diffusion gradient. Without it, you'd need impossibly high cardiac output.
Sarah: Exactly. Facilitated transport system. CO2 also binds to hemoglobin — different sites, different conformation — and as bicarbonate in plasma.
Laura: The thread touches on this but doesn't explain the cooperativity: oxygen binding increases CO2 release and vice versa. The Haldane and Bohr effects. A whole control system built into the protein.
Sarah: Recap: Claim one — inhaled nitrogen, CO2, noble gases accumulate or alter physiological function in healthy adults at sea level.
Laura: Evidence: no. They reach steady-state saturation dictated by partial pressures and solubility. No accumulation, no functional alteration — unless you change the pressure boundary condition.
Sarah: Claim two — steady-state nitrogen saturation in lipid tissues under sea-level pressure.
Laura: Evidence: solid. Henry's law, partition coefficients, measured tissue gas tensions. Textbook respiratory physiology. The thread's "saturated as much as it can be" is colloquial but correct.
Sarah: Claim three — bubble nucleation during decompression as analogue to hydraulic cavitation.
Laura: Evidence: strong. Physics is identical: supersaturation, nucleation at heterogeneous sites — gas nuclei in tissue, crevices, surfactants — bubble growth by diffusion, mechanical damage.
Sarah: The diving literature models this with equations used to track how bubbles grow and collapse — what engineers call Rayleigh-Plesset dynamics.
Laura: Independent check? No primary source linked in the thread. The diver's anecdote is unverified — only claimed on Reddit.
Sarah: Sperm whale physiology is broadly correct but the thread's explanation is incomplete — no mention of alveolar collapse depth, no mention of the physiological model for dive gas storage, sometimes called a Scholander diagram.
Laura: So: useful community discussion, but not a citation.
Sarah: One thing I appreciate — the thread doesn't overclaim. The top comment literally says "nothing much of note, really." Refreshingly honest for Reddit.
Laura: Agreed. Connects back to our deep-sea biology segment — hydrostatic pressure, continuum mechanics, fluid phase behavior. Same physics, different boundary conditions.
Sarah: Whether it's a sperm whale at two thousand meters or a diver at thirty, the governing equations don't change.
Laura: Quick recap: Dalton's law sets the driving potentials — partial pressures. Henry's law sets the equilibrium solubility — how much gas dissolves per unit pressure.
Sarah: Fick's law governs the diffusion flux — gradient times diffusivity over membrane thickness.
Laura: Nitrogen and noble gases: chemically inert, mechanically dynamic. They saturate tissues at sea level, become problematic only when pressure drops faster than diffusion can clear them. That's cavitation.
Sarah: CO2: high solubility, constant metabolic production, efficient elimination despite small gradient. Oxygen: low solubility, rescued by hemoglobin-facilitated transport.
Laura: All of it is transport phenomena — mass transfer with moving boundaries, reactive carriers, and pressure-dependent phase change.
Sarah: And the thread? A decent entry point. But for the real engineering, you go to the textbooks — West's Respiratory Physiology, the diving medicine literature, the cavitation papers.
Laura: Reddit frames the question; first principles answer it.
Sarah: Nailed it. Next time — maybe we look at how those same principles apply to gas exchange in industrial membrane contactors? Or the design of oxygenators for ECMO?
Laura: Both solid. Let's see what the threads throw up this week.
