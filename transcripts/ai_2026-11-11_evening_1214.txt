## Why machine learning models give different answers every time
Sarah: Okay, I found a thread on TheMachineLearning subreddit: "From eliminating non-determinism to asking for bug vibes." Independent check? Nowhere outside Reddit. No paper, no blog. Treat it as community chatter, not verified.
Laura: "Bug vibes." That's the hook.
Sarah: The poster says ML engineering used to treat non-determinism like a bug you squash. Now people accept it — "eh, different answer, that's the vibe." And I'm thinking: Nikhil runs validation for medical devices. If a CNC machine drifts two microns between identical runs, you don't call it a vibe. You open a CAPA.
Laura: Right. In a design-history file, every output traces to a controlled input. ML breaks that at the foundation. Let's walk through why — from the hardware up.
Sarah: Start at the silicon. GPUs. The post claims floating-point non-associativity in parallel reductions. Which — I had to look this up. Floating-point addition isn't associative. Meaning (a + b) + c doesn't always equal a + (b + c) because of rounding.
Laura: Exactly. On a CPU, you sum sequentially. Same order every time. On a GPU, you split across thousands of threads. Each sums a chunk. Then you reduce those partials. The order they arrive depends on thread scheduling — temperature, power state, what else is running.
Sarah: So the exact same tensor, same code, same seed — different GPU, different driver, different day — you get a different result in the last decimal places.
Laura: And in deep learning, those tiny differences amplify. Backpropagation is a chain of matrix multiplications. A one-bit difference in layer three becomes a completely different weight update by layer twelve. Chaos theory — sensitivity to initial conditions.
Sarah: Like a CNC tool path. Same G-code. But spindle wear, thermal expansion, coolant temp — the actual cut drifts. Same nominal input, different physical reality.
Laura: Good analogy. Except in machining, you measure the part. In ML, the "part" is a probability distribution. You can't stick a CMM probe on it.
Sarah: Next: dropout. A regularization technique — randomly zeroing neurons during training so the network doesn't over-rely on any single pathway.
Laura: Intentional stochasticity. You set a seed for the random number generator, but the post's second claim: seed control alone doesn't guarantee reproducibility across hardware or library versions.
Sarah: Because the RNG implementation changes? PyTorch 2.0 vs 2.1 might use a different algorithm for the same seed?
Laura: Or the same algorithm but different vectorization. CUDA 11 vs 12 might parallelize the RNG differently. The sequence diverges after a few thousand calls. A training run? Billions of calls.
Sarah: So even if you freeze the seed, code, data — upgrade the driver, you get a different model.
Laura: Exactly. Like heat-treating Inconel. Same furnace profile, same alloy lot. But the thermocouple drifted two degrees, or gas flow varied one percent. The microstructure changes. You can't see it until you test the coupons.
Sarah: Third source: sampling temperature. Inference-time. When the model generates text, it doesn't just pick the highest-probability token. It samples from a distribution. Temperature controls how sharp that distribution is. Zero — greedy, deterministic. One — proportional to probabilities. Above one — flatter, more creative.
Laura: And that's a user-facing knob. In a regulated system, you'd lock that parameter in the design-history file. Document the exact value. Justify why. Validate the output distribution stays within spec.
Sarah: But here's where "bug vibes" hits hard. The thread says people just don't. They run at temperature 0.7 because "it feels right." No traceability. No validation protocol.
Laura: I've seen this in simulation-driven design. Engineers run Monte Carlo with different seeds until they like the result. Call it "exploring the design space." But for FDA, you need the seed, sample count, convergence criteria — all locked before you hit run.
Sarah: The post also mentions parallel reduction order in layer normalization and softmax. Same floating-point associativity problem, baked into the architecture.
Laura: Layer norm computes mean and variance across a feature dimension — a reduction. Softmax sums exponentials — another reduction. Every forward pass, every backward pass. Thousands of non-associative sums per batch.
Sarah: So even inference isn't safe. Same input, same weights, different GPU — different logits.
Laura: Right. And if you're doing beam search or nucleus sampling on top? The divergence compounds. Like measuring a surface profile with a stylus. The stylus radius is your floating-point precision. The traverse speed is your parallel reduction order. Change either, the reported roughness changes.
Sarah: Okay, but the thread frames this as a cultural shift. "We used to fight non-determinism. Now we ask for bug vibes." Is that real? Or just one person's frustration?
Laura: That's the unconfirmed part. But I've seen the shift in literature. Ten years ago, papers reported "mean over five seeds." Now? Single seed. "We set seed 42." As if that's sufficient. It's not. Seed 42 on an A100 with PyTorch 2.3 is not seed 42 on an H100 with PyTorch 2.5.
Sarah: And the Monday episode — Apple's humanoid robot running locally on a Mac Mini. Sim-to-real validation. They had to prove the policy trained in simulation transfers to hardware. If the simulator has non-determinism, and the policy has non-determinism... how do you even write the test protocol?
Laura: You bound it. Run enough episodes for statistical confidence. Treat the policy as a stochastic process. But that requires defining acceptance criteria in probabilistic terms. "Ninety-five percent of episodes achieve success rate above ninety percent." That's a validation protocol. Not "bug vibes."
Sarah: Tuesday's single-neuron interpretability piece — the Qwen neuron that flips the model into poetic errors. That's a validation evidence problem. If you can't reproduce the activation pattern, you can't use it as evidence in a design-history review.
Laura: Exactly. Mechanistic interpretability needs deterministic forward passes. If the same input produces different neuron activations because of floating-point drift... your "circuit" isn't a circuit. It's a cloud.
Sarah: So what would a design-history file for an ML component actually capture? Let's spec it out.
Laura: Start with the compute environment. GPU model, driver version, CUDA version, cuDNN version, PyTorch version, every dependency hash. That's your machine serial number.

Laura: That's periodic revalidation. You'd need a reference dataset — golden samples — run through the model on a schedule. Track the output distribution. KL divergence from baseline. If it shifts beyond a threshold, that's a signal. Open a CAPA.
Sarah: But the thread says the community has given up. "Bug vibes." Is that because it's genuinely impossible to control? Or because it's expensive?
Laura: Both. True determinism — bitwise reproducibility — requires single-threaded CPU execution. No dropout. No data augmentation randomness. Temperature zero. That's not how you get good models. The stochasticity is functional. It's what lets the model generalize.
Sarah: So you're saying non-determinism isn't a bug. It's a feature. Like surface roughness. You don't want zero roughness. You want controlled roughness. Specified Ra, Rz, with a process that delivers it consistently.
Laura: Exactly. The problem isn't non-determinism. It's uncontrolled non-determinism. The community treats the two as the same. "It's random anyway, why bother?" But in regulated software, you don't eliminate variation. You characterize it. You bound it. You prove it doesn't affect safety.
Sarah: And the tools for that... they're immature. No "GPU floating-point determinism mode" that doesn't tank performance. NVIDIA has a flag — CUBLASLT_MATMUL_PREFER_REPRODUCIBILITY — but it's not universal, and it's slow.
Laura: Intel's oneAPI has similar. But it's patchwork. And it doesn't solve the RNG divergence across versions. The fundamental issue: hardware is designed for throughput, not reproducibility. The software stack assumes you don't care about bitwise identity.
Sarah: Which makes sense for recommending movies. Not for dosing insulin.
Laura: Right. And that's the gap. The ML community optimizes for leaderboard metrics. Regulated industries need traceability. Different objective functions.
Sarah: So if Nikhil's team wants to deploy an ML component — say, a vision model for visual inspection — what's the minimum viable DHF today?
Laura: Lock the compute environment in a container. Docker image with pinned hashes. Lock the data. Lock the config. Run training three times from different seeds — characterize the output variance. Sarah: Not "bug vibes."
Laura: Not bug vibes. You define the allowable output distribution shift. You validate that your training process — with all its stochasticity — produces models within that distribution. ## The quantum Mpemba effect: when a hotter qubit reaches equilibrium faster
Laura: So we're sticking with thermodynamics, but going quantum — and this one made me pause because the headline sounds like a paradox: a hotter qubit settling into equilibrium faster than a cooler one.
Sarah: Wait, really? That's the Mpemba effect, right? Hot water freezes faster than cold?
Laura: Exactly. Named after Erasto Mpemba, a Tanzanian student who noticed it in the nineteen-sixties making ice cream. The classic version is messy — convection, evaporation, supercooling, dissolved gases — people still argue which mechanism dominates. But the strong Mpemba effect is cleaner: it's purely about relaxation timescales.
Sarah: Okay, define "relaxation timescale" for me like I'm a mechanical engineer.
Laura: Sure. Imagine you quench a steel workpiece — drop it from austenitizing temperature into oil. The microstructure transforms toward equilibrium. The relaxation time is how long that transformation takes. Normally, the farther you start from equilibrium — say, a higher initial temperature — the longer it takes. More thermal distance to cover.
Sarah: Right, more energy to dump. But the strong Mpemba effect says... sometimes the hotter one skips a step?
Laura: Precisely. If the cooler initial state happens to sit in a metastable basin — like retained austenite that wants to become bainite but gets stuck — it decays slowly. The hotter state, with more energy, can hop over that barrier entirely and go straight to martensite. Less time in the metastable trap.
Sarah: That's wild. So it's not about total energy — it's about which decay channels are populated initially.
Laura: Yes. The relaxation isn't a single exponential. It's a sum of modes, each with its own decay rate. The slowest mode dominates the long-time approach. If your initial state has zero overlap with that slowest mode — you've "eliminated" it — you relax faster, even though you started farther away.
Sarah: And this new paper — Zhan and colleagues, Physical Review Letters, twenty twenty-six — they did this with a single qubit? Optically?
Laura: That's the claim on the Physics subreddit. A team at Southeast University in China. They encode the qubit in a photon's polarization — horizontal and vertical states. The "temperature" isn't thermal; it's the population imbalance between those two states. More imbalance equals higher effective energy.
Sarah: So they prepare different initial imbalances — some "hotter," some "cooler" — and watch how fast each decays to the thermal equilibrium distribution?
Laura: Exactly. They couple the qubit to a engineered reservoir using waveplates and liquid-crystal modulators. It's a programmable open quantum system. The master equation — that's the Lindblad equation, the quantum version of a rate equation — governs the populations. Coherences, the off-diagonal elements of the density matrix, they actively suppress those. That's the "genuine quantum" part: they isolate the population dynamics from coherence effects.
Sarah: Lindblad equation — that's like... a set of coupled differential equations for the state probabilities, with jump operators describing dissipation?
Laura: Spot on. Think of it like a state-space model in control theory: x-dot equals A x plus noise. The eigenvalues of A give the decay rates. The slowest eigenvalue — the spectral gap — sets the asymptotic relaxation time. The strong Mpemba effect happens when the initial condition has no projection onto that slowest eigenmode.
Sarah: And they measured this non-monotonic relaxation time? Like, plotted equilibration time versus initial energy and saw a dip?
Laura: That's what the phys.org summary says — though I couldn't open the link, it returned a four-oh-three. The Reddit post quotes the abstract: "non-monotonic relaxation time as a function of initial energy." They claim to see the dip where the hotter state wins.
Sarah: Okay, independent check time. You said the phys.org link failed?
Laura: Failed. And I couldn't find the PRL paper on arXiv or the journal site yet — might be too new, or behind the paywall. So right now, the only source is the Reddit post itself, which quotes the abstract and the phys.org press release. No independent verification.
Sarah: So we treat it as unconfirmed. Got it. But assuming the experiment works as described — this connects directly to Saturday's H-theorem and fluctuation theorems, doesn't it?
Laura: It does. The H-theorem says entropy production is non-negative for Markovian master equations — the quantum version of Boltzmann's H. But the path to equilibrium can have transient entropy decreases if you start in a special state. The strong Mpemba effect is one manifestation: you prepare a state that avoids the slowest relaxation channel, so entropy production rate is higher initially, even though total entropy change is the same.
Sarah: And fluctuation theorems — Crooks, Jarzynski — they quantify the probability of those transient entropy-decreasing trajectories?
Laura: Exactly. In a driven open system, the work distribution satisfies the Crooks relation. The Mpemba effect means certain initial states have a higher probability of "fast" relaxation trajectories. This experiment, if real, gives a clean single-qubit testbed: you can prepare the initial state precisely, measure the full trajectory statistics, and check the fluctuation theorems mode by mode.
Sarah: That's cool. A quantum simulator for non-equilibrium thermodynamics. But — friendly pushback — is a single qubit enough? Real systems have many degrees of freedom. The Mpemba effect in water might need hydrogen-bond network rearrangements, not just two-level populations.
Laura: Fair point. The strong Mpemba effect can exist in a two-level system if the reservoir is engineered right — the Lindblad operators need specific asymmetry. But in nature, the slowest mode often comes from a conserved quantity or a symmetry. A single qubit with a tailored bath is a minimal model. It proves the mechanism can work without many-body complexity. Whether it explains water... different question.
Sarah: So it's like... validating a constitutive model on a coupon specimen before you trust it for a full forging simulation?
Laura: Laughs Yes. Exactly. You isolate the mechanism — eliminate coherence, control the spectrum — so you can say "this term in the master equation causes the non-monotonicity." Then you ask: does the same term appear in the many-body master equation for water?

Sarah: Phase-covariant — that means the dissipation treats all phases equally, so off-diagonals decay independently?
Laura: Right. The Lindblad operators are proportional to sigma-plus and sigma-minus — raising and lowering operators. No sigma-z terms. So populations and coherences decouple. Smart experimental design.

Laura: Perfect. And the "strong" qualifier means this happens for any cooler initial state in a certain range — not just a fine-tuned one. The relaxation time as a function of initial energy has a genuine local maximum. You can be colder and slower.
