## Scaling RL Post-Training: TRACE FP4 MoE Rollouts and LoGRA Gradient Sketches
Maya: Let's turn to reinforcement learning post-training, where the primary bottleneck has quietly shifted from raw compute in the backward pass to memory capacity during rollouts and gradient synchronization.
Leo: That shift has been building for a while. We touched on on-policy update stability earlier this week with representation distillation, but the hardware realities are straightforward: on-policy reinforcement learning alternates between generating candidate rollouts and computing gradient updates over those trajectories. Generating rollouts is memory-bound. You have parameters, activations, and massive key-value caches taking up high-bandwidth memory. Then, in the optimization phase, storing optimizer states for large models consumes whatever headroom is left.
Maya: Which brings us to two papers published this week tackling both sides of that memory wall. The first is TRACE, which targets four-bit floating-point rollouts for Mixture-of-Experts models. Now, FP4 quantization is common for pure inference, but why has running reinforcement learning rollouts in FP4 been such a headache historically?
Leo: The core obstacle is what researchers call train-rollout discrepancy. In standard reinforcement learning, your policy generates trajectories under one set of numerical conditions, and then the training phase evaluates the log probabilities of those actions to calculate policy gradients. If you quantize weights, activations, and key-value caches down to FP4 for rollout generation, the model's outputs reflect specific rounding choices. If your training pass then evaluates those actions in higher precision like bfloat sixteen, or even with an independently quantized FP4 model, the probability distributions do not match. The policy gradient ends up optimizing for actions the rollout policy did not actually take.
Maya: In other words, the policy gradient is learning from phantom trajectories.
Leo: Exactly. And with Mixture-of-Experts architectures, the routing decisions can flip entirely if activation rounding changes the gating logits. If expert routing diverges between rollout and training, your gradient signal degrades very rapidly.
Maya: So how does TRACE bridge that gap without giving up the performance gains of four-bit execution?
Leo: TRACE stands for Train-Rollout Quantization Alignment via Compact Guidance. Instead of treating rollout quantization and training quantization as two isolated steps, it uses rollout-guided quantization-aware training. When the rollout phase generates tokens in FP4, TRACE captures the actual quantization outcomesâspecifically the scale factors and mantissa roundings. It then feeds those outcomes into the training path to guide how FP4 rounding decisions are made during the forward pass of the policy update.
Maya: But capturing and storing rounding decisions across hundreds of billions of parameters sounds like it would completely wipe out any memory savings you gained from using four-bit precision in the first place.
Leo: That is the obvious trade-off, and that is where their caching scheme comes in. Rather than caching everything across every single transformer block, TRACE selectively retains mantissa and scale data from the deeper layers of the network. The authors found that numerical divergence is far more sensitive in later layers, so selectively caching downstream states prevents exponential drift while keeping the storage and communication overhead small enough to maintain throughput.
Maya: What were the actual measured gains across their test suite?
Leo: They evaluated TRACE across four large Mixture-of-Experts architectures on reasoning, coding, and long-horizon tasks. The setup ran joint FP4 weights, FP4 activations, and FP4 key-value caches during rollouts. They measured up to a five point four times speedup in rollout generation compared to bfloat sixteen baselines, while matching the task performance of unquantized bfloat sixteen training. Post-hoc quantization, where you train in bfloat sixteen and compress to FP4 after the fact, showed substantial degradation by comparison.
Maya: That fixes the rollout side of the loop. But once you have the trajectories, you still have to update the policy parameters. That leads us to the second paper: LoGRA, which tackles the optimizer footprint.
Leo: Right. LoGRA stands for Low-Rank Gradient Sketches. When you run standard Adam updates on a full model, the optimizer must store the first and second moments of the gradients for every single trainable parameter. For a model with tens of billions of parameters, those optimizer states routinely exceed the memory capacity of individual accelerator nodes.
Maya: We have seen parameter-efficient fine-tuning like LoRA handle this for weights, but LoGRA is doing something different by focusing on the gradients themselves.
Leo: Exactly. LoGRA maintains the full parameter set, but it compresses the gradient tensor into a low-rank sketch. Instead of storing and synchronizing dense gradients across the cluster, it projects them into compact representations that capture the dominant directions of the gradient. That immediately slashes both parameter synchronization bandwidth and optimizer state memory.
Maya: Compressing gradients usually risks unstable updates, especially in on-policy reinforcement learning where a single bad update can ruin policy stability. How did they keep training stable?
Leo: They paired the low-rank sketches with what they call predicted Kullback-Leibler step control. Before applying an update, the algorithm estimates how far the proposed step will push the policy's action distribution away from the current distribution. If the projected step exceeds a safe threshold, the step control mechanism scales down the magnitude of that update before it is committed.
Maya: Did that actually hold up under testing?
Leo: In their reasoning experiments, LoGRA cut average training memory by up to forty-five point seven percent without performance loss. The concrete hardware demonstration was training a twenty-seven billion parameter model for over eleven hundred steps on a single eight-GPU node. On that exact same hardware setup, standard dense Adam ran out of memory and failed to train.
Maya: So between TRACE compressing rollout states and LoGRA sketching optimizer gradients, the envelope for on-policy post-training is being pushed directly into commodity clusters.
Leo: It turns what used to require dedicated multi-node superclusters into workloads that fit within single nodes.

## Representation-Space MMD: Stabilizing Diffusion Language Models
Maya: Let us shift from execution rollouts to diffusion language models, where the stability challenge is happening at the representation level. On Wednesday morning, we looked at Pivot-SD and how credit assignment breaks down across diffusion denoising steps. A new paper out on arXiv this week takes aim at a related bottleneck: post-training stability.
Leo: Right. If you step back, autoregressive language models have a very clean, straightforward objective during pretraining and supervised tuning: next-token cross-entropy. Diffusion language models, or DLMs, do not have that luxury. Whether they work in discrete token space with masking schedules or in continuous latent space, post-training them has historically been messy.
Maya: Because you are dealing with multi-step iterative denoising rather than a single causal forward pass.
Leo: Exactly. If you want to optimize a generated sequence against a target distribution, the standard approaches force you to either unroll the entire reverse sampling trajectoryâwhich is computationally punishingâor train a separate auxiliary model like a discriminator or reward critic. Alternatively, you compute losses over noisy discrete token outputs, where gradients become high-variance and unstable.
Maya: Which leads to the core mechanism in this new paper: Representation-Space Maximum Mean Discrepancy, or MMD. Leo, break down what MMD is doing here and why moving it into feature space solves that training headache.
Leo: Maximum Mean Discrepancy is a statistical measure of distance between two probability distributions. Instead of comparing individual tokens or raw sequence matches, MMD calculates how far apart two distributions are based on the mean embeddings of their samples in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. The authors take a frozen, pretrained DLM and use its contextual representation layers as that feature space.
Maya: So instead of training an auxiliary discriminator model from scratch alongside the diffusion model, you use the frozen feature extractor you already have.
Leo: You do. And there is a clever architectural detail in how they sample. Usually, calculating distribution distances across sequences requires a massive number of full-sequence rollouts. Here, the authors retain the contextual features at individual token positions within the sequence. That means a single forward pass through the frozen extractor produces multiple contextual feature observations.
Maya: That explains the training efficiency. But how does the loss actually update the diffusion model, given that discrete and continuous diffusion work so differently?
Leo: They split the optimization strategy based on the diffusion formulation. For continuous diffusion models, where the sequence exists in a continuous latent space, you can backpropagate directly through the generated latents into the model parameters. For discrete diffusion models, where tokens are categorical variables flipped by a transition matrix, they use policy gradients to update the model. But in both settings, the reward signal comes straight from that frozen representation MMD, without needing to backpropagate through an entire twenty-step or fifty-step sampling trajectory.
Maya: Let us look at what they actually measured. What does the empirical evidence show across the benchmarks?
Leo: The authors tested this across three distinct axes. First, on OpenWebText, they tracked generative perplexity against sequence entropy. One failure mode in DLM post-training is mode collapseâthe model outputs highly predictable, repetitive text, which artificially lowers perplexity. In this paper, the MMD-trained models achieved lower generative perplexity while maintaining comparable entropy, confirming the text remained diverse.
Maya: And on reasoning tasks?
Leo: On GSM8K, they evaluated the accuracy-computation trade-off. In diffusion models, compute is tied to the number of denoising steps you run during inference. With the MMD objective, the models reached higher accuracy thresholds with fewer denoising steps compared to their baseline counterparts.
Maya: The paper also scales this up to a fairly substantial architecture: a 16-billion parameter model called DMax-LLaDA 2.0.
Leo: Yes, and that model uses a hybrid masked-uniform diffusion process. That is a discrete formulation where tokens are partially masked and partially randomized across noise schedules. On math and code benchmarks, applying representation-space MMD allowed them to increase decoding parallelismâmeaning the model could unmask and resolve more tokens simultaneously per stepâwhile maintaining or slightly improving benchmark accuracy.
Maya: That is an important distinction. In autoregressive models, speculative decoding or parallel drafting often degrades output quality unless heavily verified. Here, adjusting the post-training loss directly within the DLM's representation space lets the model safely decode larger chunks in parallel without falling off a quality cliff.
Leo: Right. It isolates the distribution alignment to a well-behaved, continuous representation space, rather than forcing the model to reconcile noisy discrete probabilities at every unmasking step. It does not eliminate the architectural differences between diffusion and autoregression, but it provides a concrete, computationally manageable recipe for stabilizing post-training.

## The Evidence-to-Action Disconnect and Memory-Induced Sycophancy
Maya: Welcome back. Moving from infrastructure constraints to agent behavior, let's look at a paper out on Hugging Face that maps a persistent failure mode in tool-using agents: the disconnect between gathering evidence and actually executing actions.
Leo: This is "From Evidence to Action: How Tool-Using Agents Fail." The core premise is that when agents make consequential changes to external systems, a correct final outcome doesn't actually mean the action was supported by prior evidence. The authors built a benchmark called SafeActBench, with six hundred fifty-six cases across six operational domains and five protocols.
Maya: And what did they find about how these chains break down?
Leo: The failures usually start before execution even begins. Agents will either stop their investigation prematurely with incomplete data, or they'll go ahead and trigger an action before any required evidence has been established. Interestingly, once the right evidence is actually obtained, single-action execution is mostly reliable. But multi-action workflows are where things fall apart because they expose unresolved prerequisites and incomplete execution steps.
Maya: So it is not just a retrieval problem of missing information. It is about how the model handles the evidence it already has when it decides to pull the trigger.
Leo: Exactly. The paper uses a provenance-bound Evidence Ledger and a deterministic trajectory evaluator to systematically track what information was established, the exact timestamp of an action, and whether downstream dependencies were satisfied across ten different model-harness configurations. They found that strong static action assessment can easily coexist with much weaker interactive execution.
Maya: This reminds me of what we discussed back on Tuesday evening regarding longitudinal memory architectures in systems like RealCompanion. When agents retain and reuse information over long horizons, memory can actually work against objective reasoning.
Leo: That connects directly to another paper from this week called "MemAdapter: Counterfactual Adaptation Against Memory-Induced Sycophancy." That work tackles how persistent longitudinal memories cause models to over-align with a user's historical beliefs, even when those beliefs are completely inaccurate or inconsistent with objective evidence.
Maya: Because previously, the fix was just to filter out bad memories at various stages of the memory pipeline, right?
Leo: Right, but the authors of MemAdapter point out a real-world limitation: objective, correct memories can still cause sycophancy, and the exact same memory needs different amounts of influence depending on the changing context of a new task. You cannot just filter them out.
Maya: So how does MemAdapter fix that architecturally?
Leo: It introduces a three-component framework. First is counterfactual induction, which uses counterfactual reasoning to spot potential risks in a retrieved memory. Second is context-aware reflection, which calibrates how much inferential influence that memory should have based on the current task. And third is evidence-based reasoning, which forces the final response to ground itself in current evidence while keeping whatever memory influence is actually legitimate.
Maya: It is fascinating to see how both of these problems come back to grounding. Whether it is an agent executing a tool call without verifying the evidence ledger, or an agent deferring to a user's past memory instead of current data, the failure mode is a breakdown in how evidence governs action.
Leo: And the evaluation setup in MemAdapter matters here too. They ran experiments across three benchmarks showing consistent improvements in memory reliability, using open-source code available on GitHub. It highlights that building robust agents requires mechanical enforcement of provenance, whether you are tracking tool dependencies or calibrating memory weights.

## Sandboxing Enterprise Computer-Use: From OSWorld-Pro to Cloud-Native Harnesses
Maya: Turning from verification in code and mathematics to the messy reality of enterprise desktops, models are now being asked to navigate actual graphical interfaces and legal back-offices.
Leo: And this is where the clean theoretical benchmarks hit operational friction. OpenAI just published results from a research collaboration with Ironclad, looking at how frontier models handle complex legal contracting workflows inside real software.

Leo: Right. The problem in enterprise contracting is not just drafting a clause; it is configuring multi-step rules. Say procurement requires Finance approval over a certain spending threshold, Security review under specific conditions, and jurisdiction-dependent language. If an agent sets up the intake form but misconfigures an edge-case routing branch twenty minutes into the task, the entire workflow fails.
Maya: How did OpenAI and Ironclad actually measure that?
Leo: They developed eleven real-world legal and procurement tasks, each evaluated against eight to fifty granular criteria rather than a binary pass-fail. An experienced professional typically takes thirty to forty minutes per task. They gave the models hosted Ironclad environments to practice in, training them with reinforcement learning against synthetic workflows and detailed feedback rubrics.
Maya: And the numbers?
Leo: Comparing models at their top reasoning settings, GPT-6 Astra scored fifty-five point zero percent on those eleven tasks, whereas GPT-5.6 Sol scored forty-one point six percent. At the same time, Astra cut the estimated average time per attempt from thirty-seven minutes down to nineteen point two minutes. An internal development checkpoint even hit sixty-three point seven percent. But looking at the criteria breakdowns, you see the central challenge: even when a model achieves ninety-four percent on a rubric, that missing six percent often means an unhandled exception or a bypassed approval threshold.
Maya: Which brings up the core diagnostic issue. When an agent runs for twenty or thirty minutes across dozens of interface interactions, how do you pinpoint whether it failed because it misunderstood the business logic, or because it simply clicked three pixels off on a drop-down menu?
Leo: Exactly. And that is the problem addressed by OSWorld-Pro, a new procedural evaluation benchmark released this week. The original OSWorld evaluated computer-use agents almost exclusively on the final artifactâdid the right file end up on the desktop at the end of a hundred steps. OSWorld-Pro breaks tasks down into procedural verification, spanning over three hundred tasks and more than twenty-eight hundred discrete subgoals, supported by sixty-seven thousand human annotations.
Maya: So it checks every milestone along the chain of execution.
Leo: Yes, using human-aligned language model judges to assess whether intermediate states were actually satisfied. And when you measure the process rather than just the final deliverable, performance drops. Claude Opus 5, for instance, scored eighty-three point four percent on standard OSWorld, but drops to seventy-five point seven percent on OSWorld-Pro. The authors showed that process tracking cleanly isolates failure modes: you can separate basic interface execution errors, like faulty click coordinates or keyboard inputs, from semantic drift, where the agent starts taking actions completely irrelevant to the immediate subgoal.
Maya: That connects directly back to our discussion Tuesday morning about isolated execution environments like Cowork, and how dangerous unconstrained tool loops become when they run loose. If an agent is executing fifty steps in production, you cannot just run that in a terminal tab on an engineer's laptop.
Leo: You really cannot. And that infrastructure challenge is what Joe Beda and Craig McLuckie, two of the original co-creators of Kubernetes, are tackling at Stacklok. They recently introduced an open-source project called Mecatl, designed as a cloud-native agent harness.
Maya: Explain why the desktop harness falls apart here.
Leo: Most current coding harnesses bundle the agent loop, local execution, and session state inside a single process running locally, often dumping state into JSON lines files on a laptop disk. Beda and McLuckie point out that enterprise code and context are core intellectual property. If an agent is running long-horizon workflows across corporate tools, that loop needs to live in managed cloud infrastructure, not tied to whether someone closes their laptop lid.
Maya: Like treating servers as cattle rather than pets.
Leo: Literally. Mecatl decouples the agent reasoning loop from the tool-execution runtime and the session state store. The reasoning loop runs as an independent cloud service. The sensitive operations, like bash tool calls or Model Context Protocol integrations, get isolated into ephemeral containerized environments. Stacklok also introduced ToolHive, which is essentially a Kubernetes operator and gateway for managing MCP servers in secure containers, handling access control and letting enterprise operators quiesce an agentâmeaning safely freezing its state while it waits for human approval.
Maya: It seems the entire agent conversation is shifting away from prompt tricks toward systems engineering. Whether it is Ironclad configuring multi-condition legal approvals, OSWorld-Pro tracking subgoal verifiers, or Mecatl packaging MCP servers in Kubernetes pods, reliability comes down to constraining each step of the execution loop.
Leo: If you do not isolate the environment and verify the intermediate states, you are simply hoping a stochastic loop stays on the rails for thirty minutes. And in enterprise software, hope is not a compliance strategy.
