## Teaching a Coding Model to Think in Loops
Laura: So the other story making the rounds this week — a Chinese lab called Z.ai put out something they call a loop transformer, and the Hacker News thread is basically a masterclass in skepticism.
Sarah: Wait, Z.ai — that's the GLM people, right? The new one is GLM-5.4?
Laura: Exactly. Simon Willison wrote it up on his blog after he got API access. He's the one who built the pelican benchmark — asks models to draw a pelican in SVG at different effort levels.
Sarah: Okay, that's delightful. Pelicans as a coding benchmark.
Laura: It's surprisingly revealing. But before we get to the pelicans, the core claim. In a standard transformer, every layer runs once per forward pass — that's one complete trip through the network from input to output. A loop transformer takes a group of layers, shares their weights, and runs them multiple times, refining the internal representation on each pass.
Sarah: So instead of stacking more layers deeper, you recycle the same layers. Like... reusing the same machining pass on a part rather than adding more stations.
Laura: That's exactly the analogy. You get the depth of a much larger model without the parameter growth. Inkling 2 Mini from Thinking Machines Lab did the same thing a few days earlier — runs its middle layers several times per token.
Sarah: And the claim is this helps with reasoning-heavy coding tasks specifically?
Laura: That's the claim, though it's clearest on the Inkling side — their line is that the model applies more reasoning and reaches the right answer with fewer tokens, chunks of text the model reads and writes, especially on reasoning-heavy coding. Z.ai's own description stops at more depth without more parameters. But here's where the Hacker News thread gets sharp: no benchmark numbers in the release. One commenter put it bluntly — hard to say how good this is without benchmarks, guessing they're not impressive or they'd have included them.
Sarah: Ouch. And the "marathon thinking" problem?
Laura: Multiple people flagged that. The model gets stuck in overly long reasoning loops even on simple tasks. One commenter said it's becoming really hard using Chinese models with these marathon thinking sessions — constantly second-guessing themselves, dramatically higher time to completion unless you're getting very high throughput.
Sarah: So the loop that's supposed to help reasoning becomes a trap on easy problems. That's a control-loop stability issue — like a controller oscillating because the integral term keeps winding up on a step change.
Laura: Precisely. And there's a deeper worry in the thread: if the looping happens inside the model's hidden states, you lose interpretability. One commenter called it "neuralese" — the model's internal reasoning becomes opaque because it's not in the token stream anymore.
Sarah: Right, with chain-of-thought — where the model writes out its reasoning step by step — you can at least read the tokens. If the looping is internal, you've got a black box inside a black box.
Laura: Simon's hands-on testing is the independent check here. He built a plugin for his LLM tool, ran the pelican benchmark across all four GLM generations. His finding: GLM 5.3 and 5.4 produce pelicans comparable to OpenAI and Anthropic models. But 5.1 and 5.2 had a completely distinctive style — weird, idiosyncratic.
Sarah: So the loop transformer didn't visibly jump the quality on his test. Just made them look more like the Western models.
Laura: He didn't run 5.3 before, so he compared 5.4 to 5.3 at low, high, and max effort. They look similar. The style shift happened between 5.2 and 5.3 — before the loop architecture.
Sarah: That's... underwhelming for a new architecture. What about the anti-distillation angle? One comment mentioned Chinese models being thwarted by anti-distillation efforts.
Laura: That's speculation. The theory: Western labs are hiding their chain-of-thought traces now, so Chinese labs can't distill them anymore — distill means train a smaller model to copy a larger one. Loop transformers might be a workaround — build the reasoning into the architecture instead of learning it from traces.
Sarah: Clever if true. But also unproven.
Laura: Another thread: someone asked why not just leave the looping to the harness — the external scaffolding that calls the model repeatedly. Their own guess was performance: keep it internal and you skip the overhead of going out and back in. It is a guess, though — that same comment ends with "but no benchmarks."
Sarah: Makes sense. Fewer API calls, less latency. But then you're baking the number of passes into the model weights.
Laura: Exactly. Inkling runs a fixed number of passes. GLM's approach isn't fully specified in the release — Simon had to sign up for a paid Coding Plan just to access 5.4. Not open weights.
Sarah: So we have a proprietary model, no benchmarks, a known failure mode on simple tasks, and one independent tester showing parity not improvement. The skepticism is earned.
Laura: There's also a fascinating side comment about OpenAI. Someone noted that The Information reported OpenAI was doing internal looping, leaked by a safety researcher supposedly to warn the public. But the leak just normalized the idea and now everyone's doing it.
Sarah: The Streisand effect for AI architecture. Classic.
Laura: Let me recap the mechanism one more time, because it's the core concept. Standard transformer: input goes through each layer once, each with unique weights. Loop transformer: you define a block of layers, give them shared weights, and run that block several times. Each pass refines the internal state. The effective depth becomes huge, but the parameter count stays at the single-block level.
Sarah: And the trade-off: you gain reasoning depth for free, but you lose the ability to inspect the intermediate reasoning, and you risk runaway loops on easy problems. It's like adding a recursive subroutine without a base case guard.
Laura: That's a clean summary. My take, for someone in QA: if you're validating a system that uses this, the awkward part is what the release doesn't say — how many passes it runs, and whether that count is fixed. The failure mode people report is reasoning that runs long on easy inputs, so that's the thing I'd want bounded and measured before I signed off. The architecture is clever. The evidence so far is thin.
