Laura: Think of it like a CNC machine. PyTorch is a machining cell where the tool offsets drift between runs, the coolant pressure fluctuates, and the controller rewrites its own G-code on the fly. You can't validate that. You can't put it in a design-history file.
Sarah: Okay, that analogy lands. So llama.cpp is a hardened controller?
Laura: Closer to a fixed-cycle lathe. Pure C and C-plus-plus. No interpreter, no garbage collector, no hidden heap allocations after startup. The model weights are memory-mapped — mmap — straight from disk into the process address space. Zero copy, zero fragmentation.
Sarah: Memory-mapped. That's the operating-system call that lets a file on disk look like it's already in RAM, right?
Laura: Exactly. The kernel pages in only the chunks the CPU actually touches. For a seven-billion-parameter model at four-bit quantization, that's roughly four gigabytes on disk. You don't need four gigs of free RAM; you need the working set. It's like loading a massive CAD assembly — only the visible parts hit the graphics card.
Sarah: And quantization — that's compressing the floating-point weights into integers?
Laura: Four-bit, eight-bit integers. Instead of thirty-two-bit floating-point matrix multiplies, you do integer arithmetic with a per-block scale factor. Deterministic, faster on CPUs, and the math is bit-exact across architectures. No GPU floating-point reduction-order surprises.
Sarah: Which we talked about Wednesday — non-associativity in parallel reductions giving different results on different cards.
Laura: Precisely. Integer math is associative. Same input, same output, every time. That's what a design-history file needs: evidence that the computation is repeatable.
Sarah: The blog mentions fixed scratchpad memory pools allocated at initialization. That's the other half?
Laura: Yes. At startup, the runtime reserves a single contiguous buffer — the scratchpad — for all intermediate tensors: attention scores, hidden states, KV cache. No malloc during inference. No fragmentation. It's like a fixture plate: every workpiece clamps to the same holes, every time.
Sarah: So the whole inference graph — model weights mmapped, activations in a pre-allocated pool, integer kernels — runs without a single dynamic allocation after init.
Laura: That's the claim. And if it holds, you can write a validation protocol: known input, known output, hash the weights, hash the binary, done. Change control becomes: new model weights? New hash. New llama.cpp minor version? Regression test the hash. Major version? Re-validate.

## How a Mothers Vaccine Shielded Danish Babies From Severe Illness
Sarah: I was reading the Nature briefing this morning — the one that opens with an epidemiologist telling her colleague the data must be broken because there are barely any babies in hospital with RSV.
Laura: Wait, really? That's the kind of "something's wrong with the data" moment you live for.
Sarah: Exactly. Denmark rolled out maternal RSV vaccination nationwide on October first, twenty twenty-five.
Laura: Seventy-two percent of eligible pregnant women took it.
Sarah: That winter, infant admissions for RSV dropped from an average of about eleven hundred to just under three hundred.
Laura: A seventy-five percent reduction.
Sarah: Okay, that's wild. That uptake number — that's not a pilot study. That's a national program.
Laura: And the Reddit thread on the science subreddit lit up. Parents posting that their newborns sailed through winter while older siblings got wrecked.
Sarah: A pediatrician in the US saying they normally send five to ten babies a year to the ER in respiratory distress — last two years, zero.
Laura: Pharmacists laying out the exact decision tree: due date October through January, mom gets Abrysvo between thirty-two and thirty-six weeks; due date February through September, baby gets nirsevimab in October.
Sarah: Abrysvo, nirsevimab — let's pause. Abrysvo is the maternal vaccine, Pfizer's bivalent prefusion F vaccine.
Laura: Nirsevimab is the long-acting monoclonal antibody given directly to the infant. Beyfortus is the brand name.
Sarah: The thread has that classic mix — grateful parents, a few saying they paid two hundred seventy euros in Estonia because only one hospital offered it, and the inevitable "my kid got reactive asthma after RSV at age two, still on a nebulizer."
Laura: That last one hits different. RSV isn't just a bad cold for infants. It can remodel airways.
Sarah: Which brings us to the mechanism — and this is where the engineering analogy writes itself. The vaccine presents the viral fusion protein locked in its prefusion conformation.
Laura: That's the shape the protein has before it snaps open to fuse with a human cell. Think of it like a spring-loaded latch held in the cocked position.
Sarah: So the immune system sees the "armed" shape, not the "fired" shape. It makes IgG antibodies against that specific conformation.
Laura: IgG — that's the main antibody type in blood, the only class that crosses the placenta in significant amounts.
Sarah: And it doesn't just diffuse across. Specialized proteins called neonatal Fc receptors, or FcRn, act like cellular pumps that actively move IgG from maternal circulation into fetal circulation.
Laura: It's an active transport system, not passive leakage. Active transport — like a pump curve. You need the receptor density, the binding affinity, the gestational timing.
Sarah: Third trimester is when FcRn expression ramps up and maternal IgG transfer accelerates exponentially.
Laura: Pre-charging a hydraulic accumulator before factory deployment. The baby arrives with a pressure vessel already full of neutralizing antibodies. No lag time for the infant's own immune system to spin up.
Sarah: And the accumulator drains following first-order half-life kinetics. IgG half-life in infants is about three to four weeks.
Laura: So protection wanes predictably. Six months out, you're down to roughly three to six percent of starting titer.
Sarah: That's why the Danish protocol pairs maternal vaccination with nirsevimab for high-risk infants whose mothers weren't vaccinated — preterm babies, mostly.
Laura: Nirsevimab has an engineered Fc region that extends its half-life to about seventy days. One shot covers the whole RSV season.
Sarah: Let's check the claims against the source. The Nature piece cites Klint Johannesen and colleagues, Lancet Regional Health Europe, twenty twenty-six. That's the primary registry study.
Laura: Independent check: Nature News published the summary on twelve November twenty twenty-six. The registry paper is the source for the seventy-two percent uptake and seventy-five percent admission drop.
Sarah: Claim one: seventy-two percent uptake among eligible pregnant women. The paper reports seventy-two percent coverage in the target population. That's registry data — every pregnancy in the national health system. Not a survey.
Laura: Claim two: admissions fell from an average of one thousand one hundred twenty-eight to two hundred ninety-six. The paper uses the pre-pandemic five-year average as baseline. Twenty ninety-six is the observed count for the twenty twenty-five twenty twenty-six season. That's a seventy-four point seven percent reduction — they round to seventy-five.
Sarah: Claim three: real-world effectiveness aligns with or exceeds pre-approval trial efficacy. The phase three MATISSE trial reported about eighty-two percent efficacy against severe medically attended RSV in the first ninety days, dropping to sixty-nine percent through six months.
Laura: The Danish registry shows a seventy-five percent reduction in hospitalizations across the whole season — broader endpoint, real-world population, no exclusion criteria.
Sarah: So the verdict: the uptake number is solid registry data. The admission drop is solid registry data with a reasonable baseline.

Laura: And the cost trade-off nobody talks about: maternal vaccine is one dose, given in a prenatal visit you're already doing. Cold chain is standard two to eight Celsius.

Laura: Manufacturing side: prefusion F stabilization is a protein engineering win. They introduced two proline substitutions and removed the furin cleavage site — locks the trimer in prefusion.
Sarah: That's structure-based design, not empirical screening. And the FcRn engineering on nirsevimab — they made three amino acid changes in the antibody's tail region.
Laura: These changes increase binding to FcRn inside acidic cellular compartments called endosomes, but not at neutral pH in the bloodstream. That's the half-life extension. Pure protein engineering.
