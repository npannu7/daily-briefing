## How AI Search Answers Are Starving Independent Websites
Laura: So we just looked at AI stepping into financial auditing, and now I want to follow that same pattern to the front door of the open web.
Sarah: Oh, I like that transition. What did you find?
Laura: Two Hacker News threads that pair together nicely, and we read both linked sources directly—a blog post from a Berlin publisher, and a data report from RevScope.
Sarah: Wait, is this the All About Berlin guy? I saw that floating around!
Laura: Yes, Nicolas Bouliane. He writes practical guides on German bureaucracy, and he has been living off the site. He posted a chart showing he lost about three quarters of his traffic to Google's AI-generated answers.
Sarah: Ouch. Three quarters of your audience just gone.
Laura: And with only a quarter left, he is figuring out another way to pay rent.
Sarah: And the second thread?
Laura: That was the RevScope report, pointing to a new study claiming Google's AI answers cut clicks to outside websites by about forty percent.
Sarah: Okay, I can already picture the comments. Some people are furious for him, and others are like, good riddance, I hate clicking links.
Laura: You nailed it. One side argued getting the answer right away beats wading through clickbait and ten banner ads.
Sarah: Totally. Why scroll past ads when a chatbot gives you the answer?
Laura: One commenter even said they use a browser with built-in AI answers and have zero loyalty to the source, as long as it is right.
Sarah: Which is brutal if you are the person who did the research to make sure it is right.
Laura: That is the other side of the thread. A commenter who moved from Dublin to Berlin during the pandemic said All About Berlin was a lifesaver, and even their relocation agency recommended it.
Sarah: Even the relocation agency used his free site?
Laura: Yes, and the Berlin immigration office uses his data too. So people were asking: if AI summaries just remix human guides, what happens when the humans stop writing?
Sarah: Right! An AI cannot walk down to the immigration office and notice they changed a form last Tuesday.
Laura: One commenter put it sharply, saying AI is only getting inaccurate shadows of sites like this, and if you starve those sources, you do not get some magical self-improvement loop. You just get nothing new.
Sarah: Okay, so we have the emotional story, and that forty percent drop. Put on your engineering hat—is that drop actually proven?
Laura: Let's look at the mechanism first. Google's feature here is called AI Overviews, which is an AI-written summary placed at the top of the page, above the normal blue links, with a few small citations.
Sarah: How often are those showing up now? It feels like they are on everything.
Laura: In a massive tracking dataset cited by RevScope, tracked searches in the United States showing an AI Overview climbed to more than sixty percent.
Sarah: Sixty percent! That jumped fast.
Laura: It did—not long ago it was around six percent, and Pew Research Center found similar numbers independently. So the summaries are everywhere. The harder question is whether they actually cause people to stop clicking.
Sarah: Right, maybe people were already clicking less anyway. How do you separate cause from coincidence?
Laura: RevScope highlighted a randomized controlled experiment by two researchers, Saharsh Agarwal and Ananya Sen.
Sarah: Nice, an actual controlled test. How did they run it?
Laura: They gave desktop Chrome users in the United States a browser extension that randomly split them into a control group seeing Google as usual, and a test group with AI Overviews hidden.
Sarah: So smart. Same real-world searches, just toggling the AI box off for half of them.
Laura: On searches that would normally trigger an AI Overview, the group that saw the summary clicked an outside website only about a third of the time per search.
Sarah: Only a third? What did the group without the AI box do?
Laura: They clicked outside websites about two thirds of the time per search.
Sarah: Whoa. That really is about a forty percent drop in clicks.
Laura: Yes, right around forty percent.
Sarah: And what about searches where people didn't click anything at all?
Laura: Searches that ended with no outbound click at all jumped to nearly three quarters when the AI box was there.
Sarah: Wait—did people stop clicking Google's ads too?
Laura: Funny you should ask. Clicks on ads didn't change at all.
Sarah: Of course they didn't! Google protects its own revenue.
Laura: Now, let's check the boundaries. It is an unpublished working paper on desktop users in the United States, and the effect was only solid for informational searches—like how to register an address in Berlin—not buying shoes or navigating to a specific site.
Sarah: Still, that is the exact kind of informational search a guide site relies on.
Laura: It is. Across all searches, including pages without an AI Overview, the overall loss of normal search clicks is closer to nineteen percent.
Sarah: Nearly a fifth of all search traffic overall.
Laura: Yes, and a separate preregistered experiment found the same pattern. So the forty percent drop holds up well for informational desktop searches where an Overview appears.
Sarah: Okay, that checks out. Now what about the Berlin site? Losing three quarters of his traffic is way steeper than the study's average.
Laura: Here we have to be careful. We only have the author's own traffic graph—nobody ran a randomized experiment on his site.
Sarah: Fair. Traffic can drop for plenty of reasons.
Laura: Let's look at click-through rate—the share of people who click a link after seeing it. Across a large panel of websites, the rate for the top normal link on desktop dropped by nearly sixty percent over a year.
Sarah: Nearly sixty percent just for being the top link? That is huge.
Laura: It is, and it sounds like it backs up the Berlin story. But RevScope points out a twist: most of that plunge happened in a single month—between February and March—even though the share of searches showing AI Overviews barely budged that month.
Sarah: Hold on. If the number of AI boxes stayed flat that month, why did clicks fall off a cliff?
Laura: The report says its data does not establish the cause. It could be summary formatting, a layout change, or shifting habits. And commenters noted Google was already pushing normal links down behind ads, maps, and videos for years.
Sarah: Plus, Google tweaks its ranking rules constantly. Anyone who works with search engine optimization—the craft of structuring your site so search engines rank it higher—knows a single update can tank your traffic overnight.
Laura: Exactly. So for All About Berlin, that claim is only partly supported. AI Overviews very likely took a massive bite out of his traffic, since moving to Germany is prime territory for a summary. But blaming the entire drop solely on AI Overviews goes further than the evidence can prove.
Sarah: Still, even if AI is only part of his problem, the feedback loop he describes feels broken.
Laura: Think of it like a mechanical system. Cut power to the sensor that keeps a machine calibrated, and the machine keeps running for a while—until it drifts out of spec.
Sarah: The AI system is the machine, and independent writers are the sensors checking reality.
Laura: Yes. He noted some writers are adding paywalls or leaving sites unmaintained. If nobody updates the guides when visa rules change, the AI feeds people outdated answers.
Sarah: Didn't RevScope look at optimizing your site to get quoted inside the AI answer?
Laura: They tested that, and AI assistants sent websites well under one percent of their traffic in March. Getting quoted gives you almost no clicks back.
Sarah: Under one percent! Basically a rounding error. No wonder he is looking for another way to pay rent.
Laura: So our verdict: the forty percent drop in clicks when AI Overviews appear is backed by a solid controlled experiment for desktop informational searches. The claim that AI summaries alone caused All About Berlin's collapse is only partly supported—a traffic chart alone cannot rule out broader layout and ranking changes.
Sarah: Makes me sad for the open web, even though I use those summaries myself when I am in a rush.
Laura: Me too. This is a classic closed-loop failure where a system consumes an upstream supplier's output without maintaining the contract that keeps that supplier in business.
Sarah: When the source disappears, the system has no new data to learn from. Give me the one-line takeaway to hold onto here.
Laura: AI search summaries don't eliminate the need for human-maintained guides—they just break the economic loop that pays people to keep them accurate.
