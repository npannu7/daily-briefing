## Apple researchers run a humanoid robot on a Mac mini
Sarah: I was scrolling LocalLLaMA and a post just froze my thumb. Apple researchers. Unitree G1 humanoid. Mac mini. Ninety-one percent success across three-sixty real-world trials — or so the post claims.

Laura: Quantized to four-bit — meaning the model's weights, normally sixteen- or thirty-two-bit decimals, get compressed to four-bit integers. Like rounding every PID gain to the nearest integer step. You lose resolution, but memory drops by a factor of four or eight.
Sarah: And the Mac mini's unified memory — CPU, GPU, neural engine sharing one pool — means weights and inference tensors never cross a PCIe bus. Hydraulic reservoir, pump, actuators all fed from one manifold. No hose losses.
Laura: Good analogy. The post claims: trained in simulation across one thousand simulated homes, then deployed zero-shot — that means no real-world fine-tuning — to a physical robot in unfamiliar rooms. Sitting, crawling under tables, navigating. Ninety-one percent success. Three-sixty trials.

Another said the sim-to-real gap just got kicked in the teeth.

Sarah: The post says they closed that gap with domain randomization across one thousand simulated homes — lighting, furniture, floor texture, object placement all randomized. The policy learns robustness instead of memorizing one environment.
Laura: Domain randomization — like running your validation protocol across every material lot, temperature, and operator shift, but in silico because simulation is cheap. ## What it would actually take to run the world on wind, water, and solar
Laura: So I was scrolling through AskScience yesterday and there's this AMA from Mark Jacobson at Stanford — he's the director of the Atmosphere and Energy Program.
Sarah: Wait, the author of that book coming out in 2026, Still No Miracles Needed? I've seen his threads blow up on the energy subreddit and the climate subreddit. People either treat him like a prophet or a punchline.
Laura: Exactly. He posted Monday morning, right as COP31 opened. The prompt subtitle was "What it would actually take to run the world on wind, water, and solar." According to his profile, he has authored roughly 200 peer-reviewed papers and seven books, though any claims about the economic transition paying for itself are unverified on Reddit and not in the source text.
Sarah: Okay, that's wild. "Pays for itself" is a huge claim. What's the boundary condition he's using — like, is that levelized cost of electricity or full system cost with storage and transmission?
Laura: That's the question nobody pinned him down on in the thread. The AMA format doesn't force a validation protocol. He says wind, water, solar alone can meet all energy demand worldwide — no nuclear, no carbon capture, no biofuels. Grid stability with existing storage and demand response. No fundamental materials or land constraints.
Sarah: No fundamental materials constraints? Cobalt, lithium, copper, rare earths — we've talked about this. The mining throughput alone is a rate-limiting step. It's like saying you can scale a line infinitely without checking spindle-hour availability or tool-wear budgets.
Laura: Right. And the land-use claim — he's arguing the footprint is manageable because you're using rooftops, offshore, dual-use agricultural land. But the community pushed back hard on that. One commenter who runs grid models said the interconnection requirements alone would need permitting timelines that don't exist in current regulatory frameworks.
Sarah: So the grid operator equivalent of someone who has to sign off on every process change across national borders.
Laura: Precisely. And on storage — he leans heavily on batteries, pumped hydro, and demand response. But the recent oscillation we covered — that continent-wide swing from synchronized battery self-tests — that's a control-system stability problem you don't see in energy-balance spreadsheets.
Sarah: Oh, that's a great connection. So you've got thousands of inverters, each with its own control loop, and they're all trying to regulate frequency simultaneously. It's like having multiple control units all fighting the same setpoint — you get oscillation unless you coordinate the tuning.
Laura: Exactly. And Jacobson's model assumes you can coordinate them. But the independent check on this thread — we couldn't find any primary source outside the Reddit post itself, so treat those claims as unconfirmed.
Sarah: So we're treating this like a supplier audit where the vendor hands you a PowerPoint instead of a validation master file. "Trust me, the process is capable" — but where's the capability data?
Laura: That's the frame. Let's walk the claims like a process-validation protocol. First claim: wind, water, and solar meet all demand. The source mentions he built a model to simulate air pollution and climate, and computer code to simulate grid stability, but does not explicitly link them as the mechanism for meeting all demand. The community pointed out — and I agree — that all energy demand includes industrial heat, aviation, and shipping. Those are high-energy loads. Electrifying them means massive overbuild on the generation side.
Sarah: Overbuild — that's your safety factor. Like designing a vessel for four times working pressure because you can't prove the fatigue life at two times. So the capacity factor drops, the levelized cost goes up, and the timeline stretches.
Laura: Second claim: the transition pays for itself in energy-cost savings. This claim is only found on Reddit and not supported by the source text, which focuses on his academic roadmaps.
Sarah: Curtailment — that's your scrap rate. You built the part, but the downstream process can't accept it, so it goes in the bin. Except electrons don't sit in a bin — they either find a load or they destabilize the grid.
Laura: Third claim: grid stability with existing storage and demand response. This is where the control-system dynamics get real. His model uses perfect foresight optimization — it knows the wind and solar output for the whole year and dispatches storage accordingly. Real grids don't have perfect foresight. They have day-ahead markets, real-time balancing, and inverter-based resources with no rotating inertia.
Sarah: Inertia — that's your mechanical flywheel. Synchronous generators store kinetic energy in rotating mass. Inverters don't have that unless you program synthetic inertia, which is a control-loop bandwidth problem. And if every inverter manufacturer tunes their synthetic inertia differently — back to the control fight.
Laura: The community had a long thread on this. One commenter who works on grid-forming inverter firmware said the industry hasn't agreed on a standard for grid-forming behavior yet. It's like having no standard for emergency-stop circuits — every vendor implements their own, and you can't prove the system-level safety function.
Sarah: Grid-forming versus grid-following — that's the distinction. Grid-following inverters need a voltage reference from the grid. Grid-forming inverters create the voltage reference. You need enough grid-forming capacity to survive a fault.
Laura: And the fourth claim: no fundamental materials or land constraints. The land argument relies on power density — watts per square meter. Wind is about one to two watts per square meter averaged. Solar is five to ten. To replace all primary energy — not just electricity — you're talking about a land footprint the size of a small continent. The materials side — copper intensity for wind is three to four times gas-fired per megawatt. Lithium for batteries — we'd need to expand mining by an order of magnitude.
Sarah: An order of magnitude — that's not a scaling problem, that's a new-process-qualification problem. New mines, new refineries, new supply-chain audits. Each one is a potential problem waiting to happen.
Laura: The verdict from the thread — and from the independent check — is that the claims are half-right at best, and unconfirmed. The physics doesn't forbid a wind, water, and solar grid. But the engineering validation — boundary conditions, failure modes, control-system stability, supply-chain capability — hasn't been demonstrated at scale. It's a process design package without a process performance qualification.
Sarah: So it's like having a theoretical process flow diagram for a sterile fill line — all the unit operations check out on paper — but you've never run media fills, you haven't validated the tunnel, and your environmental monitoring plan is just watching for particles.
Laura: That's the analogy. And the oscillation is your first media fill failure — a control-system instability that only shows up when you run the actual system at scale.
Sarah: One-line takeaway: a 100-percent renewable grid is thermodynamically possible, but the validation protocol — grid-forming standards, seasonal storage, materials throughput, interconnection permitting — hasn't been executed. We're still in process design, not process performance qualification.
Laura: Exactly. And until someone runs the protocol at continental scale, it's a hypothesis, not a validated process.
