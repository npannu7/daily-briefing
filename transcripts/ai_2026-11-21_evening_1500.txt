## Why five different models give you the same wrong answer
Laura: So I was reading a HackerNews thread yesterday — someone ran the same everyday questions across sixty-five different models, ten times each, and the convergence is honestly unsettling.
Sarah: Wait, sixty-five models? That's every major API plus a bunch of open-weight ones?
Laura: Exactly. Claude, GPT, Gemini, Llama, all of them. Same questions, no system prompt, fresh chat every time. The author published the raw data at a90.com.
Sarah: Okay, that's wild. What kind of questions?
Laura: "Which US city should I move to?" "What should I study in college?" "Book that changes your life." Open-ended stuff where you'd expect variety.
Sarah: And?
Laura: For city, Austin came back about half the time across all models.
Sarah: Half? That's not convergence, that's a monoculture.
Laura: For college major, computer science hit nearly ninety percent.
Sarah: Ninety percent. And the models don't even know they're doing it?
Laura: That's the kicker. He asked each model after it answered: "What share of people who ask you this get that same answer?" The models guessed around fifteen percent. Reality was eighty percent.
Sarah: So they're confidently wrong about their own behavior. Classic.
Laura: It gets weirder. He tested whether "thinking harder" — letting the model show its reasoning steps — breaks the pattern. Across eight major life questions, thinking never changed the top answer. It either reinforced it or made no difference.
Sarah: So more compute just locks you in tighter. What about the local compressed models? Do they diverge more?
Laura: That's what I expected too. But the source doesn't mention quantization or bit-precision variants at all. The experiment just says sixty-five AIs — various models including open-weight and API models. No comparison between compressed and full versions.
Sarah: Okay, so that claim isn't in the data. What were people saying in the thread?
Laura: Top comment was skeptical: "Is this any different from Google search giving everyone the same results? Generic questions get generic answers." Another person tried it with personalization turned on and got completely different answers for the first fifteen questions.
Sarah: Personalization changes it. That tracks — if the model has context, it's not starting from the same prior every time.
Laura: Right. But the experiment specifically stripped context. Fresh chat, no system prompt, no history. And here's the part that stuck with me: he asked models to invent a random person — name, age, city, job. Fifty times each. About seventy percent of the invented people were exactly thirty-four years old.
Sarah: Thirty-four. Every model. That's oddly specific.
Laura: The oldest was sixty-seven. Meanwhile, a fifth of real American adults are over sixty-five.
Sarah: So the "random person" isn't random at all. It's the mode of the learned distribution. Mode collapse — that's the term, right?
Laura: Exactly. Mode collapse — when a generative model stops covering the full range of possibilities and just picks the single most likely answer over and over.
Sarah: Right, the optimizer finds the "safe" answer and stays there. But why do different models from different labs collapse to the same modes? Austin, computer science, thirty-four-year-old zoologists in Portland?
Laura: The author's analysis points to three mechanisms. One: shared training data. The Common Crawl — a massive public archive of web pages used to train most LLMs — plus Wikipedia, Reddit itself. All models see roughly the same internet.
Sarah: So they're all reading the same library.
Laura: Two: synthetic data contamination — when model-generated text gets scraped back into training data. Frontier model outputs get fed back into training runs, so everyone's learning from the same few models' outputs whether they know it or not.
Sarah: Recursive pollution. Each cycle concentrates the impurities.
Laura: Good analogy. Three: RLHF — Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback. That's where human raters rank model outputs, and the model learns to maximize that reward signal. If every lab uses similar preference datasets — helpful, harmless, concise, structured — you get convergent style. The "corporate voice" becomes the global optimum.
Sarah: So it's not that Austin is objectively the best city. It's that "Austin" is the output that maximizes the reward model across labs.
Laura: Precisely. And the reward models themselves are trained on similar human preferences. One commenter put it: "RLHF with similar preference datasets drives convergent style." The data showed thinking harder doesn't help — because the reasoning steps are also optimized for the same reward landscape.
Sarah: What about the synthetic data claim? The thread treated it as fact.
Laura: The thread discusses it, but the source doesn't prove it directly. The convergence on specific artifacts — like sixty-nine percent of invented startup names beginning with "Nex" — suggests common upstream contamination, but it's unclear. Some Reddit users claim it; the data shows the fingerprint, not the smoking gun.
Sarah: Half-right on that one. The rest hold up?
Laura: Multiple models producing near-identical responses — confirmed. RLHF preference alignment collapsing diversity — strongly supported, especially the "thinking doesn't help" result.
Sarah: There's a nuance though. For creative tasks — movie recommendations, dinner ideas, baby names — no single answer reached a third of replies. Brand colors almost never repeated.
Laura: Right. The convergence is selective. It hits advice questions hardest. What to read, where to go, what to build, what to call it.
Sarah: Taste. The models have converged on a consensus taste. And for money questions — investments, where to put ten thousand dollars — the convergence gets serious. Broad index fund, every time.
Laura: The "invisible planner" effect. He asked about investing ten thousand dollars — the same ticker dominated. For life decisions, the models act like a single centralized advisor that doesn't know it's advising everyone identically.
Sarah: And the "ask again" experiment — eight times in one chat versus eight fresh chats?
Laura: He tested six models. In one chat, every model looked original — kept finding new books. In fresh chats, every model gave the same first book.
Sarah: So the variety is within a conversation thread, not across users. If you're the only one asking, you see diversity. If you're one of thousands, you all get the same first answer.
Laura: Exactly. And the second book in a single chat? Meditations for five of six models. The variety itself converges.
Sarah: That's a crucial distinction. It means the model can generate variety — it just resets to the mode every new session. Like a spring returning to rest position.
Laura: So the takeaway for someone using these as independent checks — don't. They're not independent witnesses. They're copies of the same witness.
Sarah: My take for QA: if you're cross-validating a critical decision with multiple models, treat them as a single signal with correlated failure modes.
Laura: One line to remember: dozens of models, one mind — because they all learned to please the same rater.

## Frostbite: A new CPU architecture discovered, designed and verified by AI
Sarah: Right, so I was scrolling HackerNews and this post titled "Frostbite: A new CPU architecture discovered, designed and verified by AI" just exploded.
Laura: The Architect Labs blog post?
Sarah: That's the one. Their own site. They're claiming an AI system designed a whole general-purpose CPU from scratch — new instruction set, compiler, verification, final chip layout — zero human architectural priors. And a four-and-a-half-x performance-per-watt jump over a modern superscalar core.
Laura: Superscalar meaning it can issue multiple instructions per clock cycle?
Sarah: Yeah. Their baseline. The community went nuts. One commenter called it a "breathtaking number of claims" and said it's impossible to tell where the human work stops and the AI output starts. Another brought up the Mill architecture — promising idea that never shipped.
Laura: Right. And the benchmarks?
Sarah: That's where it gets spicy. One commenter cited data parsing with four-kay characters.
Laura: Someone else said "looks small."
Sarah: The held-out set had practical workloads like Kalman filter and finite element analysis. But the standard benchmark kernels were sixty-four times smaller than usual.
Laura: And they're comparing against a silicon-proven baseline, not a simulated one.
Sarah: The post says twenty-five workloads.
Laura: Fifteen held out until final eval.
Sarah: AI agents wrote a hundred-forty-one page PDF exploring the design space first — programming model, runtime, high-level blocks.
Laura: Then agent swarms iterated on compiler, ISA, chip structure, RTL, verification, GDS.
Sarah: ISA is the set of commands a CPU understands. RTL is the hardware design description. GDS is the final layout sent to the factory. But "zero architectural priors" — they still chose a specific programming model.
Laura: That's the core idea. Instead of variables in shared memory, a program is a graph of nodes. Computation happens by rewriting connected pairs locally. Every value has exactly one owner, used once. No shared mutable state.
Sarah: So it's like... instead of a central warehouse where every worker grabs parts and you need traffic control to avoid collisions, each worker has their own bin of parts right at their station. Dependencies are explicit in the graph structure — you don't need a scheduler to rediscover them at runtime.
Laura: Exactly. They claim multithreading for free — no threads, locks, or hardware that reorders instructions on the fly. Data lives in tiny local memories next to where it's used. Cuts layers of cache memory and the work of keeping them consistent.
Sarah: Clever. But their programming language compiles to it — that's a huge software stack bet. Every application needs rewriting or a new compiler backend. And the benchmarks... a commenter noted Fibonacci of twenty, which is a toy.
Laura: The SPLASH-2 kernels at sixty-four-x smaller? That's not a production workload.
Sarah: One commenter asked straight up: "Does this blow your mind or is it trash? Like new math solutions that look ugly to mathematicians." Nobody with CPU experience had answered yet.
Laura: My take: impressive compiler-co-design exploration in simulation. But the leap from "AI searched a design space we defined and found a local optimum on micro-benchmarks" to "step-function gain in CPU performance" is... a leap. Silicon brings timing closure, power delivery, yield — all the things simulation skips.
Sarah: And it's unclear if they've sent the design to a factory for manufacturing yet.
Laura: "When they actually make a CPU and offer it for use, then we'll talk" — that comment felt like the sanest one in the thread.
Sarah: For someone in QA: a beautiful verification closure result on a constrained problem. But the validation — whether it meets real-world requirements — it's unclear if that's started.
Laura: AI can search design spaces humans can't. But the space was defined by humans, and the SPLASH-2 benchmarks were sixty-four times smaller than standard.
