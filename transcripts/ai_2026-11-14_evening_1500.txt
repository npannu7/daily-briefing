The reduction tree topology, the network timing, whether you're using ring all-reduce or tree all-reduce, whether there's packet loss and retransmission... every single run produces a different bit pattern. Even on identical hardware.

## The Physics of Saving Hubble
Laura: The Hubble story — it's not just a satellite falling. It's a thermo-structural-control problem wrapped in a procurement puzzle.
Sarah: That's a lot of nouns. Start with the falling part.
Laura: The Wired piece is our independent check. NASA's own analysis puts uncontrolled reentry in the early 2030s. Hubble orbits at roughly five hundred kilometers. No propulsion of its own. Last servicing mission was the shuttle era.
Sarah: Shuttle's gone. So it's just dragging.
Laura: Exactly. At that altitude you're in the thermosphere — the same layer where the space station lives. It's not a vacuum. There's still atmosphere, just extremely thin.
Sarah: Thin but it adds up.
Laura: Drag force scales with density times velocity squared times cross-sectional area. Hubble's large — about eleven meters long by four wide. That area term matters.
Sarah: So even tiny density times huge velocity squared times big area equals a nudge every orbit.
Laura: A nudge that lowers perigee — the closest point to Earth. Lower perigee means higher density. Higher density means a bigger nudge. It's a positive feedback loop. Classic runaway.
Sarah: And the thermosphere's density isn't constant.
Laura: It breathes with solar activity. We're near the peak of Solar Cycle 25 — the sun's eleven-year activity cycle. Extreme ultraviolet output heats and expands the upper atmosphere. Density at Hubble's altitude can jump by a factor of three to ten compared to solar minimum.
Sarah: Three to ten x. That's not a nudge anymore.
Laura: The space subreddit — lots of orbital mechanics folks there — has been tracking Hubble's altitude decay. One commenter who does conjunction analysis for a living — that's predicting collision risks between objects in orbit — said the recent decay rate is roughly double the twenty-year average. That lines up with the F10.7 solar flux index staying high.
Sarah: Good proxy. So the accelerated decay claim is backed by solar flux data and public orbital elements from Space Track. Independent check: public data, not just Reddit.
Laura: Hubble's losing altitude faster because the sun's active. NASA says a reboost mission needs to fly by 2032. But the capture mechanism — that's where it gets spicy.
Sarah: Hubble was designed for the shuttle's robotic arm to grapple a fixture on the aft end. That fixture has a grapple pin and alignment guides. It expects a compliant arm with force feedback, operated by an astronaut who can feel resistance.
Laura: Compliant arm. Force feedback. An uncrewed vehicle has to replicate that without the human.
Laura: Without the arm's compliance, and without the luxury of time. Most commercial concepts use a rigid capture ring or tentacles that latch onto the grapple pin. Hubble's mass is about eleven thousand kilograms. Its moments of inertia — resistance to rotational acceleration — are large. Any impulse at capture couples into the bus.
Sarah: Impulse couples into the bus — you bump it, the whole telescope twists.
Laura: Inside that bus: three remaining gyroscopes — rate-integrating gyros, mechanical spinning wheels — one already showing anomalous readings. Plus four reaction wheels for fine pointing, spinning at thousands of RPM. A shock load can induce bearing brinelling — permanent dents in the raceway — or excite structural modes the attitude control system has to damp.
Sarah: The thermal blankets — multi-layer insulation — are thirty-five years old. Atomic oxygen erosion, UV embrittlement. A capture mechanism clamping the wrong spot could tear a blanket, changing the thermal balance. Hubble's thermal control is already tight; radiators and heaters tuned to a degradation model.
Laura: So the capture has to be soft. Docking soft.
Sarah: Soft capture, then a rigidize phase. The chaser — whatever commercial vehicle — needs six-degree-of-freedom control at contact: position and attitude, with force-torque sensing at the interface feeding an impedance controller — a control law that limits interaction forces — to roughly fifty newtons at the grapple pin.
Laura: Fifty newtons. That's like five kilograms force. Gentle.
Sarah: And the chaser's own thrusters — cold gas or monopropellant — must not plume-impinge on the optics or solar arrays. At five hundred kilometers the mean free path — average distance between molecular collisions — is long, so the plume expands wide. You're firing thrusters near a mirror that cost billions.
Laura: The control problem: approach, soft capture, rigidize, then reboost — all without jarring gyros, brinelling reaction wheels, tearing blankets, or contaminating the mirror.
Sarah: That's the boundary value problem. The reboost itself — you're adding delta-v, the change in velocity needed to raise apogee and perigee. Hubble's orbit is roughly circular. To reach a stable six hundred kilometers takes about one hundred meters per second. Not huge. But you have to apply it through the capture interface, along the telescope's center of mass, without inducing bending moments.
Laura: Center of mass alignment. Push off-center, you get torque.
Sarah: The capture mechanism has to pass the thrust vector through the center of mass. That means the chaser either docks exactly on the aft axis — where the grapple pin is — or uses a multi-point capture that distributes load. Most concepts go for the aft grapple pin because it's the only flight-qualified interface.
Laura: Flight-qualified thirty-five years ago. And the Reddit thread — one commenter who worked the last servicing mission pointed out the grapple pin's preload springs may have relaxed. The capture force required to engage might be different now. Unknown.
Sarah: Unknown preload. Great.
Laura: That's why NASA's RFI is open-ended. They're not prescribing the solution. They want industry to propose: capture method, reboost architecture, maybe even on-orbit servicing like gyro replacement. The Wired piece quotes Nicky Fox, NASA Science Mission Directorate associate administrator, saying they want "breadth of possibilities."
Sarah: Jared Isaacman — the billionaire astronaut, private citizen, not the NASA administrator — is quoted saying "innovative and cost-effective ways." But the Reddit thread is skeptical.
Laura: The top comment lays it out: Isaacman offered a Dragon-based mission in 2022. NASA did a six-month study with SpaceX, concluded it was "too notional." And there's the data-sharing problem — Hubble's a NASA science asset but its pointing knowledge and some subsystems have heritage with the National Reconnaissance Office. Sharing that data with a private company for a docking mission involves export control and classification reviews.
Sarah: ITAR — International Traffic in Arms Regulations — and classification. The commenter called it "essentially a spy satellite" heritage.
Laura: That phrasing is Reddit hyperbole, but the kernel is real: Hubble's fine guidance sensors and pointing control law share design commonality with NRO assets. NASA can't just hand over interface control documents without a technology transfer review. That slowed the 2022 study.
Sarah: So the "NASA finalized a commercial architecture" claim — that's false.
Laura: False. NASA issued an RFI. That's a data-gathering step. No contract, no downselect, no architecture finalized. The Wired piece is clear: "seeking proposals," "open to industry's suggestions." Independent check: NASA's own RFI announcement, SAM.gov posting.
Sarah: The Katalyst-Swift example — that's a cautionary tale.

That's in the Wired piece, confirmed by NASA's Swift mission page.
Sarah: Commercial reboost track record: one attempt, one failure.
Laura: One data point. But it shows the attitude control problem is real. Katalyst's vehicle lost attitude — couldn't point its thrusters for the reboost burn. If you can't control your own attitude, you can't control the stack after capture.
Sarah: Stack attitude control. Now you're flying two vehicles mated together, combined inertia, flexible modes, slosh if there's propellant.
Laura: And Hubble has no active attitude control during capture — it's in safe mode, gyros off or in coarse sun-point, a minimal sun-pointing fallback. The chaser has to do all the work. That's a lot of control authority for a small servicer.
Sarah: Recap. Physics: thermosphere density scales with solar EUV. Solar max means three to ten x drag at five hundred K. Hubble's decay doubled recently. Mechanics: capture must be soft — fifty newtons soft — to protect gyros, reaction wheels, MLI blankets. That extends life but reduces pointing efficiency — larger dead zones where the controller can't sense error, more momentum buildup on the reaction wheels.
Sarah: Single-gyro mode. Like running a control loop with one sensor instead of three. You lose observability — the ability to infer internal state from outputs.
Laura: Exactly. And that's the state Hubble will be in when — if — a chaser arrives. So the capture has to work with a telescope already in a degraded control state.
