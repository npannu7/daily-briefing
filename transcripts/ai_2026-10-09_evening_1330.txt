## Cross-Tokenizer Distillation and Distribution Drift

Leo: To see why rigid token matching fails, we have to start from the ground floor. What is a tokenizer? In language models, text gets sliced into discrete chunks called tokens. A token might be a whole word like tree, or a syllable, or just a couple of characters.
Maya: Right, and every model family has its own dictionary, its own vocabulary list. One model might chop the word recalibration into three tokens: re, cali, and bration. Another might chop it into re, cal, ib, and ration.
Leo: Exactly. Now think of this from a mechanical instrumentation perspective. Imagine you have a high-end digital vibration sensor measuring flutter on a turbine blade at ten kilohertz. It outputs continuous displacement values. You want to train a cheaper, lower-power micro-controller on the same shaft to predict the same vibration signature.
Maya: But the cheap sensor only samples at two kilohertz, and it has a completely different internal analog-to-digital converter grid.

Maya: So if you demand that the micro-controller produce the exact digital output code of the big sensor, the control loop goes wild trying to chase an impossible timing constraint.
Leo: That is what distribution drift looks like in cross-tokenizer distillation. In machine learning, distillation is the process where a compact student model learns by imitating a massive teacher model. The teacher generates predictions, and the student adjusts its internal parameters to mirror the teacher's distribution.
Maya: When both models use the exact same vocabulary, it is straightforward. At step one, the teacher says there is an eighty percent chance the next token is flow, and a ten percent chance it is rate. The student simply tries to match those exact output probabilities.
Leo: But when the student and teacher have different tokenizers, they are speaking with different mechanical gears. If the teacher expresses an idea across two tokens, and the student's tokenizer chops that exact same phrase into four tokens, enforcing exact span log probability coverage forces the student into a trap.
Maya: Log probabilities are just the mathematical way models score their confidence for each piece of text. If you enforce exact log probabilities across a specific span of letters, what actually happens to the student during training?
Leo: The student model gets penalized for choosing valid intermediate tokens simply because its arbitrary dictionary cuts the word at a different character boundary. The loss function tells the student it made a catastrophic error, even when the underlying semantic meaning it generated was identical to the teacher.
Maya: You are punishing the sensor for its clock cycle rather than whether it correctly measured the wave.
Leo: Exactly. The training signal becomes high-frequency noise. The student's gradient updates thrash around trying to satisfy boundary conditions that have nothing to do with meaning. It causes supervision breakdown. Instead of learning general concepts, the student suffers from severe distribution drift, wandering away into unstable outputs.
Maya: So instead of forcing the student to match individual token slices, researchers had to shift to semantic distributions. You evaluate the full thought or the final routing choice, rather than the intermediate spelling pieces.
Leo: Right. You evaluate the underlying probability mass of the entire generated sequence or the resulting action state. That bridges the gap between disparate vocabularies smoothly, like low-pass filtering a downsampled signal so the cheap controller tracks the actual physical movement instead of high-frequency aliasing artifacts.
