A lot of folks assume distillation just means someone copies text outputs and feeds them into standard supervised training. But frontier distillation is much more surgical than scraping raw chat logs.
Laura: Think of it like precision metallurgy. If an aerospace foundry produces a proprietary high-strength turbine alloy, a competitor cannot just buy one ingot, melt it down, and expect to match the exact crystalline grain structure. The competitor needs to know the thermal profile, the cooling gradient, and the minute trace elements. In language models, when a smaller student model distills from a frontier teacher, it is not just learning the final answer. It is trying to match the probability distribution of the teacher across tokens.
Sarah: Right. The teacher produces logits, which are unnormalized log probabilities over the entire dictionary of possible next tokens. In pure distillation, the student minimizes the Kullback-Leibler divergence between its own probability distribution and the teacher model distribution. It learns that word A had an eighty percent chance, word B had a fifteen percent chance, and word C had five percent. That spread gives the student the reasoning landscape, not just a single binary answer.
Laura: But via an ordinary cloud interface, frontier labs do not expose those raw probabilities. You only get the sampled text.
Sarah: Yes, but distillers get around that. First, they harvest reasoning traces, the scratchpad thoughts where the model talks to itself before giving an answer. Anthropic found that training a student on summarized reasoning dropped SWE-bench Verified coding scores from eighty point four percent down to seventy-one point eight percent. Full internal reasoning is pure gold for students.
Laura: That makes total sense from an optimization standpoint. The scratchpad exposes intermediate state variables. If you only see the final displacement of a mechanical linkage, you have to guess the internal force vectors. If you get the full kinematic trace, fitting the governing equations is straightforward.
Sarah: But when distillers push harder with fraudulent accounts to force verbatim reasoning traces, labs deploy output watermarking and deliberate logit perturbation. That is the core defensive mechanism.
Laura: Walk through the perturbation, because that is where the physical engineering analogy really clicks.
Sarah: Imagine you are an industrial manufacturer producing medical-grade polymer resin, and you want to prevent unlicensed repackaging. You add a microscopic taggant, like a specific inert tracer isotope, at parts-per-billion concentrations. To the end customer molding a catheter or a syringe, the tensile strength, viscosity, and biocompatibility are completely unchanged. The part is within design tolerance.
Laura: But under a spectrometer, that taggant lights up like a beacon.
Sarah: Exactly. In model serving, output watermarking subtly shifts the logits before sampling. The server might partition the vocabulary pseudo-randomly into a green list and a red list based on a hash of the preceding tokens. Then it adds a tiny bias to the green list tokens. For an ordinary human reading the text, the sentence reads naturally. It is grammatically perfect, functionally accurate, and preserves the semantic intent.

## Air-Gapped Optical Links: High-Speed Screen-to-Camera Data Diodes
Laura: Picking up on Nikhil's note from yesterday morning about independent developers building tangible projects with these tools, I ran across a fascinating thread in the Claude Code community.
Sarah: Oh, I saw you bookmarking this. Someone claimed they built an optical air-gap data link hitting thirty-two megabits per second.
Laura: Thirty-two megabits per second using just a computer monitor and a camera. The developer called the project Lizard, and the goal was to transfer files across physically separated machines without any physical connection or radio frequency signals.
Sarah: Which is classic data diode territory. In cybersecurity, and honestly in medical hardware telemetry, an air gap means you physically isolate a secure network from untrusted networks. No Ethernet, no Wi-Fi, no Bluetooth. Traditionally, if you wanted to bridge that gap without plugging in a thumb drive, you flashed QR codes on a monitor and pointed a webcam at it. But anyone who has ever tried transferring a video file over QR codes knows it is agonizingly slow.
Laura: It usually crawls at a few dozen kilobytes per second. So when this developer posted that they were clocking roughly four megabytes per second, the thread lit up. Predictably, half the comments immediately jumped to sci-fi exfiltration horror stories. People started quoting that animated show Pantheon where an artificial intelligence escapes an air gap using phone vibration motors, or claiming frontier models will soon broadcast their own weights across office security cameras.
Sarah: Reddit loves an escape-from-the-lab fantasy.
Laura: Every single time. But tucked underneath the existential panic, a few commenters asked the real engineering question: what does the frame synchronization look like, and how are you proving zero packet corruption at that speed?
Sarah: Because that is the whole ballgame. To understand why this developer got so much attention, you have to look at why old projects like Decimen hit a ceiling. Decimen relied on standard QR codes. And QR codes were fundamentally designed for printed paper in the nineteen-nineties.
Laura: Paper is an unforgiving medium. You have ink bleed, physical warping, low resolution, and poor contrast under bad lighting. To survive being printed on a cardboard box, a QR code has to use binary black-and-white blocks with massive spatial margins.
Sarah: Exactly. It is like designing a mechanical stamping die with loose tolerances because your sheet metal varies in thickness. But a liquid crystal display does not have ink bleed. A monitor has precisely controlled pixel grids, high refresh rates, and hundreds of levels of luminance. The developer realized that forcing a computer monitor to display binary black-and-white stamps is an absurd waste of channel capacity.
Laura: Nikhil will recognize the physical counterpart immediately: it is an optocoupler. In safety-critical medical instrumentation, you often need galvanic isolation. You want to pass sensor readings from patient-connected electrodes into high-voltage processing circuits without a conductive copper path, preventing stray electrical currents from shocking the patient. You do that with an optical diode, pulsing light across an insulating gap to a photodetector.
Sarah: Only here, the screen is the light emitter and the phone camera is the receiver. Instead of binary QR tiles, this developer borrowed ideas from a twenty-sixteen academic paper out of ETH Zurich called Focus. That paper introduced optical orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing for screen-to-camera links.
Laura: Let us unpack that acronym without the computer science haze. Orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing is simply splitting your data across multiple distinct frequencies that do not interfere with each other. Think of it like a multi-spoke drive shaft, or sending several distinct musical tones down an acoustic pipe simultaneously. On a screen, instead of just black or white, you modulate patterns of varying brightness across different spatial frequencies.
Sarah: The developer took that concept, ditched the bulky square finder patterns from standard QR codes, and designed a custom concentric ring. That ring packs around fifteen bits of orientation and metadata, protected by Reed-Solomon error correction.
Laura: Reed-Solomon is the same algebraic error correction used in compact discs and aerospace communications. It adds calculated parity symbols to your data payload. If dust scratches the disc, or in this case if the camera lens catches a reflection, the decoder solves a system of polynomial equations to mathematically reconstruct the missing bits without requesting a retransmission.
Sarah: And here is the clever part: by keeping that metadata ring down to fifteen bits, there are only about thirty-two thousand possible codewords. A graphics card can decode that using pure brute force in parallel across thousands of shader cores in microseconds.
Laura: That brings us to where Claude Code entered the workflow, because the post claimed the model assisted in writing this pipeline. But the developer was refreshingly blunt about where the language model hit a wall. Claude initially tried to port the computer vision finder algorithm straight into Vulkan compute shaders using traditional branching logic.
Sarah: And as every graphics programmer knows, conditional branching on a GPU is brutal. In hardware, graphics processors execute instructions in lockstep across groups of threads called warps.
Laura: It is just like a multi-spindle manufacturing drill head where thirty-two drill bits descend simultaneously. If thirty-one drills hit soft wood but one drill encounters a knot and has to switch to a slow drilling cycle, the entire machine has to pause. The other thirty-one spindles sit idle waiting for that single branch to finish. That is warp divergence. The human developer had to step in, override the model, and force a completely flat, non-branching brute-force check that fit the hardware architecture.
Sarah: So Claude was not autonomously inventing new communication theory here. It was acting as a high-speed code generator for boilerplate math, but the engineer had to guide it away from architectural dead ends. The developer also ran into platform walls. In a standard web browser, camera processing stuttered, oscillating wildly between zero and one megabyte per second because of frame desynchronization.
Laura: To get real throughput, you have to bypass web wrappers and write native camera drivers on Android to lock the exposure, gain, and frame timing. That brings us to our independent check. Did this developer actually sustain over thirty-two megabits per second across an air gap?
Sarah: On air, we have to say: right now, this claim lives purely on Reddit and a personal open-source code repository.
Laura: Exactly. There is a linked demonstration video, but no independent research lab or verified testing harness has replicated those transfer speeds. We do not have third-party telemetry on the sustained bit error rate, the required physical distance, or how ambient lighting changes throughput.
Sarah: So for our verdict: the underlying physics and signal processing are completely legitimate. Building an optical data diode using spatial luminance modulation and parallel GPU decoding is grounded in established literature. But the headline claim of an easy, robust thirty-two megabits per second in the wild is unconfirmed until someone outside that thread benchmarks it under controlled conditions.
Laura: It is a brilliant weekend prototype, but not yet an industrial standard. The takeaway for your commute: an artificial intelligence can help you assemble the signal processing math, but squeezing bandwidth out of real-world hardware still requires an engineer who understands where the physical bottlenecks live.
