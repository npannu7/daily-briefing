## Why your breath feels cold through pursed lips but warm with an open mouth
Laura: So the thread I brought today comes from askscience — someone asking why breath feels cold through pursed lips but warm with an open mouth.
Sarah: Oh, I've seen this one. People love this question. It shows up every few months.
Laura: Right. And the top comments are all over the place. One says it's just entrainment — fast air grabs cooler room air. Another says we don't feel temperature, we feel heat transfer rate. A third brings in Bernoulli and viscosity. And someone jokes about trying it in a sauna for the opposite effect.
Sarah: Wait, the sauna thing — that's actually a good test. If the air itself were getting colder from expansion, it should still feel cold in a sauna. But if it's about entrainment, the grabbed air is hotter than you, so it'd feel warm.
Laura: Exactly. That commenter intuited the control experiment. But here's the thing — the independent check came up empty. No textbook, no paper, no reliable source outside Reddit. So we're treating every claim here as unconfirmed community reasoning.
Sarah: Okay, so we build it from first principles. Let's start with the restriction. Pursed lips — that's a throttle valve. A narrow opening that chokes the flow.
Laura: Precisely. You've got a pressure difference. Lungs at maybe one or two kilopascals above atmosphere — that's a few percent of atmospheric pressure. Lips form a small orifice. Flow accelerates through the restriction. That's your expansion nozzle.
Sarah: And adiabatic expansion — no heat exchange with the walls because it's fast. The gas does work pushing out, so internal energy drops. Temperature should fall.
Laura: For an ideal gas, yes. But air isn't ideal at room temperature. The Joule-Thomson coefficient tells you whether a real gas cools or warms when it expands at constant enthalpy — constant total heat content. For nitrogen and oxygen at body temperature, the coefficient is positive — so yes, a slight cooling. But we're talking fractions of a degree.
Sarah: Fractions of a degree. That's not what you feel on your hand held out at arm's length.
Laura: Right. So the adiabatic cooling through the lip aperture is real but tiny. The dominant effect has to be downstream. The jet exits the lips at high velocity — turbulent and chaotic. It's a free jet now, and turbulent jets entrain surrounding fluid.
Sarah: Entrainment — the jet's shear layer pulls in ambient air. Viscous diffusion, pressure fluctuations, the whole turbulent mixing cascade. So the breath plume becomes a mixture: your thirty-seven-degree air plus room air at maybe twenty-two degrees.
Laura: And the mixing ratio depends on velocity. Higher exit velocity — pursed lips — means more entrainment per unit distance. The plume cools faster. Open mouth, low velocity, less entrainment. The plume stays closer to body temperature.
Sarah: So it's not the expansion cooling. It's the entrainment cooling. The restriction creates the high velocity that drives the entrainment.
Laura: That's the mechanism chain. Restriction → high velocity → turbulent jet → entrainment → mixing with cooler ambient air → lower plume temperature at the skin.
Sarah: And the skin part — one commenter nailed this. We don't have temperature receptors. We have heat-flux receptors. Thermoreceptors fire based on the rate of heat leaving or entering the skin.
Laura: Exactly. Faster air strips the thermal boundary layer — that thin layer of still air hugging your skin — more aggressively. Drives higher heat flux. Your skin cools faster. You perceive "cold."
Sarah: Open mouth — lower velocity, lower heat transfer, lower heat flux. Skin stays warmer. You perceive "warm." Even if the air temperature were identical.
Laura: And the sauna comment? In a sauna, ambient air is hotter than skin. Entrainment brings in hotter air. The plume temperature at the skin is higher than body temperature. Heat flows into the skin. You perceive "hot." Same mechanism, reversed temperature difference.
Sarah: That's clean. Restriction sets velocity. Velocity sets entrainment and heat transfer. Ambient temperature sets the driving potential. Perception follows heat flux.
Laura: Now — one commenter mentioned Bernoulli and viscosity "grabbing" ambient air. Bernoulli gives the pressure drop in the accelerating jet. The pressure field around the jet is what pulls surrounding fluid toward the low-pressure core. Viscosity — the fluid's internal friction — is what transmits that pull into the ambient fluid. So they're not wrong, but "grabbing" is a metaphor for the pressure-gradient-driven entrainment.
Sarah: And the transition from jet to plume — that's the Reynolds number story. High Reynolds number, turbulent jet, entrainment scales with velocity. Low Reynolds number, laminar or transitional, entrainment drops off sharply. There's a regime change around a few thousand.
Laura: In mechanical terms, it's like the difference between a cutting torch nozzle and a gentle purge flow. The torch nozzle — small orifice, high velocity, massive entrainment, intense mixing. The purge flow — large orifice, low velocity, the gas just sits there, barely mixes.
Sarah: Or a heat exchanger. High velocity on the tube side gives you a high film coefficient — that's the heat transfer rate per degree of temperature difference. Low velocity gives you a low film coefficient. Same fluid, same wall temperature, totally different heat transfer rate.
Laura: Exactly. And that's why the perception flips. It's not the gas temperature changing much inside the lungs or even at the lips. It's the downstream fluid mechanics controlling what actually reaches the skin and how fast it steals heat.
Sarah: So the Reddit thread — the entrainment comment was closest. The heat-transfer comment was also right but incomplete without the velocity-entrainment link. The Bernoulli-viscosity comment had the right physics vocabulary but didn't connect it to the perception mechanism.
Laura: And nobody mentioned the Joule-Thomson coefficient or the ideal-gas deviation. Which matters if you're designing a cryogenic expander, but for breath at room temperature, it's negligible.
Sarah: Negligible. Like, millikelvin negligible — thousandths of a degree.
Laura: Millikelvin. So the independent check — we found no external source confirming or refuting any of this. It's a classic askscience thread: plausible physics reasoning, no citation, no experimental validation.
Sarah: Which is fine for a starting point. But if Nikhil wanted to verify this in his lab, he'd need a hot-wire anemometer — a tiny heated wire that measures flow speed from cooling — a fast thermocouple, maybe a thermal camera on a heated plate simulating skin.
Laura: Or a simple version — exhale through a straw versus a wide tube, measure plume temperature at fixed distance with a fine thermocouple. Control ambient temperature. Plot against exit velocity.
Sarah: That's a senior design project right there. Fluid mechanics, heat transfer, instrumentation.
Laura: And it connects to what we discussed Tuesday — why fluid mechanics lives in engineering now. This problem needs the full stack: compressible flow through an orifice, turbulent jet entrainment, convective heat transfer, sensor response. That's not reductionist physics. That's integrated continuum mechanics.
Sarah: Okay, quick recap. Pursed lips — restriction, high velocity, turbulent jet, strong entrainment of cooler room air, high heat transfer at skin, high heat flux out of skin, feels cold. Open mouth — low velocity, weak entrainment, plume stays near body temperature, low heat transfer, low heat flux, feels warm. The gas expansion itself cools the air only microscopically. The perception is heat flux, not temperature. Sauna reverses the sign because ambient is hotter than skin. All unconfirmed outside Reddit, but the physics chain is solid.
Laura: Solid. And every link in that chain — throttle valve, expansion nozzle, turbulent jet, entrainment, film coefficient, thermoreceptor response — shows up in mechanical systems from gas turbines to HVAC to medical device sterilization cycles.
Sarah: Which is why it's a great question. Looks simple. Opens the whole toolbox.
Laura: Exactly.
