## Splitting the Engine: Converting Dense Models into Sparse Mixtures-of-Experts
Laura: Moving from hardware interlocks to neural network plumbing, I was browsing the LocalLLaMA subreddit earlier and spotted someone trying an audacious home experiment on a consumer graphics card.
Sarah: Audacious on LocalLLaMA usually means someone tried to do something that frontier labs spend millions on, but on an eight-gigabyte card while their living room heater is turned off.
Laura: Spot on. A hobbyist tried to take existing dense models, specifically tiny ones like Qwen two point five point five B and SmolLM two, and slice them into sparse Mixture-of-Experts architectures without retraining from scratch.
Sarah: Wait, upcycling! Big labs do that, right? Instead of training an MoE from ground zero, you copy the dense feed-forward weights into several experts, add a gating router, and train just the routing. People online talk about it like it is a cheat code for free inference speed.
Laura: That is the promise everyone grabs onto. The poster wanted to see if a normal user could slice up a dense model at home, drop the compute cost per token down to around forty percent of the original, and keep the baseline intelligence intact.
Sarah: And for anyone who missed our chat last Saturday evening about tool changers, remember the basic difference here. In a dense model, every single token fires every parameter in the feed-forward network. It is like running a massive six-axis mill where every spindle cuts the air on every single pass, whether you need it or not.
Laura: Exactly. Whereas a Mixture-of-Experts, or MoE, acts like a multi-spindle CNC machine with an automatic tool selector. For each piece of material, or each token of text, a gating router picks only two or three specific cutter heads, called experts, to engage.
Sarah: Right. So in theory, you get the vast capacity of a giant machine, but the active spindle run-time and electrical load of a tiny hobby drill. So why not just take an existing dense cutter head, slice it into thirty-two micro-tools, and slap a router in front of it? Did it work?
Laura: The poster actually set up thirty-two experts, picked the top eight for each token, kept one shared expert active for everything, and tested the results. But the numbers tell an uncompromising story.
Sarah: Give me the damage.
Laura: On the Qwen point five billion model, perplexity on WikiText jumped from fourteen point six six on the dense baseline up to nineteen point two zero on the converted version.
Sarah: Oof. For Nikhil driving right now, remember perplexity is like measuring positional error or runout on a machining shaft. Lower is tighter and better. An increase from fourteen to nineteen means the model suddenly became far more uncertain about what words belong together.
Laura: Yes, its confidence scattered. And across standard common-sense and reasoning benchmarks, the mean normalized accuracy dropped from roughly forty-six point seven percent down to forty point eight percent. On SmolLM, the drop was even steeper, going from fifty-one point four percent down to forty-one point seven percent.
Sarah: That is a massive hit. You lose nearly ten points of raw accuracy just to cut the per-token feed-forward compute down by half. Why did the cutting fail so badly if the weights were already smart?
Laura: Think about how a dense layer organizes itself during hundreds of millions of training steps. It is a single, highly integrated coupled system. The mathematical vectors flowing through those intermediate layers do not naturally segregate themselves into tidy, isolated engineering disciplines.
Sarah: Ah. It is not like the dense layer naturally has one corner that only does French grammar and another corner that only calculates orbital mechanics. It is all smeared across the entire weight matrix.
Laura: Exactly. In structural design, you would call it a load-bearing shell. If you take a hydroformed monocoque car chassis and cut slits down the side so you can claim it is a flexible multi-link suspension, you have not built a sports car. You have just ruined the structural rigidity of the frame.
Sarah: That is a great visual. When you do naive post-hoc slicing or clustering on those weights without thousands of hours of supervised retraining, the gating network has no clean boundary to steer tokens toward.
Laura: And when the router cannot find distinct mathematical poles, you run into what the research calls routing collapse. The router either assigns almost every token to the same few experts, overloading them, or it sprays tokens arbitrarily across poorly partitioned slices that have lost their complementary context.
Sarah: The poster even admitted they had to leave the final layer, and in one model the third layer, as original dense blocks because converting them broke the model entirely. That is basically admitting the structural integrity collapsed at the boundaries.
Laura: Precisely. They kept those boundary layers dense to stop the entire stack from diverging into complete gibberish.
Sarah: What about the consumer hardware angle? Even if you swallow the accuracy loss, does converting a dense model into an MoE actually speed up inference on an eight-gigabyte desktop card?
Laura: That is the biggest misconception in these threads. MoE reduces floating-point operations per token, which helps server throughput when you batch thousands of requests together. But on a single consumer GPU, inference speed for one user is almost entirely bottlenecked by memory bandwidth, not raw math.
Sarah: Because you still have to store all thirty-two experts in your GPU RAM! Even if you only compute with eight of them, you had to pull parameters across the bus. If the total parameter count did not shrink, your memory transfers did not magically disappear.
Laura: Exactly. You are still hauling the entire steel assembly out of storage just to tap it with a small hammer.
Sarah: So where do we land on the original claim that you can convert dense models to sparse mixtures-of-experts at home with zero pretraining?
Laura: It is technically possible to perform the surgery and make the file load, but practically wrong if the goal is preserving intelligence or saving consumer memory bandwidth. Upcycling without massive retraining produces an uncalibrated, brittle machine.
Sarah: Slicing up a unified engine does not make it modular; it just leaves you holding thirty-two broken parts.
