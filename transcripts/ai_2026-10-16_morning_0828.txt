## Can Artificial Intelligence Audit Scientific Research?
Sarah: I was scrolling through the singularity subreddit and a post there just blew up. It claimed a model called GPT-6.1 Sol — described as a reasoning-optimized variant of the latest release — had audited nearly eight hundred mathematics papers from arXiv, which is a public preprint server where researchers share drafts before formal peer review. The headline numbers from that post: six percent major errors, thirty percent minor mistakes.
Laura: Okay, quality-engineer hat on. Six percent major defects on first inspection — that's not a pass rate, that's a recall campaign waiting to happen. And thirty percent minor? In medical-device land, we'd call those "observations" that still need root-cause analysis before you ship. But the post also says researchers estimate ninety percent of those major findings are correct — not that independent teams confirmed each one, just that the community's best guess is most are real.
Sarah: Right, the wording in the thread is "researchers estimate ninety percent of its major findings are correct." And the post notes that in newer preprints, the major-error rate has fallen to just one point five percent. The comments section immediately started debating why. One user said, "It makes sense error rates are shrinking as more researchers double-check with these models." Another called it "the ironic benefit nobody talks about — humans make a lot of errors and researchers are relying heavily on models to make sure their ideas are grounded."
Laura: That's the survivor-bias argument in disguise. If you only measure papers that passed an AI filter, you're measuring the filter's selectivity, not the underlying process capability. It's like saying your optical inspection station reduced surface defects to zero — but you scrapped every part that failed. The outgoing quality looks great; the yield might be terrible. And since we couldn't find this claim anywhere outside of that Reddit gallery post — no arXiv write-up, no blog from the verifying researchers, no public audit logs — we have to treat the cause as community speculation, not established fact.
Sarah: A skeptic in the comments pushed back: "Not discounting the findings, but isn't this recursive? Of course papers using AI to error-check have fewer errors for AI to find after the fact. This would be true for any reviewer." Another commenter replied: "If the AI checking isn't truly doing anything useful, there's no reason to believe it would ever converge on zero errors. If it's just a hallucinating parrot, every time you say 'review this paper,' it should always come back with some errors, relevant or not. It wasn't guaranteed that if you fix the errors and ask again fresh, it would say 'this paper is really good.'"
Laura: That's a falsifiability test. If the model is just generating plausible-sounding criticism — what we'd call a high false-positive rate in automated inspection — then re-running it on a corrected paper should still produce "defects." If it stops flagging issues after genuine fixes, that suggests it's tracking something real. But we don't know the re-test protocol. Was it the same conversation window? Fresh session? Did the author feed it the previous error list?
Sarah: The thread also had a physicist who said they fed their five grad-school publications to a rival model — Astra — and it "absolutely shredded them." Which tracks. I've seen mathematicians post threads where a model found a sign error in a lemma — that's a helper theorem — that propagated through three larger theorems. But the top comment on the whole post was: "The headline is misleading. Eight hundred papers submitted to arXiv — meaning these are pre-peer-review papers. Peer review is the part made to catch these errors. I'd be much more interested in post-review error rates."
Laura: That's the non-destructive testing analogy. arXiv is the incoming raw material inspection. Peer review is your final acceptance test. Finding six percent major defects at receiving inspection? That's expected. The question is: what's the escape rate to the customer — the published literature? If peer review catches ninety percent of those, your outgoing defect rate is point six percent. If the AI replaces peer review, you need its false-negative rate — the errors it misses — near zero. And we have zero evidence of that.
Sarah: Which brings us to the mechanism. How does a language model "audit" a math paper? It's not running a formal verifier like Lean or Coq — those are programming languages where every definition and proof step must be encoded in a strict logical framework called dependent type theory. The model is reading natural-language math: prose, notation, skipped steps, phrases like "it follows that" or "without loss of generality." It's trying to reconstruct the logical argument in its internal representation and check for local consistency. That's... ambitious.
Laura: It's basically automated stress analysis on a structure where the blueprints are written in shorthand and the load paths are implied. The model has to infer the missing reinforcement. And we know models can exploit ambiguous premises. If the paper says "let X be a compact manifold" but doesn't specify the topology, the model might assume the wrong one and "find" an error that isn't there. Or miss a real one because it filled in the gap incorrectly.
Sarah: In mechanical terms: you're doing finite-element analysis — a computer simulation of physical stress — on a mesh the model generated from a napkin sketch. The mesh density, the boundary conditions, the material model — all hallucinated from context. A "major error" flag could be a real stress concentration, or it could be the model putting a fixed support where the sketch implied a roller. The ninety-percent precision claim — if real — means the model's inferred mesh matches the author's intent nine times out of ten. But we don't know the denominator. How many real errors did it miss? That's the false-negative rate. In nondestructive testing, we care way more about misses than false alarms.
Laura: And the "minor errors" at thirty percent? In the comments, someone said: "It's very hard to write dozens of pages with no errors at all. You just hope it doesn't kill the paper, and most of the time it doesn't." That's notation sloppiness, off-by-one indexing, a missing "for all epsilon" — the mathematical equivalent of surface finish deviations. They don't affect structural integrity. But the model flags them with the same confidence as a broken theorem.
Sarah: That's your false-alarm rate in automated optical inspection. If thirty percent of boards get flagged for "minor solder variation" that's within spec, your operators start ignoring the alarm. Alarm fatigue. The model needs a severity classification — critical, major, minor, cosmetic — calibrated against actual impact. Does it have that? The Reddit post doesn't say.
Laura: There's also the compute trade-off. The post mentions GPT-6.1 Sol being four point six times slower than the non-reasoning variant. Auditing an eighty-page paper with chain-of-thought reasoning — that's where the model shows its step-by-step work — could take hours of compute per paper. At scale, you're not running this on every draft. You're running it on the final draft — which means the one-point-five-percent figure, even if real, measures the final defect rate after human-plus-AI iteration, not the AI's standalone catch rate.
Sarah: And that's the key. The process is: author writes draft → author runs AI check → author fixes flagged issues → repeat → submit. The observed defect rate at submission is a function of the loop, not the tool alone. If the tool has high precision but low recall — catches what it sees, misses subtle logic gaps — the loop still converges to fewer detectable errors. But the undetectable ones? Those ship. In medical devices, we call those "latent defects." They show up in the field.
Laura: So the verdict: the six-percent and thirty-percent numbers are plausible for arXiv preprints — that's just the nature of the beast. The ninety-percent precision on major errors? If the verification happened, that's genuinely impressive for a natural-language reasoner. But it's unconfirmed, single-source, no methodology disclosed. The one-point-five-percent drop? Almost certainly survivor bias plus pre-screening. The papers that would have had errors got fixed before submission. The ones that still have errors after AI checking? Those are the ones the model can't see.
Sarah: Half-right, with a side of "too early to tell." The tool is clearly useful as a pre-review lint — like a static analyzer, a program that checks code for bugs without running it, but for math prose. But it's not a proof checker. It's not a substitute for peer review. And the community's excitement is real but uncalibrated. They're measuring the wrong thing: outgoing defects on a self-selected population, not the tool's intrinsic capability.
Laura: One-line takeaway: an AI that reads your math paper like a tired reviewer at 2 AM is a great lint, but don't confuse the lint's clean bill of health with a structural proof — because the beams it can't see are the ones that hold the roof up.

## Do Weight-Loss Injections Curb Alcohol Cravings?
Sarah: So I was scrolling the science subreddit yesterday and there's this post about a JAMA Psychiatry paper — a national survey of U.S. adults — claiming nearly half the people on GLP-1 drugs like Ozempic or Wegovy say they're drinking less, and among heavier drinkers it's over half.
Laura: Wait, really? That's the thread where the top comment is someone saying "Ozempic literally changed my life, killed the alcoholism I wouldn't admit I had"?
Sarah: That's the one. Six months in, they felt smarter, better sleep, realized alcohol was the problem. Then the replies pile up — "my doctor would never take this seriously, she'd just say CBT and exercise," someone else links a Boston University article about diabetes drugs curbing addictions broadly.
Laura: And the anecdotes keep coming — four months for seventy-five bucks a month, alcohol use dropped, marijuana use dropped. Someone jokes alcohol companies are about to start lobbying to ban these drugs.
Sarah: Right. So the community's excited. The post frames it as the drugs acting on the brain's craving and reward pathways directly. But here's the thing — I couldn't actually read the JAMA paper. The link returns a four-oh-three forbidden error.
Laura: So we have a Reddit post describing a study we can't verify, and a comment section full of personal stories.
Sarah: Exactly. The independent check turned up nothing outside that thread. No press release, no preprint, no other outlet reporting the numbers. So every claim — the participant count, the overall percentage, the heavy-drinker percentage — is unconfirmed. It only lives on Reddit.
Laura: Okay, but let's say the survey exists. It's still a survey. Self-reported. People knowing they're on a "weight-loss shot" and then estimating how much they drink. That's not a randomized controlled trial — a study where people are randomly assigned to get the real drug or a placebo, and neither they nor the researchers know who got which.
Sarah: It's not even close. In manufacturing terms, this is like asking operators "does the new fixture feel more stable?" instead of running a proper measurement study with calibrated parts. You're measuring perception, not performance. And the perception is confounded — nausea, delayed gastric emptying (that's food staying in your stomach longer), early satiety (feeling full sooner) — all of those could make someone drink less without touching the reward pathway at all.
Laura: Right, the peripheral mechanism. GLP-1 agonists — drugs that mimic a natural gut hormone — slow gastric emptying and hit the area postrema in the brainstem, which triggers nausea. If you feel queasy every time you eat or drink, you're going to consume less alcohol. That's a mechanical side effect, not a craving cure.
Sarah: Precisely. The central hypothesis is different — that GLP-1 receptors in the nucleus accumbens and ventral tegmental area, parts of the brain's mesolimbic dopamine circuit (the main reward highway), directly blunt the reward signal. Less dopamine spike when you anticipate alcohol, less craving. But a survey can't distinguish "I don't want it" from "I feel sick when I drink it."
Laura: And the Reddit thread treats them as the same thing. "Acts on the brain's craving pathways" — that's the claim. But the evidence, even if the survey is real, only shows correlation between starting the drug and drinking less.
Sarah: There's also selection bias. People who get prescribed GLP-1s for weight loss or diabetes are already engaging with healthcare. They might be more motivated to change multiple behaviors at once. Or they're the subset who tolerate the drug long enough to answer a survey — dropout rates in clinical trials for semaglutide are substantial, mostly from GI side effects, though the exact percentage is unclear from the sources we have.
Laura: So the heavy-drinker figure — if it's real — could just be the people who didn't quit the drug because of vomiting.
Sarah: That's a hypothesis you'd test in a proper trial. Randomize heavy drinkers to semaglutide versus placebo, blind it, measure drinks per week with timeline follow-back interviews (a structured calendar method to reconstruct drinking history), maybe phosphatidylethanol blood markers (a lab test that detects recent alcohol use) for objective verification. That's the FDA standard for a new indication — not a survey.
Laura: Which brings us to claim three: that this observational evidence justifies off-label prescribing for alcohol use disorder.
Sarah: Off-label prescribing happens all the time — clinicians can prescribe approved drugs for unapproved uses. But "justified" is a regulatory word. For FDA to add alcohol use disorder to the label, you need adequate and well-controlled investigations. Usually two pivotal Phase Three trials (large, late-stage studies in the target population). Primary endpoint: percent subjects with no heavy drinking days, or reduction in WHO risk drinking levels (a standardized scale from low-risk to very-high-risk drinking). Safety database at the target dose in that population. Pharmacokinetics in hepatic impairment (how the drug moves through the body when the liver is damaged) — alcohol use disorder often comes with liver disease, and semaglutide clearance changes in renal impairment, so you'd need that data too.
Laura: And the dose matters. The weight-loss dose is two point four milligrams weekly for semaglutide. The diabetes dose tops out at one milligram. If the anti-craving effect only shows up at the higher dose, you're exposing a new population — people with AUD who may not have obesity or diabetes — to the full side-effect profile. Pancreatitis risk (pancreas inflammation), gallbladder disease, thyroid C-cell tumors in rodents.
Sarah: Plus drug interactions. Naltrexone and acamprosate are the current FDA-approved AUD medications. They work on opioid and glutamate systems respectively. If you stack a GLP-1 on top, nobody's studied the combination systematically.
Laura: So the verdict on the three claims?
Sarah: Claim one — the survey numbers — unconfirmed. The source is inaccessible, no independent replication. Claim two — mechanism is central dopamine not peripheral GI — the survey design cannot answer that. It's a mechanistic question that needs neuroimaging, microdialysis (sampling brain fluid to measure chemicals directly), or at minimum a trial with nausea as a covariate (a statistical control for a confounding variable). Claim three — observational evidence justifies off-label use for AUD — that's a category error. Observational data generates hypotheses. It doesn't validate indications.
Laura: Half-right at best. The signal is interesting enough to fund the real studies. And there are actually trials underway — I think one at UNC, one in Denmark — testing semaglutide for AUD specifically.
Sarah: Good. That's how it should work. Reddit notices a pattern, science tests it, regulators evaluate the evidence. But right now, the thread is confusing anecdote with endpoint.
Laura: One-line takeaway?
Sarah: A survey of what people say they drink on a drug that makes them nauseous is not a clinical trial of an anti-craving mechanism.
